# Chapter 7: Paradise

The word *aunt*, on the rue Cambon telephone, meant the shop.

She went there on foot, by the long way, through streets where there were no lamps and the windows were all blind and the only light in the fifth arrondissement came from the cigarettes of the men walking home before curfew, going past her in the dark like small red animals. Twice she stopped in a doorway to let a patrol go by: four men and a Feldwebel, and the Feldwebel was singing, quietly, in tune, and the four men walked behind him with their rifles slung and their hands in their pockets, and none of them looked at a doorway in the rue des Écoles where a well-dressed woman stood with a hatpin in her hand.

The shop with the green door and no name on it was shut and dark, and it was also open by an inch, which was the thing that told her everything.

She stood in the shadow of a doorway on the other side of the street and watched it for six minutes, and in the six minutes she learned: that a car had been at the kerb and had gone, because there was a puddle in the shape of a car under the streetlamp with the blackout ring round it, and a boy of fourteen was walking up and down in front of the shop, which is what boys of fourteen do on the orders of men; and that in the window on the second floor there was a light on behind a curtain, and the shape of the curtain was a shape that it had not had in the four months that Marion Herschlag had been going there.

She walked past the shop on the other side of the street, slowly, at the pace of a woman going home from an evening with a woman of seventy, and she did not once look at the window on the second floor, and as she came level with the arch of the courtyard next door she heard, from inside the shop, from behind the shutters, three sounds: a chair going over, and a woman's voice saying something in a level, reasonable tone, like a woman explaining to a tradesman that the delivery is wrong; and then the voice stopping.

Marion Herschlag walked home, and said good evening to the concierge, who could not hear German, and went up in the dark and let herself in and sat down in the hall on the chair where the Colonel's cap went, and took off her shoes, and put her fist into her mouth and bit it until she could breathe.

---

She spent the next morning being a woman in a hat shop.

That was the discipline: she went at eleven, as she always did, and she stood outside the shop of Odile Marchand on the rue Cambon in the grey light of the fifth of April, and the shutters were down and there was a notice on the door in French, in a handwriting that was not Odile's: *FERMÉ POUR INVENTAIRE*. Closed for stocktaking.

She stood in front of it for eleven minutes, thinking, and the thinking was not about Odile.

It was about the film in the lining of her coat. It was about the fact that the man in the hat shop, the courier of February, the woman at Modane, the boy at the market, the man who had bought her a book in the rue de Rivoli, were now all of them, separately and in their own ways, aware that there was a different woman in Paris; and about the fact that Kroll of the Sicherheitsdienst had an audit and a little green notebook and a schedule with three questions in it; and about the arithmetic of what happens to a network when the woman who runs the hats is taken into the rue des Saussaies and given a chair and a cigarette and three days.

And underneath all of it, flat as a stone on the bottom of a river, was a second arithmetic that she was not permitted to do, and did anyway, in the eleven minutes: *she is thirty. She has a brother in the Auvergne with a wireless set. She has never once, in four months, asked me for anything; and the last thing she said to me, on the telephone, was the word aunt, in German, so that I would know what had happened, because she wanted me to run.*

At noon Marion went to the Majestic, because it was Wednesday and she was expected.

She sat in the outer office of the Abwehrstelle Paris at the corner where the officers' women sat, and she waited for the Colonel, who was in a conference, and she was, for forty minutes, the most obedient piece of furniture in the room; and in forty minutes nobody looked at her twice, which was the point; and at the end of it, going down the stairs, in the middle of the flight, the Hauptmann came the other way.

He stopped. There was nobody on the stair between them.

"You were in the rue Cambon this morning," he said.

She looked at him. There was a landing light behind him and she could see nothing of his face at all, only the outline: a captain on a stair.

"I was."

"There is a notice on a door in that street, and I saw it from the car on my way to the arsenal, and there is a woman at a bus stop on that street who has been at the same bus stop for two hours in the rain, and she is not waiting for a bus, because no bus stops there." He was speaking in French, quietly, at a normal speed, in the voice of a man discussing a memorandum. "And this morning, at nine o'clock, three of our cars went out of this courtyard with an officer of the Sicherheitsdienst in the first one, and they came back at half past eleven with four file boxes and a woman and a coffee-machine, and the coffee-machine was carried by a soldier, and the file boxes by a clerk, and the woman by nobody at all."

So it was true.

"Thank you," said Marion Herschlag.

"Do not thank me." He came down two steps. "I am telling you because it is going to be known in this building by tonight, and when it is known I want you to remember that I was the one who told you at ten o'clock in the morning, and that the Colonel's adjutant does not tell the Colonel's woman anything without a reason." He put his cap on. "There was a man in that shop in February, in a brown coat, who bought a hat for his sister and paid with a note that had a number written on the back of it. I saw the note. He was a Pole; he had a Polish way of holding it. So when they take the milliner, they will have the note, and they will have the hat, and they will ask her for the name of the man in the brown coat, and there is exactly one name that a Pole in Paris would have given at a hat shop in February, and it is not on the note, because a Pole who buys a hat with a number on a note is a man who has been told what name to give, and who is now, at this hour, either dead or in the Auvergne." He looked at her for one second more. "You have people who are in the Auvergne. Everybody in Paris has people in the Auvergne. Do not come to the archive room again."

He went up the stairs, past her, without touching her, and was gone around the turn of the landing; and she stood on the stairs of the Hôtel Majestic for four seconds with her hand on the banister, and then she went down and out into the courtyard and got into the car and Ehlers drove her home.

And what she thought about, all the way home, with the wet grey front of a city going by her at eleven kilometres an hour, was not Odile.

It was the sentence *I saw the note.*

Because the note had been in Odile's shop for six weeks, in a drawer, in a bundle of paper; and a captain of the Abwehrstelle Paris had seen it, and had stood at a counter in a hat shop and looked at a piece of paper with a number written on the back of it and had understood, in one glance, three things: that the man was a Pole, that he had a name he had been told to give, and that the name was not on the note. And then he had put the paper back in the bundle, and paid for nothing, and gone out of the shop into the rue Cambon.

Which meant that Phillip Brandt had known about the shop in February.

Which meant that Phillip Brandt had known about the shop, and had said nothing to the Sicherheitsdienst, and had said nothing to his Colonel, and had said nothing to the woman — for six weeks, and through an archive room on the fourth of April, and past a stair on the fifth, until the morning when three cars went out of a courtyard at nine o'clock and came back at half past eleven with a coffee-machine.

She sat in the back of the car and looked at the back of Ehlers's head and at the black trees of the boulevard going by, and for the first time since she had come to Paris, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna did the thing she had been trained never to do, which was to reason about a German officer from the inside.

*Why.*

The car went along the avenue Henri-Martin. The concierge was sweeping the steps, which was his way of knowing everything. In the apartment, Kleist had laid out her gloves and a note about the laundry. In the small north room, the hatbox with a red ribbon on it stood empty on the table, and the film was in the lining of her coat, and the coat was on her, and the film was against her wrist, and in eleven days she was going to get on a train with a colonel of the Abwehr and go to Warsaw, and between this hour and that train there were the two most dangerous days of her life, and she still did not have a courier.

She got out of the car and stood on the pavement in the rain, and looked up at the third floor of the house where she lived, at the tall windows and the cream curtains of a room that belonged to a dead woman, and she made the arithmetic and got the only answer that was left.

She was going to have to be the courier herself.

---

---

On the sixth of April she went to Saint-Roch, at the hour when the church was empty, and did the thing that she had been told never to do.

The drop behind the second column of the north aisle was dead. She knew it before her fingers closed on the tin, which was cold and had a centimetre of dust in it and did not contain anything, and had not contained anything for eleven days; and she knelt in the dark with her hand inside the niche for a while and understood, the way a woman understands the temperature of a house she has lived in, that there was no one left to listen.

So she wrote it on the inside of her glove.

*Hats taken Wednesday. Do not go to the rue Cambon. The brother in the Auvergne must be told and must not travel. The shop boy knows nothing. The notice on the door is not in her hand.*

*Tell her, when there is anyone to tell, that the last thing she did was say the word "aunt", in German, on a telephone, in order to save somebody who was not able to save her.*

She did not sign it. She put the glove in the niche with the dust and the tin, because there was nowhere else left in Paris that was not either a German office or a friend's coffin, and then she went and sat at the back of the church in the smell of old candle-smoke for eleven minutes, the length of one of her mother's rosaries, and did not pray, because Krystyna Stankiewiczówna had stopped praying in September 1939 in a ditch outside Otwock, and had stopped for a reason that would have interested a priest: not because she had decided there was no God, but because the arrangement seemed unworthy of one.

Outside, the rue Saint-Honoré was full of the ordinary grey afternoon. A car went past with two officers in it and their caps on the shelf behind the glass. A woman was selling matches on the corner out of a shoebox. Two boys of eleven were doing something with a hoop and a stick that was either a game or a very small conspiracy, and a German soldier of eighteen sat on a wall watching them with his face, and looked, for one unguarded second, exactly like a boy who used to do the same thing with a hoop on a street in Breslau.

---

On the seventh, the last evening, they packed, and she played the piano.

It had been the Vasseurs' piano: a French instrument of 1904 with a light action and a crack in the soundboard that came up on the second F below middle C, and Marion Herschlag, in the five months of her residence in that apartment, had touched the keyboard three times: once in December, with two fingers, in front of the Colonel, when he asked whether she played; and twice in the middle of the night of the twenty-eighth of February, when she woke at three and could not sleep and went into the drawing room in the dark and put her hands on the keys for a long time without pressing a single one.

On the seventh she sat down at it in a dress of her own grey wool, in an apartment full of wooden crates, with her wardrobe on the floor and the silver, which was somebody else's silver, wrapped in paper on the table; and she began, deliberately, with the thing she had learned at the age of nine, in a flat on the Mokotowska: a two-part invention, slowly, with the wrists flat as a plate, the way a schoolgirl plays it for an examination in a room with a photograph of the inspector of schools.

She played it badly. She had spent two thousand hours and eleven hundred francs of her mother's money, at the Conservatory in Warsaw between her fourteenth and her nineteenth years, in becoming one of the two or three best young pianists of her year in Poland, and she had spent the last three years perfecting the art of playing badly, and the badness was now so good that it could not be detected; it was a badness with a very particular smell, the badness of a woman of the middle class who had been taught by a nun.

The music went down the corridor, into the hall, out along the boards where a small bar of kitchen light still lay in the evening, from the open door where a fifty-seven-year-old orderly stood with his hands folded in front of him and his eyes closed.

She saw him in the polished black of the piano's cheekpiece.

"I am sorry," she said, without stopping. "I am out of practice."

"Herr Oberst's late wife did not play," said Kleist, in German, into the room, in a way that was not a sentence about anybody's wife at all. "My mother played. In Breslau. She had a harmonium out of the church at Zimpel, and it had six stops, and two of them did not work, and when she died in 1928 the harmonium went back to the church and my father did not go to the service." He was quiet for a moment, standing in the doorway. "It is a very good instrument. From a house in the rue de la Faisanderie, I think. The crate has the number."

"It is a stolen piano," said Marion Herschlag, in French, still with her hands flat, "sitting in a stolen flat in a city in its third year of being stolen; and I am a woman in a stolen dress being taken away tomorrow in a stolen car."

"Yes," said Kleist.

"Yet you stood in the doorway with your eyes shut."

"Yes," said Kleist; and then he went down the corridor and did not shut the kitchen door, and after a while she heard him washing the cups.

She sat at the piano until half past ten, alone in the drawing room, and when she was certain of the whole house she took her hands off the two-part invention and put them, without any transition at all, in the correct position; and for four minutes, in a requisitioned flat in the sixteenth arrondissement, on the night of the seventh of April, 1943, with a German driver asleep in the same building, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna played the mazurka she had played in the eleventh year of her life in a room in Żoliborz with her mother sitting behind her with a book that was not a book, and she played it the way it was written to be played, which is not beautifully: with the tempo of a thing being remembered.

Then she stopped, and closed the lid over the keys, and went to bed, and did not sleep.

In the morning, in the wardrobe, under the row of eleven dresses that she had been given and could not take with her, she took down the twelfth: the pale dove-coloured thing with the wide collar, the one she had never worn because of what was in the lining. She stood with it over her arm for a full minute by the empty crates; and then, because it was a dress and she was not able to leave it in a flat for the next German woman, and because there was a trunk going to Warsaw with the Colonel's papers and the winter coats in it, she folded it, with two sheets of tissue paper, and put it in the trunk, on the bottom, under everything.

The film went out of Paris on the eighth of April in the lining of a coat belonging to a civilian employee of the Wehrmacht, a woman called Marion Herschlag, travelling in a first-class carriage of the night train with a colonel, a captain, a driver, a crate of the Colonel's papers and two suitcases; and it went out because of a trick so simple that she laughed, once, out loud, in the bathroom of the flat on the evening before, with the door shut and the tap running, when she thought of it.

The trick was to put the film on a coat-hanger.

The frontier at Forbach takes six hours, and in those six hours every piece of luggage in the train is taken off and put on a table in a shed and opened by two men in grey; and the train in which a colonel of the Abwehr travels is not searched, because the men in grey are customs men and the colonel is a colonel. But the Colonel had told her, in Berlin-accented French, in the car the week before, while explaining to her what she must expect: *I will be looked at on the platform by the district staff; there will be a formality; you will wait in the carriage with the captain, and mind that nobody in that shed decides to be a hero.*

And so on the eighth of April, in the corridor of a first-class carriage of the night train from the Gare de l'Est, at fifteen minutes before midnight, a woman in a good coat handed her coat to a captain of the Abwehr and asked him to hang it up for her, and the captain hung the coat in the compartment in which the Colonel would ride, over a leather valise, in the third position from the window; and the two men in grey who walked down the platform at Forbach at three in the morning looked through the windows of that carriage and saw a colonel asleep with his mouth open and a woman asleep with her head on his shoulder and a captain in the corridor reading a newspaper; and they saluted the carriage and went on the platform, and did not open the door, because a colonel's belongings are private, and the coat hung inside the compartment all the way to Warsaw with the film in the sleeve, and nobody in the world ever touched it but the woman who had sewn it.

She had arranged it. It was not a trick, in the end. It was a ledger, and she had balanced it: the film went out because a colonel trusted a woman, and the woman had spent five months buying that trust with everything she had; and in the sleeve of the coat there were sixteen exposures containing the plan of the underground of half of Europe, a page about a line on the Vistula that was to be held until the eighth of August 1944, and the personnel file of the man who hung it up.

---

---

The platform at the Gare de l'Est was long and dark and smelled of coal smoke and wet wool and oranges, which was the smell of Paris in the spring of the fourth year of the war, because the oranges were for the officers' cars and had come from Spain by arrangement with a government in Madrid.

Ehlers put the Colonel's boxes in the van with the marks on them and stood by the carriage with the rain on his shoulders. Kleist had taken the household goods on Tuesday: he had gone at dawn with the crates and the crate of the stolen piano; he had shaken her hand for the first time in six months, and had said, in German, in the middle of the platform, *I shall see you in a garden, gnädige Frau*, which was the loose and terrible sentence of a man whose whole life was arranged in offices.

The Colonel came along the platform with the district staff of the eastern command, who had been at the barrier to do him honour: a General, two Obersts, an Oberstleutnant with a chest like a small balcony. They walked with their hands behind their backs, slowly, in the way of men who have not paid for a railway ticket since 1939. Marion stood at the door of the carriage in her good coat with the film in the sleeve, and watched them come, and understood that her life was now this: a woman standing at a carriage door in the middle of a landscape of uniforms, with a face on, being looked at by men who thought she belonged to one of them.

Twenty metres away, by the bookstall, with the rain coming down through the gap in the roof of the station, stood René Tissier.

He had his hat in his hands. He was wearing the same good coat with the collar turned up, and he was not looking at the General, and he was not looking at the Colonel; he was looking at her, and when he was sure of her eyes on him, he did the thing that Krystyna Stankiewiczówna carried with her into the Generalgouvernement and thought about, on and off, for the sixteen months that followed, in bed, in corridors, and once in a cellar in the burning city with the shells coming down on the roofs above her.

He raised his hat.

Not far. A small movement, two fingers on the brim, the way a Frenchman salutes a woman he knows at a distance; and with it, exactly one nod of the head; and then he turned and walked off along the dark of the platform and was gone.

It was a farewell, and it was a compliment, and it was a threat, and she never learned which of the three he had meant, and she never saw him again in Paris.

The Colonel came up and took her elbow.

"Ready?"

"Yes."

"Thirty-one hours." He looked along the train with the pleasure of a man who had been given an assignment and a railway timetable, and put his hand on the back of her neck, in the middle of the platform, in front of the General, and left it there while he said something to the Oberst about the coal situation in the east; and she stood with the film against her wrist and the rain on her face, and looked at the last two hundred metres of Paris that she was ever to see: the dark arches, the lamps with their blackout hoods, a boy with a trolley of mail, a woman in a headscarf saying goodbye to a soldier through a window, and beyond the barrier, forty metres away, a crowd of Parisians waiting for news of a train from the east that had been delayed, standing in the rain under an umbrella of umbrellas, silent, grey, and alive.

She stayed awake for the last night, lying beside the Colonel in a first-class compartment between Frankfurt and Breslau, with the dark of Germany going by outside and the train making the long, patient, four-beat sound that it makes between sleepers, and his hand on her hip, warm, proprietary, even in his sleep.

Her whole body ached. There was an ache in her left wrist from the seam of the coat, and an ache behind her eyes from eleven nights of four hours, and an ache in the bones of her face from holding it still for three years; and under the ache there was something else, something that she had not felt since she was twenty-two and had stood in a doorway in Warsaw in September 1939 with a suitcase in her hand and a paper in her pocket and no idea in the world where her father was: the flat, enormous, unmistakable feeling of a plan working.

*Paradise* was what the men in the room in Kraków had called it, when they had taught her the last and worst of the four rules, the rule they saved for the women who got through the first three. *If you are ever, in your service, given a place in the household of an enemy; if you are ever in a room where no other person on earth can go; if you are ever trusted by one of them, then you will have arrived at the state for which we use the word paradise, and I want you to remember the other meaning of the word, which is the one they put on the sign at the door: the garden from which there is no coming back.*

At four in the morning, in the dark of a carriage on the way to Warsaw with her hand in the hand of a man who had told her that he wanted to be buried in her, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna lay awake and did the four questions for the last morning in Paris, in order, one at a time, the whole set, properly, the way she had done them every day for five months.

Who has seen me where I should not have been.

*A boy in a cinema in the fifth arrondissement, who is dead. A key-cutter in the Marais, who will not speak; a nephew in the Auvergne, who will speak, sooner or later, and will be believed by nobody. Tissier, who saw my face in a corridor and holds it in a drawer. A woman called Odile Marchand, in a chair, in the rue des Saussaies.*

Who has heard me say something I should not have said.

*Nobody. In eleven days I said one word in Polish, on a quai of the Seine in January and in a stairwell of the Majestic yesterday, and each time the man was the same man, and the man is the file in the fourth paper in the safe.*

Who has seen my hands.

*The key-cutter. A wax cake. A camera in a corner of green velvet in a bathroom in the sixteenth arrondissement; and, at four o'clock on the fifth of April, a captain on a stair who told the truth to a woman in a corridor for no reason that a captain of the Abwehr could ever have explained.*

And the last one, the one she did not permit herself to do in daylight:

Who has seen the inside of my head.

And in the dark of the carriage, going east, with the whole machine of the Reich clanking around them through the forests of Silesia, she lay still and looked at the ceiling of a railway carriage and answered the question, honestly, for the first time in three years, and the answer was this:

*A man on a stair, who knows what I am, and who has said nothing to anyone, and who is going to Warsaw with me on the same train.*

The train slowed. Somewhere in the dark outside the window there were rails and sidings and men with lamps, and the carriage swayed, and the Colonel's hand tightened on her hip for a second and then loosened, and he said, in his sleep, in German, a word she chose not to hear.

And at eleven o'clock the following morning, the locomotive came down off the long embankment into the eastern suburbs of a city she had not seen since the winter of 1939, and she smelled it before she saw it, because the train smelled it, and men in the corridor covered their mouths with their handkerchiefs, and the Colonel woke and looked out of the window and said, without any expression at all:

"There. That is our district. That is the smoke of the ghetto."

And Warsaw came towards her out of the flat grey morning: the broken roofs, the church spires standing above the burned squares, the river, and the two hundred thousand people who were still alive in it, who were not permitted to buy a railway ticket, who went to work, who queued for bread, who wrote letters to London on cigarette paper, and in one of the station canteens a woman in a Wehrmacht clerk's uniform was signing for the storage of a crate of papers, and did not yet know that the film in the sleeve of her coat was the first of the things that would kill her.
