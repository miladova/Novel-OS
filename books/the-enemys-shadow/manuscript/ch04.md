# Chapter 4: Five Minutes of Candlelight

On the eleventh of March, at Mont-Valérien, forty-eight men were shot.

The placards went up on the walls of Paris at noon, white on red, in the places where the walls were smooth, and by three o'clock in the afternoon there were people standing in front of them in the rain: the ordinary French crowd, the grey crowd, reading the list with their hands in their pockets. Twenty-two of the names were Communist. Eleven of them were Jews. Fifteen of them were nobody, in the sense that nobody could say what they had done, which is a distinction that the red placards did not observe and the ordinary French crowd did not discuss; they read the names, and looked at the date, and one woman in the rue de Rivoli cried, and the crowd did not look at her, because to look at her was to be, for a moment, part of a thing.

Marion Herschlag read the placard in the rue de Rivoli at four in the afternoon, in a good coat, on the arm of the Colonel; she read it because he stopped and read it, and he read it because to walk past it quickly would have been a kind of comment.

She knew the list. She had read it in his briefcase on the Tuesday, photographing the names in the bathroom with the velvet curtain around her hands, and among the forty-eight there had been two that she had struggled with for a whole night: a boy of nineteen, second from the bottom, who had been the kind of boy who carries a folded paper in his shoe, and a locksmith of sixty-one from Ivry who had probably mended a bicycle for the wrong man.

*Only two,* she thought. *Out of forty-eight, only two were ours, only two would be worse than the rest if I had stopped them; and the other forty-six were somebody else's, and I could not have saved them either, and the arithmetic, therefore, is that I have done nothing wrong, and I will be able to say that to myself in a very level voice for the rest of my life.*

She stood in the rain on the arm of the man who had signed the order, and looked at the names of forty-eight men as though she were reading the prices on a menu; and because the only thing worse than the arithmetic is the arithmetic with no feeling in it, she permitted herself, on the fourth name from the top, to have one small, sharp, entirely private thought that she would keep forever: *I have stood where you are, and I have not signed anything, and there is no one alive who will ever know the difference.*

"There will be a reprisal for this," the Colonel said.

"There is always a reprisal for the reprisal."

"That is not the same thing. That is the circle. The reprisal for this will not come from the Communists. It will come from the Gaullists, who have been waiting to be *given* Paris, and it will be a German officer in a café at four in the afternoon, shot in the back while he is paying for his breakfast." He turned away from the placard. "It is all arithmetic, and the difficulty is that the men who do my arithmetic for me are clerks."

They walked. In the car, going back, he sat with his glove off, drumming two fingers on his knee, and said, in a different voice, the voice he used for the things that mattered: "I have someone for you to meet on Friday."

"I have met everyone."

"Not this one." He looked out of the window. "Brandt. My new adjutant. He came to me on Monday. I want to know what you think of him."

"You never want to know what I think of anyone."

"No." He was still looking at the grey fronts of the houses. "I want to know what he thinks of you."

---

The Friday was at the flat of a Colonel of the Militärbefehlshaber, a big dinner with too many men and three women, one of whom was the hostess and one of whom was the wife of a French industrialist who had decided, that winter, that the war was being fought in his drawing room.

Marion wore the black and the diamonds. She was placed, by accident or by design — she never learned which and it never stopped worrying her — at the corner of the long table, with the Colonel at the head and, on her left, the new adjutant.

He came in at three minutes past nine and was presented to her by von Reuth with a hand on his shoulder, which was a thing the Colonel did with men he had decided to keep.

"Hauptmann Brandt. Madame Herschlag."

She had rehearsed the moment for eleven days, in the bath, in the car, at the cinema, and every rehearsal had been a failure, because in every rehearsal something in her face did a thing she could not control. In the event, none of it was necessary.

"Madame," he said, and bent his head, and looked at her exactly as long as it took to memorise her face and not one fraction of a second longer, and then he was seated, and unfolding his napkin, and asking the woman on his left whether the winter had been as bad in Paris as they said.

She watched him for the first course the way she watched all new men: the hands (clean, kept, a bricklayer's scar on the left, from a fence-wire, old), the shoes (German, best quality, badly polished — a man who had someone else's standards of appearance), the way he cut his meat (fast, economical, un-German — a boy who had been raised in a house where food was quick), and the way he said the word 'Warsaw' when the Generalmajor's wife, who had been reading the wrong newspaper, asked him where his mother was from.

He said it in Polish. Deliberately, with a French flattening of the vowels, and instantly converted to the German form, and he did it as he was putting down his fork, without any expression at all. But she heard the softness of the 'w', the shape of the city in the mouth of somebody who had learned it on a street. His mother was from Warsaw. He had told them all that, and they had all heard the German name of the city; and she had heard the other thing, and now she knew, as she had known in the corridor but could not permit herself to believe, that he had grown up there. That he had walked it. That the summer of 1938 in the Botanic Gardens was not a thing she had invented out of a bad dream.

At the fish course she asked him, because she had a job and a job is a shield: "And do you find Paris endurable, Hauptmann?"

"I find it unbearable," he said, and the two women on his left laughed, and he smiled politely, and turned his head, and him arrived at her for the first time: the pale grey eyes, the father, the heavy jaw of a man who had been hit twice in the face in his life, and something at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a scar.

"Unbearable?"

"I am told it is the most beautiful city in Europe. The difficulty is that I am not permitted to see it, and neither, madame, are you. We stand in rooms with mirrors and talk about the spring, and the spring is on the other side of a door that has a sentry in front of it." He drank. "And the food is better than Berlin."

"The food is a fantasy of pre-war France produced by the Generalgouvernement," Marion said. "It is the only joke in Paris that everyone tells and nobody makes."

He laughed, all at once, without any of the ceremonial reserve of ten minutes before, a short genuine laugh, and the laugh was the same. It was the same laugh as a boy of nineteen in a Botanic Garden in June, and she had to put her hand on the table edge and hold it, and while she was holding it she saw the Colonel watching her from the head of the table.

"It seems the captain amuses you," he said, afterwards, in the car.

"He is intelligent. You do not usually keep intelligent men."

"I keep them for their intelligence and I hang them for their opinions." He was in a good humour. He had won something at dinner; she never knew what. His hand came onto her knee. "Well? You did not answer my question."

"You asked me what he thinks of me. The answer is nothing. He thought about me for the length of one course, in the way that a soldier thinks about the wife of his colonel, and then he thought about the eastern front, and then he thought about whether it was permitted to smoke." She said it flatly, as a fact. "He is one of those men who looks past furniture."

"I see." He took his hand away. "And you?"

"I thought he had good hands and bad shoes, and I thought that when he laughed it came out of him all at once, and I thought that if you let him get near enough to be interesting, he would be a great deal of trouble."

The car stopped. He did not get out. In the dark of the back seat, with Ehlers still as a stone in front, the Colonel sat for a moment and looked at the window, and then he said the sentence that would keep her awake until the end of March.

"He has one defect," he said. "He is a Pole."

"Half a Pole."

"There is no such thing." He opened the door. "His father was German. His mother was a Warsaw woman; she is dead. He was raised there and he went to their schools and he learned their poetry and I will tell you a fact about the eastern front, my dear, since you have never had the good fortune to be shot at: in a burning village, a man runs toward the house where his mother was born." He got out, and held the door for her, and stood in the cold with the blackout lamps of the avenue behind him like two candles on a table. "I have known eleven men like Brandt in my career. Nine of them were excellent. The other two are the reason I keep a drawer of photographs of hanged men."

---

The keys were the problem.

There were six of them on a steel ring, and the ring had a habit of coming out of his trousers and lying on whatever surface was nearest — the hall table, the desk in the study, the edge of the basin — because a man who has been an officer for thirty years takes off his keys the way other men take off their hats.

She had spent February understanding the six. Door, safe, desk, the drawer at the Majestic (which she would never in her life touch, because a drawer in an office is watched), the wine cabinet, and a small silver key with a worn shank that she had narrowed to one of two possibilities and would, in the end, never identify; and the important one was the fifth, the safe key, which was flat, brass, and worn at the shoulder, with the number of the maker stamped on it in a place invisible to a man's eye when the key was on the ring.

*You cannot steal a key from a man who has it in his trousers*, Lis had said. *You take the shape of the key, and then you go to a man who cuts keys, and you pay him an amount he cannot explain, and you never go back to him for any reason for the rest of your life.*

To take a shape you need five minutes and something the shape will hold. Wax is best; soap is possible; a potato will do in a war if a potato can be found. And to take the shape of a key that never leaves the trousers of a man who sleeps beside you, you need the key to be off the trousers, and the man to be asleep, and the room to be dark enough that he does not see the light and light enough that you do not press the wax in the wrong place; and the light was the difficulty, because the only light in the avenue Henri-Martin after curfew was a candle, and a candle in a room where a man sleeps is a thing that even a man asleep will sometimes smell.

She waited nine days for the right night. A bad night was one in which he had been drinking (he slept shallowly); a bad night was one in which his leg hurt him (he woke at every sound and lay on his back with his eyes open, listening); a bad night was any night on which there had been a bombing in the east of the city, because then the anti-aircraft guns worked and Paris lay awake. A good night was a night when he had been made to sit through a two-hour briefing on the requisition of cattle, and had come home with the flat exhaustion of a man who has been bored by his equals.

The third of March was a good night.

He was asleep by eleven, flat on his back, arms at his sides, chin up: the position, the terrible formal position, and by midnight his breathing had gone down into the long slow shallows that meant he had gone over. She lay beside him and counted to one hundred, and then to a hundred again, and then she got up, and went out of the bedroom on the outside edge of her feet, and went down the corridor to the kitchen, where Kleist slept in a small room off the pantry, and where she had hidden, since the previous morning, in the cold box beside the ice, a candle and a cake of the wax the Vasseurs' housekeeper had once used for preserving jam, in a paper twist.

The candle stub was two and a half inches of the nine that had been on the dining table in January, and she had cut it down over three weeks so that it would not be missed.

In the bedroom she put it on the floor beside the bed, on the far side from him, and got down on her knees in her nightdress with her back to the wall, so that the candle was between her body and the bed: the whole candle, and the light, which was the size of a coin.

Then she reached up onto the chair, where the trousers were, and took the ring of keys by the shank, dead slow, and brought it down into the coin of light.

She had expected fear. She had not expected this: the pure, cold, enormous calm that came over her the moment the work began, which was, she understood later, the same thing that happened to her when she was being had by him, and the same thing that happened in Kraków in the room with the bad fire when she had learned to handle a pistol: her hands stopped shaking because there was no room in her for the shaking. There was a candle, and a key, and a cake of wax, and a man four feet away. That was all.

She warmed the wax in her fingers. She pressed the flat of the key into it, edgeways, four times: once for the shoulder, twice for the length, and once for the head, where the bit was, because a wax impression of a shaft is useless and a wax impression of a bit is a sentence. She did it with her thumb flat on the back of the key, breathing through her mouth, and the third press made a small sound — a click, the sound of two pieces of the world coming together — and on the bed, the Colonel's breathing changed.

She did not move. She did not take her hands away. That was Kraków, too: *when the animal turns its head, you do not freeze and you do not run; you become the thing the room already contains, and you wait for the animal to be bored.*

Eight seconds. Nine.

"Marion."

"I'm here."

The candle was behind her, on the floor, and the wax was in her left hand under her thigh, and the keys were in her right hand under the hem of the nightdress. His voice came out of the pillow, thick, at the bottom of sleep.

"What is it."

"Nothing. I was thirsty." She stood up, and the movement was one piece, and she put the keys back on the chair with her arm and her body behind it, and reached out and touched his shoulder with her fingertips, the way she touched him when she woke him for the telephone. "Go to sleep."

"Water," he said. "Always water. You are like a horse."

"I am like a woman who was born in a dry country. Go to sleep."

He was already gone; the sentence had exhausted him; his breathing flattened out, and she stood beside the bed for the length of a hundred breaths with the wax hardening in her hand, and then she went down the corridor, and washed her hands, and put the wax back in the paper twist, and put it in the cold box in the kitchen, and stood in the dark kitchen with the great cold of the Paris night coming up off the floor, and she had to put her hand on the dresser because her legs, in the end, in the aftermath, were the part of her that betrayed her.

Five minutes of candlelight. She had rehearsed it eleven times and it had taken four.

---

The key-cutter in the rue des Hospitalières-Saint-Gervais was a small man of seventy with a shop the size of a cupboard and a smell in it of hot brass and onions. He was called Monsieur Kahn and he cut keys for the Marais, and he had cut them for forty-one years, from before the war that had made the street, and he had cut them during the war the way he had cut them before it, one at a time, and to look at him working was to believe for a moment that there was a France left.

Marion went to him on the fifth of March, in the worn coat she kept in the north room for the times she was nobody, with her hair under a scarf and her shoes flat, and she stood in the doorway and let an old woman with a basket go past, and went in and said, "I need a key cut from an impression," and put the wax on the counter.

Monsieur Kahn looked at the wax for a long time.

"Not today," he said.

"I will pay you eight hundred francs."

"Not for eight hundred. Not for eight thousand. Not for the Banque de France, madame." He turned the wax over with one finger. "This is a Mauer, forty-one, pattern three. There are two hundred and six of these in this city and they are all on the doors of people who have things, and a man who cuts a key like this for a woman I have never seen, in a year in which eight hundred francs is what a grave costs, is a man who will be standing with his back to a wall in four months, telling a Hauptsturmführer with good manners that he does not know the lady's name." He pushed the wax back across the counter. "I am sorry. The work I can do. The work is easy. What I cannot do is be a Jew in this city and take an eight-hundred-franc job."

She stood in the little shop with the smell of brass and onions, in the worn coat, and looked at a man who was in more danger than she was, and who had just refused her with total, unhurried courage, and she did the only thing there was left to do, which was to tell him the truth about one thing only.

"The key is a safe," she said. "The safe is in the study of a German officer, and what is in it is a list of men. I am not one of yours, monsieur. I am not French at all. But the men on the list are not French either, and there are nine of them."

He was quiet for a moment.

"How do you know there are nine?"

"Because in December there were nine, and in February there were eleven, and on the second of March there were fourteen, and I have not seen the paper since."

"Ah," said Monsieur Kahn. And then, as though a small complicated machine inside him had finished its cycle and dropped a coin: "My nephew is in the Auvergne. He has a shop; he cuts keys; he is twenty-six and he is stupid and brave and he has no children, and if a woman in a scarf comes to him with a wax and a story and does not give her name, he will cut the key by Thursday and he will be very proud of himself for a month." He wrote down an address, slowly, in a hand that had been taught in a school in Warsaw province in 1881, and tore the paper off the pad and pushed it across the counter with two fingers. "I will not charge you, because if I charge you I have to put it in the book. Go away now, madame. And when you are old, if you are old, do not come back."

She went out into the Marais in the middle of the afternoon. The street was raked by the wind, and the shops were shut for the two hours of the middle of the day when no goods were coming and no one had anything to sell, and there were chalk marks on the wall at the corner, and a man of fifty in a good overcoat was standing at the bus stop reading a newspaper that was three days old, and she walked past him without turning her head, and did not know, and would never know, that he was one of Kroll's, that he had been sitting at the café on the corner since eight in the morning, that he had written her coat down in a notebook, and that it would be four months before anybody ever read the notebook.

---

The key came on the eleventh. She took it in the ladies' room of a café on the boulevard Beaumarchais, where the nephew had left it in a brown paper packet under the cistern of the second cubicle, and she put it in her bag between two folded handkerchiefs, and went home, and put it in the toe of a shoe inside a hatbox inside a room that the Colonel of the Abwehrstelle Paris believed was full of her clothes.

That night he took her to bed early and with a strange formal tenderness, and afterwards he lay with his hand flat on her stomach, in the position of a man who wants to know whether he has been told the truth, and he said, into the dark: "Tell me something."

"If I can."

"Anything. Anything at all." He moved his hand up, to the small of her ribs, and left it there, and she lay on her back and looked at the ceiling and understood, with a clarity that was almost unbearable, that in the last four months the possession and the need had begun, somewhere in him, in some cupboard where he kept his medals, to reverse their direction. "You have never once asked me for anything."

"What should I have asked for?"

"That is my point."

"I have a roof, a bed, a chair, eleven dresses and two diamonds. What does a woman ask for after that?" She turned her head and looked at him, and the terrible thing was that at that moment she could have asked him for the safe key, and he would have got up and given it to her, and that would have been the end of her, because the one thing he would never forgive her was the moment when the thing he had chosen to trust turned out to have been using the trust as a tool. "I have never asked you for anything because I have been given more than I can hold. That is not a compliment, Colonel. It is a confession. I am not brave enough to want anything more than this."

He lay quiet for a long time. Then he said: "I am being sent to Warsaw."

The clock in the hall struck two.

"Not for good," he said. "It is a command of the eastern sector, six months, possibly a year; there is a General who is a fool and a district that is the most important ground in Europe, and I have been asked for by name, which is a very great honour, and a way of saying that they have decided what I am worth. There is a house. There is a garden. There is a district with thirty thousand men in it and a river, and there is the other thing, which I have not told you."

"What other thing?"

"A reprisal," he said. "Not a small one. And when a reprisal is done, one does not wish to be the colonel who goes home to an empty flat in Paris and a photograph on the sill." He turned his head. "You will come with me."

The shop was dark at half past five.

She stood on the other side of the rue Cambon for four minutes, in the doorway of a leather shop, with the cold of the March evening coming up off the pavement, and she watched the window of the milliner's: the two hats on their stands, the second stand gone, the door shut, and the brass plate with the name on it dulled as though nobody had touched it with a cloth since Friday.

Then she crossed the street and rang.

The door opened eight centimetres and a woman of about sixty with grey hair under a black scarf put her face into the gap, and Marion Herschlag said, in the French of a customer of the establishment:

"Mademoiselle Marchand is retrimming a felt for me. The grey with the Petersham band."

"The establishment is closed, madame."

"When does it open?"

"When it opens." The woman looked at the street over her shoulder, on both sides, in the way of a person who has been told to do exactly that, and then she looked at the woman on the step for four seconds, and something in the face of a person of sixty who has worked in a shop for thirty years decided itself. "The felt with the Petersham band is finished, and the felt with the Petersham band is not in the shop, and if madame will be at the church of Saint-Roch on Thursday at the hour of the evening, at the second chapel on the left of the nave, and will put her hand into the niche between the second and the third pilaster, she will find the felt with the Petersham band, wrapped in paper, and there will be no one to be seen, and there will be nothing said, and madame will pay nothing, and madame will not come to this street again in the month of March."

"Where is she?"

The woman began to close the door.

"Where is she?"

"In the country," said the woman, in the voice of a person repeating a sentence she had been given, "and the shop is closed for the month, and there is nobody in this establishment who is able to answer a question of madame's kind, and I am the aunt of the girl, and I have been in this street for thirty-one years, and I have a sister in the country, and madame should not be at this door."

The door shut.

There was a man at the corner of the rue Saint-Honoré.

He was about forty, in a good dark overcoat without any uniform, with his hat low and his hands in his pockets, and he was standing in the position of a man who is looking into the window of a hat shop that has a window full of men's hats and no reason for anybody to look at it for eleven minutes; and Marion Herschlag walked up the rue Cambon towards the rue de Rivoli at the pace of a woman of the sixteenth arrondissement with an errand behind her and a car waiting, and she did not look at him, and she did not turn her head, and she counted the shop windows on the left-hand side the way she had been taught to count them in the long room in Kraków, and at the corner of the rue Saint-Honoré she stopped and turned and looked at the display of gloves in the window of the shop on the corner for eleven seconds, and in the glass of the window she watched the man in the dark overcoat come up the rue Cambon at the pace of a man who has nowhere to be, stop at the corner, look at the clock on the church, and walk away down the rue Saint-Honoré in the direction of the Place Vendôme.

She went home in a taxi with her hands in her lap.

---

On the Thursday she went to Saint-Roch at the hour of the evening.

The church was cold and almost empty: two women in black in the front benches with their heads down, a man asleep in the fifth row with his hat on his chest, and a priest moving at the far end of the nave with a boy and a taper. The second chapel on the left was dark; the pilasters went up into the shadow of the vault with the smell of the cold stone and the old wax; and Marion Herschlag knelt at the bench in front of the second chapel for a quarter of an hour with her lips moving, and put her hand into the niche between the second and the third pilaster, and found the paper parcel with the felt hat in it, and inside the hat, sewn into the band in the way the band of that establishment had been sewn for two years, there was a strip of paper a centimetre wide and eleven centimetres long.

The paper said, in the tiny hand of Odile Marchand, in pencil:

*The grey is finished. Wear it on Tuesday. The aunt is my aunt and knows nothing and is to be left. Do not come to the shop. Do not write to the shop. The man of the rue des Saussaies came on the third at eleven, with a sergeant and a list, and asked for the names of the ladies of the establishment; my aunt gave him eleven names and no addresses and he was satisfied, and he went away, and there is a second man who comes on the pavement and looks at the shop for a quarter of an hour in the evening. The paper of the department of the defence of the Reich of the month of November 1942 with the four names on it is in the hands of the office of the Sicherheitsdienst of Paris, in a file, in a room on the third floor of the rue des Saussaies, and there is a question mark in the margin of one of the four names, and the question mark was made with a pencil by a man of that office in the month of December and nobody in the establishment knows the name of the man. This is the last band I shall sew. After the fifteenth of April the shop is let to a glover and I am going to Lyon to my sister, and there is nothing in the world that will bring me back to this street, and the two years of the bands are finished, and I do not want to see your face again, my dear, because I have grown very fond of it and I am not going to be able to cry about it afterwards.*

Marion Herschlag put the strip of paper in her mouth and chewed it and swallowed it, in the second chapel on the left of the nave of the church of Saint-Roch, at ten minutes past six in the evening of the eighteenth of March 1943, and then she took the hat out of the parcel and put it on her head in front of the dark chapel, and settled the brim with her two hands, the way a woman does in front of a looking-glass, and walked out of the church into the rue Saint-Honoré with the hat on and the paper in her body.

There was nobody on the pavement.

---

She told the Colonel about the hat that night, at the table, with the ease of a woman of the sixteenth arrondissement, because a woman of that profession never tells a lie about an object that can be seen, and because at the end of the second week of the month of March in the year 1943 in the city of Paris there was one hat in the apartment at 12, avenue Henri-Martin that had to be explained before the man of the house noticed that it was new.

"Odile Marchand is closing. She is going to her sister at Lyon; the shop has been let to a glover."

"You will find another milliner."

"There is no other milliner," she said, "and the women of this city have begun to look at the hats of 1939 with a knife in their eyes, and the hats of 1943 are going to be made at home with the material of the curtains, and it is one of the eleven things about this war that a man is not able to see."

The Colonel cut his meat.

"In Lyon," he said, "there is a depot of the administration of the southeast, and I have a colleague there, and if the milliner of Madame Herschlag wishes a pass for the crossing of the line of demarcation, I am able to arrange it in two days, and it would be a favour done by me to a hat."

And she looked at the man across the table of the house of the Vasseurs, in the light of the requisitioned lamp, and understood for the first time in five months, with her body, the exact character of the thing she was doing: that she had spent the day in a church with the last paper of a network in her mouth, and that the man on the other side of the table would give her, for the price of a smile, the paper that would put the woman who had written it behind the wire of an internment camp in the department of the Rhône; and that the two of them were going to sit at that table for the spring and eat the food of the Generalgouvernement and be correct with each other in the way of two people of the same class, and that there was not one hour of it that was not, in the nature of things, a negotiation.

"No," she said. "She will manage. People of that kind always manage."

"People of that kind," said the Colonel of the intelligence service of the district of the army of Paris, "are the difficulty of Europe."

She looked at the ceiling, at the bandage of the curtain, and behind her ribs, in the dark, in the place where she kept the tailor's wife and the boy from the quai and the four words of Lis's handwriting, she was already doing arithmetic: two thousand kilometres, a city she was born in, a captain with grey eyes and a Polish mouth, a house with a garden and a district with thirty thousand men in it, and a list of names lying in a safe in the office of a man who had just decided that she was the only thing he had ever wanted.

"Where you go," said Marion Herschlag, in a voice that was, for the first time in five months, entirely and terribly sincere, "I have no other place to be."

He took her hand in the dark and held it like a man holding the rail of a ship, and she lay beside him, awake, with the duplicate key in the hatbox in the small north room, and the address of a key-cutter in the Auvergne burned into the part of her memory that never forgot an address, and she thought, with the flat and complete attention of a woman doing sums on the edge of an abyss: *he has decided to be happy. That is going to have to be my work from now on, and the work is going to be Warsaw.*
