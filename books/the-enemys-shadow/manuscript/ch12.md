# Chapter 12: The Ordonnanzoffizier

The office of the Ordonnanzoffizier of the intelligence section of the Warsaw district was on the ground floor of the Blue Palace, at the end of a corridor that had been the corridor of the household of a family of magnates in the eighteenth century and was now the corridor of the district of Warsaw in the fourth year of the occupation; and it had in it a desk, a table with a map on it under a green cloth, two telephones, a bookcase into which somebody had put the code books of the section in 1939, and a window on the inner courtyard, and the window was the reason for the arrangement that governed the following summer of her life, because it looked out onto the yard where the cars were parked, and through it, from the desk of the Ordonnanzoffizier, could be seen the movement of the district: who came, who went, which cars were loaded, which boxes were carried, and when the great Mercedes of the General with the flag on the wing was left standing at the door for eleven minutes with its engine running.

She came to the office four times in the third week of July, on four entirely legitimate errands, each of which required her to wait, and each of which had been manufactured by her with her craft: the papers of the household expenses of the Colonel, which went through the district's accounts; the form for the district's permission for her to be absent from the Generalgouvernement for a period of not more than eleven days, which was never going to be used; the invoice for the repair of the Colonel's field boots; and the four letters that had come from Berlin and needed, according to the Colonel's own instruction, *to be kept out of the hands of the clerks, because two of them are from my sister and one of them is from a General who writes badly.*

Each of the four errands was good for eleven to twenty minutes at the door of the office, by the simple method of there being no chair, and each of the four times she stood in the doorway of the room with the documents in her hand and watched the interior of the district of Warsaw pass through the yard of the Blue Palace; and each of the four times the captain who was the Ordonnanzoffizier of the section stood up from his desk and was, to the world, exactly what he appeared to be: a captain of twenty-eight who was being extremely correct with the woman of his colonel.

The fifth time, they spoke.

It was a Thursday, and the errand was the boots again, and the boots were unnecessary, and both of them knew it, and the room knew it, which in a German office meant that the clerk at the far end of the corridor knew that the Frenchwoman of the Oberst was standing in the door of the Ordonnanzoffizier's office for eleven minutes to hand over a paper that required four seconds; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the door of the office in the Blue Palace of Warsaw in the last week of July 1943 and made a decision that she had been carrying around for eleven days like a stone in a pocket.

"You are on the Riga list," she said, in French.

"I am on the Riga list."

"Is that what you want?"

He finished writing the line he was writing; blotted it; put the pen in the pen-rest; and looked up, and it — the pen, the blotter, the four seconds — told her, or would have told any German officer who was watching from the corridor, that the adjutant of the Oberst was being careful and correct with a woman who was talking to him about the eastern front.

"No," he said. "I want nothing that begins with an R and ends with Riga. I am a Polish boy from Warsaw whose father was a German consular official, and I have been given a German uniform and a German file, and there is only one thing in my life that I have ever wanted, and it was in a Botanic Garden in 1938, and I am not going to be able to have it, because I am also, in a manner of speaking, on the Riga list of God." He stood up. "The leather is repaired. There is a receipt. The Oberst does not need the receipt; you may keep it."

"Thank you."

"The weather in the east," said the captain, in a completely different voice, at a completely different volume, "is intolerable in August." And then, in Polish, quickly, quietly, with his face entirely blank: "There is a man of mine in the motor pool. My driver, Unteroffizier Klemens. He is from Poznań and he is a Pole in a German uniform like me, and he is the only person alive who can be in a room with me and the two of us not be in a German room; and if you ever need a car, in a manner that is not German, it can be arranged, and the arrangement looks like two soldiers who wish to go to a wedding."

She looked at the desk. She looked at the window with the yard behind it, where a lorry of the district was being loaded with crates by four Polish workmen under the eye of a Feldwebel, in the ordinary way of the world.

"One question," she said, in Polish.

"Ask it."

"Your file. The autumn of 1939. There are seven weeks in it that have been written over twice, and one of the men who wrote over them wrote *in Ordnung*, and the other one did not sign it."

He was quiet for a moment, and then he did something she had never seen a German officer do in a German office, which was to sit down on the edge of his own desk, sideways, with one foot on the floor, in the whole attitude of a Polish student in a corridor.

"In September 1939," he said, "I was twenty-four, and my father was a German consular official who had died in July, and my mother was a Warsaw woman who had died in 1937; and I was called up in the Polish reserve on the twenty-eighth of August, and I fought with my unit for eleven days, from the north, and on the eleventh day the unit was broken at a place called Modlin, and I was in a ditch with a broken wrist and two other men, and we walked east for four days and were taken by a German patrol at a crossroads; and the officer who took us looked at my papers, and saw that my father was a German official, and saw that I was, in the meaning of the law of 1939, a *Volksdeutscher*; and he did the thing that German officers did in those weeks about two thousand times, in both directions, in Poland: he made a mistake in my favour. He wrote in my file that I had been conscripted into the Polish army against my will, which was a lie; and he gave me a cup of coffee and a cigarette, and he put me on a train to the west, and I have been living in his kindness for four years, and there is not one day of my life since that crossroads at which I have been able to look at myself without that cup of coffee in my hand."

He looked at her.

"So the answer to your question is: the seven weeks were me, in a Polish uniform, with a Polish rifle, in a ditch, at Modlin; and the man who wrote *in Ordnung* over it was a German major of fifty who did not want a boy of twenty-four to be shot; and the man who did not sign it is me, because I have never once, in four years, been able to decide whether my life since that crossroads is a survival or a crime, and I have concluded that it is both, which is the peculiar position of everybody in Poland in the year 1943, and I do not require you to have an opinion about it."

She stood in the doorway with the receipt for a pair of boots in her hand.

"I am not going to have an opinion about it," she said. "But I will tell you the thing that nobody has ever told you, and that you have been waiting four years to hear from exactly one kind of person, and it is this: at Modlin, in the ditch, with a broken wrist, you were not a German officer and you were not a Pole; you were a boy of twenty-four who had lost a war in eleven days and was walking east because there was nowhere else to walk, and the fact that a German major gave you a cup of coffee afterwards does not make the ditch into a crime, and it does not make the coffee into a rescue. It makes it your life, and the rest of your life is going to be the question of what you do with the fact that somebody in a German uniform did you a kindness in 1939."

She turned to go.

"Madame Herschlag," he said, in French, at normal volume.

"Hauptmann?"

"You forgot the receipt." He held it out, deliberately, across the desk, and she crossed back and took it, and it took four seconds, and in the four seconds, in the yard outside the window, in the ordinary light of a Thursday in July, in the middle of the German administration of the district of Warsaw, Phillip Brandt said, at a volume of thirty centimetres from her ear, in Polish, without moving his face at all:

"There is a boy named Bolek in the Praga district who works in a chemist's shop on the Ząbkowska and who is going to be taken by the Kripo in the first week of October if somebody does not make him into a different boy with a different shop, and I know this because his name is on a list that came through this office on Tuesday, and I have been sitting on it since Tuesday, and I have another eleven days before I have to file it."

She did not move. She did not breathe differently. She looked at the receipt in her hand, and then she looked at him with the face of a woman of twenty-seven who has been in the room of a German officer for four minutes and is now going home, and she went out into the corridor of the Blue Palace and down the stairs and past the sentry and out into the courtyard, where Ehlers was sitting in the Mercedes with the engine running.

And she got into the car, and put the receipt in her bag, and let her mind work the way it had been built to work, which was very fast and entirely without feeling; and by the time the car was out of the gate she had made the following decision, and she never once in the sixteen months that followed regretted any part of it:

*I am going to have to tell him the truth about one thing only. I am going to have to tell him about Bolek.*

---

She did it on the Sunday, in the dining room, with the door open; and she did it in the way of a woman who has understood that in Warsaw in 1943 the only safe place for a conversation is a room with a door open and three people within earshot, and the only safe subject is one that can be interrupted.

The Colonel was in the drawing room with a map and a brandy; Anna was in the kitchen, thirty feet away, with her hearing, which was the good hearing of a Polish woman of fifty who had spent four years with the German occupation in her kitchen; and the captain was at the table with the week's paper in four piles in front of him.

She came in with the cold meat and the bottle.

"Herr Hauptmann. The Professor's son."

He did not look up from the paper. "What about him?"

"Nothing at all, in a German office. But I have been sitting at tea with the wives of this district for eleven weeks, and I have learned the thing that is worth more than a paper in a German office, which is what the wives say to each other about the Polish staff; and Frau Doktor Weingart said on Thursday, at the table of the Frau Generalin, over the cake, that the Kripo has a list of three hundred names out of the motor transport offices and the chemists' shops of the Praga district, and that they are going to take them in the autumn, because in the autumn they always take the men who have a trade, and that her husband says it is a mistake, because it makes the Poles hate the *Oberkommando* more than they already do; and then the whole table discussed it for eleven minutes, in the way they do, and I sat there, and I have decided to tell you."

He looked up.

"Civilians," he said, "should not carry lists in their heads."

"Civilians in this city carry the German administration in their heads, Herr Hauptmann, because the German administration is the weather." She set the bottle down. "There is a man of twenty-two who is a chemist's boy in the Praga district, and he is a nonentity; and he is the son of a caretaker on the housing register of a building in the Krasińskiego, whose certificate expires in the winter, and if the Kripo take the boy in October, then the caretaker's register of the whole family is opened, and the mother of the boy will be the answer to the one question that nobody in this administration has yet asked."

She had never in her life said anything so close to the truth in a room that belonged to the enemy. She said it in French, in the level, faintly bored way of a woman discussing the staff, with the cold meat in her hand; and she watched it land, in a captain of the Abwehr of twenty-eight, who had been sitting on a list since Tuesday in order not to file it.

"The mother," he said, without any expression, "is dead."

"Yes."

"And the father?"

"The father," said Marion Herschlag, "is a name on a Polish caretaker's certificate, and I do not know anything about him at all, and I never shall, because I am a Frenchwoman from Strasbourg who has been in this city for eleven weeks, and it is not my business to know anything about anybody in Warsaw; but I know that a boy of twenty-two who has the *Ausweis* of a chemist's shop is a boy who is protected, and that a boy whose name is on a list is a boy who will be taken, and that the difference between those two boys is an entry in a personnel file in a district office, and that there is exactly one officer in this district who can make the difference, and the difference, Herr Hauptmann, would cost that officer nothing at all except his life."

She went out of the dining room and into the kitchen, and put the plate down, and stood with her hands on the table for a moment while Anna Zaleska went on peeling; and neither of them said a word, because there was a German in the dining room with an open door.

There were eleven days between the corridor of the Blue Palace and the stamp.

She spent the first of them in the ordinary work of the household, and the second in the ordinary work of the household, and on the third she took the tram to the district and the boots receipt and stood at the door of the office of the Ordonnanzoffizier at the hour when the office had the slack of the afternoon in it.

The captain was not there.

The sergeant of the office was there, a man of forty from the Rhineland with the ribbon of the 1940 campaign and a bad hip, and he was filing the carbon copies of the day in the grey folders, and he said, without being asked, because a sergeant of an office of the district explains the office to a woman of a household the way a man explains a house: that the Herr Hauptmann was at the personnel section of the district, that the Herr Hauptmann had been at the personnel section since the Monday, that there was a business at the personnel section about the labour files of the district of the Praga, that the labour files of the district of Warsaw were at this hour being brought to the administrations of the labour offices of the Generalgouvernement and put on the German paper, and that there was not one man of the district who was not in the middle of it.

She stood with the receipt for a pair of boots in her hand and looked at the grey shelves of the office of the Ordonnanzoffizier of the intelligence section of the Warsaw district, and did not move her face, and heard, in the ordinary voice of a German sergeant of forty talking about his work, the exact size of the thing she had done.

She had given a captain of the Abwehr of the district two pieces of her own weight: the fact that she had an interest in a boy of the Praga with a stamped file, and the fact that she was willing to approach a German officer on her own account. There is no version of that transaction in which the officer keeps it clean. He either does nothing, and is a man who has heard a woman of the household of his own colonel ask him a thing and refused her; or he does something, and every piece of paper he uses belongs to the German administration of a city of a million people, with a number and a signature and a paragraph in a file; and she had put him in the second of those positions with a sentence in a corridor and a receipt in her hand.

The sergeant said: "The boots were the second pair this month, gnädige Frau."

"They were."

"There is a paper for the boots. The Herr Hauptmann signs the paper for the boots." He said it with the innocence of a man who has worked in an office for twenty years. "The Herr Hauptmann asked me to say, if the gnädige Frau came, that the boots are in the store of the district and that the paper of the boots is in the folder of the household, which is the third of the folders on the shelf behind me, and that the gnädige Frau is not to be troubled with the paper of the boots at any hour of the day."

Which was, in the language of an office of the intelligence service of the German army, the longest and most careful sentence that a man of twenty-eight was able to send to a woman across two metres of a floor, and it said: *it is being seen to; do not come back; do not be seen coming back.*

---

On the Saturday there was the wives' table, at the house of the Frau Generalin of the administration on the Ujazdowskie, and she sat in the green chair by the window and drank the tea and listened to the wives.

There were nine of them, German women of Warsaw between the ages of thirty and sixty; and they talked for the afternoon about the labour campaign, because in the summer of 1943 the labour campaign was the only thing in the Generalgouvernement that was talked about, in the German houses and in the Polish queues and in the offices: the round-ups in the streets, the placards of the labour offices, the boys taken from the market of the Wola and the girls taken from the tram, the transports of the district of the Praga and the factories of the north of the city emptied of their young men and filled again with the ones who had been caught. The wife of the medical officer said that the labour office had been given a quota of fourteen thousand for the district of Warsaw. The wife of the officer of the supply section said that her gardener had gone, and that there was nobody to cut the grass, and that she had written to the office about it. The Frau Generalin said that it was a necessary thing and a terrible thing and that it was not the business of the wives of the administration to have opinions about it.

And the woman of the Colonel of the intelligence section of the district sat in the green chair and drank the tea and said, in her turn, in the French of a woman of Strasbourg, that in her country the young men had been taken in the same way in the second year, in the streets of the towns of the south, and that a woman of France knew the look of a street where the boys had gone, and that there was no woman in Europe of any nation who did not know it, and that she thought about it as little as possible because thinking about it did not return a single one of them.

It was the safest sentence she had made in five months, and it bought her the afternoon of the wives' table, and it was not a lie.

And in the tram, on the way home, in the car for the German administration with the wooden seats and the notices, she looked at the city through the window and did the arithmetic that she had been refusing to do since Thursday, and the arithmetic was: *fourteen thousand in this district; and one boy of the Praga in the middle of them, whose file is a stamped file in a drawer of the labour office and whose stamp, if the camp comes to the Praga, is the only piece of paper in the city that will stand between him and the transport; and I have asked the Ordonnanzoffizier for a stamp.*

The tram stopped at the corner of the Krucza and a German officer got on with a paper under his arm, and she moved her bag on her knees, and looked at the houses of the city sliding past her, and did not permit herself, in the eleven minutes to the Mokotowska, to think about anything else.

---

The Colonel came home to the bed on the Sunday night and had her the way he had her when he had been in the district for eleven hours and had not eaten.

He was fifty-four, and he had been at war since 1914 with an interruption of twenty years, and in the bed he was a man of the province: without a word, without a light, without a hand on her face; and she had learned in the first month of the arrangement in Paris that the work of the bed was in her hands and her breath and the timing of her own body, and she did the work the way she had been trained to do it in the long room in Kraków, which is to say she did it with her attention, and, on the level above the body, with the part of her that in a bed in Warsaw in 1943 was always writing: *he is going to talk afterwards; the talking is the work; the body is only the door of the talking.*

He talked afterwards.

He lay on his back in the dark with his arm across her body and the cigarette in his left hand, and he said that the labour campaign had a paper behind it, and that the paper had been written in Berlin in the spring and signed in the spring, and that the administration of the east was at this hour in the position of the wagon behind a horse that has been frightened; and then he said that the district of the intelligence service had been asked for its assessment of the effect of the campaign on the security position of the city, and that he had written the assessment with his own hand in one page, and that the one page had said what he always said, which was that a population which is being hunted in the street is a population which has nothing to lose and that there was no intelligence service in the history of the world that had ever been able to work in a city of that kind.

He smoked.

"You are quiet," he said.

"I am thinking about the page."

"About the page."

"That a man writes a page for an office and the office puts the page in a file and the file is read by nobody, and in the time the boys are being taken in the streets; and that the woman in this bed is in the household of the man who wrote the page, and has a French passport and a card of residence, and is not taken, and will not be taken." She said it in the flat voice of the dark, with her face away from him, and it was the truth, and it was in the bed because she had spent four days thinking about a boy in the Praga and had not been able to say a word about him to any human being in the world. "It is not an opinion, Friedrich. It is a fact about the two of us, and there are facts in this house that we do not say out loud, and I am saying one of them because I am tired and because you asked me why I was quiet."

He was silent for a long time.

"You are the only person in this city," he said at last, "who has ever said a thing to me in this bed that I have not been able to answer."

And then, in the dark, with the cigarette going out in his hand, he did the thing that she was never able to explain afterwards: he put his hand flat on her stomach, the way he did when he wanted to know whether she was lying, and left it there, and it was not a question and it was not a threat, and in the five months it was the only physical contact in that house that had nothing in it for him at all.

---

On the eleventh day she took the tram to the Praga at the hour of the middle of the morning, on a legitimate errand of the household, with the basket, past the labour office of the district at the corner of the Ząbkowska, and she did not look at the door of the labour office, and she did not look at the chemist's shop, and she bought the onions and the salt and the two hundred grams of the coffee substitute at the shop of a Pole of the Praga on the Brukowa, and paid for them with the money of the household, and came home the long way, by way of the bridge.

There was a queue of eleven women under the arch of the bridge, waiting for the labour office, and a placard of the labour administration on the wall with the German text on it and the Polish text under the German text, and the notice of the transports of the male population of the district of the Praga born between the years 1919 and 1925; and the queue stood in the light of the middle of the day and said nothing at all, in the way that a queue of women of Warsaw stood and said nothing in 1943, and there was a girl in a headscarf in the middle of the queue of about twenty years of age who was holding a certificate of employment and reading the placard the way a person reads a sentence that is being read by everyone.

She walked past it with the basket on her arm.

On the eleventh of August, in the afternoon, in the city, in the office of the district labour administration on the Dzika, a clerk of fifty with a rubber stamp in his hand opened a file on a boy of twenty-two named Bolek Stankiewicz, chemist's assistant of the Ząbkowska, and read the accompanying note, which was signed by a Hauptmann of the intelligence section of the district, and which said that the man was, in the opinion of the personnel section, *unentbehrlich* — indispensable — to the chemical supply arrangement of the district hospital of Praga; and the clerk put the stamp in the ink and stamped it, and put the file back in the drawer, and the boy in the chemist's shop on the Ząbkowska lived, and worked, and stole, in the manner of his generation, and did not know, and never knew, that his life had been saved twice: once by a sister in a good coat who had broken the first rule of her existence for him, and once by a captain who had signed his name on a piece of paper in a German office with his own hand.

The Colonel came home at eight o'clock that evening and had his dinner and said nothing about anything, and at half past ten he took Marion Herschlag into the big bedroom and did what he did with her, in the way he had done it since April: holding her down on the blanket with one hand over her mouth and the weight of the east on top of her, in the dark, in the particular manner of a man who had been given a district and needed feeding; and when it was over, and he had put his arm over his eyes, and the room had gone quiet, she lay beside him in the dark with her hands at her sides, in the exact position of a woman who has been used by a colonel of the Abwehr, and she was not there at all.

She was, in the eleven minutes after it, in a garden on the Mokotowska in the dark, standing three metres from a man who had said her name.

She was in a photograph clipped into a file: a boy of nineteen in a thin shirt in front of a glasshouse, holding a closed book.

She was in a ditch at Modlin in 1939 with a broken wrist and a cup of coffee; and in a doorway in Paris in a good grey coat with a book about the botany of the Kampinos Forest; and in the corridor of a train between Forbach and Breslau, making a decision on the thirty-one hours.

And she was in a room in Kraków in the winter of 1940, standing in front of a small man with a bad heart and a bad fire, saying the fourth rule back to him, the one he had made her repeat four times: *never love; it is not a moral instruction, it is arithmetic.*

*It is arithmetic,* she thought, in the dark, in the big bedroom of a doctor's house in the city of her birth, with a hand over the place where her mouth had been.

*And the arithmetic says that a woman who has begun to be in a garden when her body is in a bed has already lost the only argument she was ever going to be able to win.*
