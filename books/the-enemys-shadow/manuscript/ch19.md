# Chapter 19: The Long Knife

The General went to Kraków on the nineteenth of July.

He left at six in the morning, in the Mercedes, with Ehlers driving and a second car following, because a General of the intelligence of a district did not travel in one car in July 1944; and he stood in the hall of the house on the Bagatela in his greatcoat at a quarter to six with his gloves in his hand, and he said, "Three days. The twenty-second at the latest. If the telephone from the Palace comes, you are to take the message and write it down, and give the paper to nobody, not even to the Oberst, and I mean that."

"Friedrich."

"Yes."

"Come back," she said.

He looked at her for a moment from the doorway with the grey light of a July morning behind him, and she saw, in his face, the single unguarded thing that had ever been on it: a man of fifty-five in the fifth year of a war, leaving a house he did not own, in a city he did not own, to a woman who was the last object of his life.

Then he went down the marble stairs and got into the car and was driven away north, and the house on the Bagatela was hers for seventy-two hours.

---

She went about it the way she had gone about the keys in Paris in the wax and the candle, which is to say with a page of arithmetic rather than a mood.

The cook was sent out at nine with money and an errand that could not be completed before three in the afternoon: a list of eleven items, four of them unobtainable, on the far side of the river in the Praga district, and a chit for a pass to cross the bridge and come back. She did it in the kitchen, in German, with the manner of a mistress of a house organising a dinner; and the German woman from Bydgoszcz put on her coat and took the chit and the money and went off at half past nine with a basket on her arm, and the house on the Bagatela was empty of every soul but one at twenty minutes to ten on the morning of the twentieth of July 1944.

She did the rest in order.

She locked the front door, which was never locked in daylight, and the side door to the garden, which was never locked at all.

She went into the study, and stood in front of the photograph of Anneliese in the garden in 1931, with the dog at her feet, and looked at it for eleven seconds, which was a courtesy she had not planned and permitted herself anyway.

Then she took the photograph off the wall.

The safe was an Organisation Todt pattern of 1939, set into the plaster of a study that had been built for the papers of a lecturer in chemistry; and its dial was brass, with a knurled edge, and the plate under the dial carried the serial number she had read off it with a duster in her hand in the house on the Mokotowska fourteen months before.

Nine. Turn right. Four. Turn left. Three. Turn right. Six.

She did it with her eyes closed, on the fourth turn, which is the trick of safe-breaking that no man in the world will ever teach a woman, and which she had worked out for herself in a bed in the house on the Mokotowska in May 1944 while a General slept beside her: *let the fingers do the number; the fingers are mine; the eyes belong to the room.*

The fifth lever gave.

The door opened on the smell of cold steel and paper, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna knelt in front of it on the floor of a German study, in a grey dress, in the middle of the morning of the twentieth of July 1944, and took the life of a colonel of the German intelligence service out of a box in a wall, one item at a time, in the order in which he had stacked it.

The portfolio of Anneliese: forty-one sheets of paper in her hand, watercolours of gardens, and a lock of fair hair in a twist of tissue, and four letters, and a programme of a concert in Leipzig, 1933.

The deeds of the Pomeranian land: two sheets, with a red seal, and a map, and a tax notice of 1943.

Ilse's letters: eleven of them, in a nurse's round hand, from Berlin, dated through the war; and the last one, from June 1944, containing a sentence that Krystyna read twice and never afterwards forgot: *Friedrich, if the whole thing is ending, come while you still can, and bring nothing, and do not bring anybody who is not ours, because in the end a house in the country is only a room with two names on the door.*

The Verdächtigenlisten: eleven pages, typed, with the carbon copy of a schedule of the German dispositions in the Warsaw district attached, and the names of two hundred and six Poles and eleven Germans, with the names of the eleven Germans underlined in ink and marked with a small cross in the same hand.

The Fall *Wotan* folder: the same page she had photographed in Paris in April 1943, and now four more pages with it, and a map, and a table of railway capacities, and a copied order of the 9th Army of the thirtieth of June 1944, and a sheet of the district's own requisition of rolling stock for the last week of July: eleven trains, with the destinations written against them, and the destinations were not in the east.

And last, at the back of the safe, a brown envelope, unsealed, on which was written a word in ink, in the private hand of Friedrich von Reuth, and the word was a name.

*Marion.*

She sat on the floor of the study of the house on the Bagatela and opened the brown envelope of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and emptied it into her lap; and what came out was four things.

A paper, in his hand, dated the ninth of April 1944 — which was the date that the office in Kraków had written on the papers of a woman of Strasbourg in the winter of 1942, and which was also, as she had learned in a bedroom in Paris in the month of the July of 1943, the birthday of Anneliese von Reuth, and she had never once, in the two years, been able to decide whether the woman in the wardrobe in the long room had chosen the date of the cover out of an office's arithmetic or out of a dead wife's head — and headed with the words *Mein letzter Wille* — my last will — and in it a bequest to a woman described as *Marion Herschlag, French citizen, of Strasbourg, presently resident in my household*, of the sum of eleven thousand marks held in a personal account at the Deutsche Bank in Breslau; and a clause, in the same hand, which said: *I have no children. My sister Ilse will understand. This is not a payment; it is the only thing that has ever been mine to leave.*

A photograph of a woman she did not know, in a bathing dress, on a beach, laughing, 1928.

A small square of paper, folded twice, on which was written, in pencil, in a hand she had never seen, eleven Polish surnames, with figures against them in a second column: it was a list from the offices of the district, and it was in the hand of a clerk, and against four of the names there were ticks. She would understand, much later, that it was a page from the card index of the Sicherheitsdienst, and that the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw had taken it out of the office of the Sicherheitsdienst in the month of April 1944 and had put it in his own safe and had never once spoken of it, and that the surname in the middle of the eleven, with a tick against it, was *Zaleski*.

And a card.

A stiff card, four by six centimetres, of the kind that the district administration used for the registration of aliens, with the eagle of the Reich at the top left, and the stamped word *Ausweis* across the corner, and — typed, by a German typewriter in a German office, at some hour of some day in the spring of 1944 — four lines in the middle of it.

*Herschlag, Marion. Geb. 9.4.1919 in Straßburg. Französin. Angestellte der Bezirksverwaltung.*

And on the back, in pencil, in the hand of a Hauptsturmführer of the Sicherheitsdienst with a green notebook and a fine clerk's script: *Brauchbar. Prüfen im Sommer.*

Usable. To be tested in the summer.

She sat on the floor in her own house on the Bagatela with a colonel's private existence in her lap and a card in her hand, and looked at the pencil sentence on the back of the card for eleven breaths; and the arithmetic, when it came, came to her in a single piece and it was not an arithmetic at all.

The General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw had taken a card out of the office of the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw and put it in his own safe, in an envelope with her name on it, among the letters of his sister and the papers of his dead wife, and he had never spoken of it, because a man does not speak of the thing that he has done; and the thing that he had done was this: he had gone into the office of the man with the green notebook at some hour in the spring, when the district was full of reports and the man with the notebook was not at his desk, and he had taken a card out of an index of two hundred thousand cards, and he had put it in his pocket, and he had done it as a General of the Wehrmacht's intelligence service for a woman of Strasbourg who had never once asked him for anything.

He had not known how to say it. That was the entire horror of the leaf of paper in her lap.

In the two years of the arrangement, in the four hundred nights of the bed of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, he had never once been able to find the sentence with which to tell a woman that he had taken a card with her name on it out of the index of the man who was hunting him, and had kept it in the safe between his sister's letters and his wife's watercolours; and the sentence he had found instead, in the month of December 1943, in the dark, in a bed, on the night of the Wola, had been the sentence about the combination of the safe: *nine, four, thirty-six.*

He had hidden her name from the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw, and then he had given her the key to the place where he had hidden it, and he had done both of those things without ever once saying a word about either of them, and she had spent four months spending that, and the city of Warsaw was going to be burned in thirteen days.

She put the card in the lining of her coat, with the rest of it.

She put the Pomeranian deeds back in the portfolio; she put Ilse's letters back in their bundle with the string; she put the will back in the brown envelope, and the envelope back at the back of the safe, because the envelope was his and not hers and she was not going to be the woman who took the last thing a man had; and she took, instead, one thing only: the folder marked *Fall "Wotan"*, with its five sheets and its map and its table of railway capacities and its copy of the order of the 9th Army.

She closed the safe. She turned the dial twice on the last number, the way a professional does. She hung the photograph of Anneliese back on its nail, and stood back and looked at it, and moved the bottom left corner half a centimetre, because the papers were going to be looked at in the last week of the war by men with boots and files, and a photograph that hangs crooked in a German house is a photograph that has been moved.

She went out of the study and up the stairs, and put the folder in the suitcase in her room, under the winter coat, and sat down on the edge of the bed with her hands in her lap, and looked at the window for four minutes; and in the four minutes, in the house on the Bagatela, there was no sound in the world: no cook in the kitchen, no telephone, no car in the street; and it was, in the fifth year of the war, the only four minutes of silence that the city of Warsaw gave her.

---

There was a way out of the garden.

It was the way that a Polish cook had described to a Frenchwoman in a kitchen in the house on the Mokotowska in the month of March, in a paragraph about a horse: over the wall, down between two chestnuts, through the side gate, into the yard of the block in the next street, and the yard, and the laundry with the three doors and the woman who was the cousin's wife of Piotr Zaleski, who kept a horse and washed the linen of eleven German households of the district of Warsaw.

She went through it at four o'clock in the afternoon of the twentieth of July with the suitcase in her hand.

The laundry was a long brick shed with a boiler and four tubs and a mangle and lines of wet sheets across the yard between the wall and the trees, and the woman who owned it was about fifty, with arms like a man's and a cigarette that she never took out of the corner of her mouth, and she did not look up from the tub when the side gate opened; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna walked between the wet sheets to the third door of the laundry, and stood in it, and waited, in the smell of soap and wet cotton and coal smoke, with the folder of the German intelligence service of the district of Warsaw under her arm inside a suitcase.

He came at quarter past four, through the second door, in a shirt with the sleeves rolled, and stopped in the middle of the yard between the sheets, three metres away.

"Ehlers has the car in the Nowy Świat," he said. "The General is at the King's Castle in Kraków, and I have the day, and the estate of the Oberst of the supply section in the north of the city which is my cover for it." He looked at the suitcase. "It is done."

"It is done."

"Then give it to me now, and go back through the wall, and I will be at the Blue Palace at six, and the paper will be in the hands of the district by eleven tonight, and none of it will be in the same room as you." He put out his hand for the suitcase. "Krystyna. Give me the case."

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the doorway of a laundry in the yard of a house in the Bagatela, with a suitcase in her hand, in the last days of the fifth year of the war, and looked at the man who had been standing in a Botanic Garden in June 1938 with a book he had never read; and she did not give him the suitcase, and she did not move, and what she said, in the smell of the wet sheets and the coal smoke of a Warsaw laundry, she said in Polish, quietly, and it was the only time in four years that she had made a decision that was not arithmetic.

"Not in the yard," she said. "Inside."

The room at the end of the laundry was the office of the woman with the horse: two metres by three, a table, a chair, a lamp, an ironing board, a window with a muslin curtain and a line of the shirts of an Oberst of the supply section hanging over it, in the afternoon.

She shut the door.

And after that there was nothing in the world that was not two people and the length of a war, and it did not happen the way it happens in the world of the barracks and the French novels of the twenty years; it happened the way it happens to two people of thirty who have been standing in the same city for a year, in the same rooms, in the same weather, one of them being had four hundred times by a man of fifty-five in a German house, and the other one watching it from a doorway with a file in his hand.

It was slow: it was, at the beginning, almost nothing at all; a hand on a wrist, and the shirts of an Oberst of the supply section of the district of Warsaw on a line over the window, and the noise of the ironing board being set against the wall; and she stood in the middle of a laundry office in the last days of July 1944 and let a man take her coat off her shoulders, and she looked at him the time, and there was no arithmetic in her head at all; there was a room, and a smell of soap, and a man's hands, and the noise of her own breathing, which she had not heard in five years.

And what she discovered, in that hour, in a laundry office in the inner yard of a German house in the city of her birth, was the thing that no one had ever told her in a room in Kraków, because in Kraków they taught the four rules and the rules were about the death; and the thing that no one had told her was that a woman's body is not a filing cabinet; that it is a body; that it can, in a warm room with the door shut and an ironing board against the wall, and a man who says her own name; and that in the four hundred nights in the German house, in the bed of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, in the four years of the arrangement, she had been present at it, and had never once been in the room.

Afterwards he lay on the boards on the two coats with her head on his arm, and there were the shirts of a German Oberst on the line over the window, and the light of a Polish afternoon came in, and out in the yard a woman of fifty with arms like a man's ran a mangle, over and over, in the same dull regular sound.

"Krystyna Stankiewiczówna," he said, out loud, into the room.

"Yes."

"I have said it in my head a thousand times," he said, "in an office, in a corridor, on a stair, at a railway station in a March, in a garden; and I have never once said it out loud, because in the war there is not a single man in the German administration of Poland who is permitted to say the name, and I have said my own mother's name eleven times in five years, in the dark, into a pillow." He looked at the ceiling of a laundry office. "There is a house on the Bagatela with a steel box in the wall of the study, and it has a certain card in it with your the name on it, in the German of a German office, and the man who owns the house put it there, and I have known that since the April, because the office of the Ordonnanzoffizier is the office of the safe."

"Phillip."

"The the last thing that I have to say to you," he said, "is the thing that I have never once been able to say in the year, and it is the plan. There is a man in the Klarysew, in the villa of a friend of my father, and there is a car in the garage of the district which is my business, and there is the road through the Kampinos, and the forest, and there is a monastery in the Silesia, and a name, and a wireless set; and there is no man in the world who is going to look for a woman of twenty-seven and a captain of the Abwehr of the district of Warsaw in the same car, on the same night, in the last week of the July; and the plan is the only thing that I have, and I am not going to be able to tell it to you in the office of the Ordonnanzoffizier of the district of Warsaw, and I am telling you it now, on the boards of a laundry, with the shirts of an Oberst of the supply section of the district of Warsaw on a line over the window, and it is the truth, and I want you to say a single word."

She lay on the floor of a laundry office in Warsaw in the last days of July 1944 with her head on the arm of a captain of the German army and the ceiling of a Polish shed above her, and she looked at a spider's web in the corner of a window frame, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic took a long time, and at the end of it there was only one item in the column, and the item was not survival.

"No," she said.

"Krystyna."

"No." She sat up; and she looked at him; and she was, at that moment, in the room, with the shirts over the window and the mangle going in the yard, exactly what she had been made to be: a woman of twenty-seven with a folder of German intelligence under her arm and a city about to burn. "There is a thing I have not told you, and it is the last thing I have, and I am going to tell you now, in this room, and then I am going to pick up my coat and walk out of that door and be Marion Herschlag on the Bagatela at six o'clock, and I want you to understand it, because this is the last time in the war that the two of us are going to be in the same room." She put both hands on his chest, flat, and left them there. "There is a plan in this city for the first of August at five o'clock in the afternoon, and there are going to be a hundred thousand men in the streets, and them are going to be killed, and the paper in the case, and the arithmetic, and the year in the house of a General, is the small thing that a woman of twenty-seven is able to put against that; and I am the woman of the small thing, and I am not able to leave the house on the Bagatela, and I am not able to get into a car with a captain of the Wehrmacht and go to a monastery in the Silesia, and I am telling you the truth, in a laundry, with your mother's Polish name in my mouth, and you have to believe me, because I am never going to say it to you again."

He lay on the boards with the shirts over the window.

Then he said: "I know."

"You know what?"

"I have known it since the March," said Phillip Brandt. "I know the plan is a thing the men of this city decided for the men of the war, and I know the hour, because there is not a Pole in the district of the Warsaw who has not been talking about it since the June, and there is not a German in the administration who has not been reading about it in a weekly report, and the two of them are the same week, and the world knows the hour, and the world is going to be in the streets on the first of August, and I am a captain of the German army and my mother was a Warsaw woman and I have seven weeks written over twice in the file of the Wehrmacht." He sat up. "So if there is no car and no monastery and no Klarysew, then tell me what there is, and I will do it, and I will do it without a question, and if it is a thing that ends on the first of August in a room in a house on the Bagatela, then tell me the room and the hour, and I will be at the door."

She looked at him for a long time.

"There is the laundry," she said. "And the woman with the horse; and if the city is in the streets, then the thing that a woman with a General's safe in her arms is able to do is to be in a place where the German army in the district will not look for the house of the General; and there is a plan, and I am not going to tell it to you in a laundry, on the last afternoon, with the shirts of an Oberst of the supply section over the window; and there is one thing more, and then I am going to go, because the cook comes back at the six."

"Say it."

"On the first of August," said Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, "you are to be on the Wola side of the city, with the men of your mother's people, and I am not going to see you again in this war, and you are going to be alive, and you are going to be a Pole with a Polish wife in a Polish city in a year that neither of us is going to be able to imagine in this room." She stood up, and put her coat on, and stood in the middle of the office of a laundry in a Warsaw July; and she said the last sentence that she said to him in the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and she said it in Polish, in her own voice, and she said it without one single tear in her, which she was afterwards never able to explain to herself:

She came back to the house on the Bagatela at half past four in the afternoon of the twentieth of July 1944, at the hour of the ordinary end of the afternoon of a German administration, with the street in the ordinary state of a week in the middle of the summer: the chestnuts along the avenue of the Ujazdowskie in their full leaf, the two sentries at the gate of the district command in their shirtsleeves with the tunics on the hooks inside the box, and the Polish woman who sold the flowers at the corner of the Krucza sitting on her stool with the buckets and not one customer in the afternoon.

The furier of the staircase met her in the hall.

He was sixty-one, a reservist of the district administration of the city of Posen who had been on that staircase for four years, and he had, in the four years, never once come up the steps of the house to meet her; and he came up the steps of the house, in the middle of the afternoon of the twentieth of July 1944, in the greatcoat of the district, in the heat, with his cap on and one hand in the air, and said:

"Gnädige Frau, the General of the district telephoned from Kraków at two o'clock, and the General of the district is on the road and will be in Warsaw at nine o'clock tonight, and the General of the district has ordered that the car is to be at the station of the Warsaw West at eight o'clock, and that the household is to be informed, and that the house is to be in the ordinary state, and that the Frau of the household is not to be told anything about anything, which is the order of the General of the district, and I am giving the order of the General of the district to the Frau of the household exactly as the telephone gave it to me."

"Why is the General coming home?"

"I do not know, gnädige Frau."

"Why is the flag of the district on the gate half-mast, Furier?"

The man put his hand up to his face.

"Because at a quarter to one this afternoon the wireless of the district carried the announcement of the office of the Führer," said the furier, "and the German administration of this city has been at the wireless since a quarter to one, and there has been an attempt upon the life of the Führer of the German Reich in the headquarters of the east, and there is an address of the Führer to the German people at midnight, and the garrisons of the east are at the alert, and there is not one officer of the district of Warsaw who is in the building of the command at this hour, and there is not one man in the German administration of this city who has been told anything at all."

She stood in the marble hall of the house on the Bagatela with the case of the district's papers in her hand — the five sheets of the Fall **Wotan** in the waxed envelope in the lining of the case, and behind the boiler of the laundry in the yard of the building in the next street there was nothing at all, because she had moved it to the laundry on the fourteenth of the month and had not been able to say to any human being in the world that the safe of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw had been opened in the middle of the morning of the same day — and looked at the furier of the staircase in the heat, and did the arithmetic of the twentieth of July.

It came out in the order of the danger:

*The wireless of Europe is carrying the news of an attempt on the life of the head of the German state, and the administration of this district is going to spend the night and the following day doing what an administration does at a moment of that kind, which is to open the files and check the lists and look at the men; and a general of the intelligence service of a district who comes home from Kraków in the middle of the night of that day is a general who has been in a room with the staff of the army group of the south for eight hours; and there is going to be, in the next four days, a purge in the German administration of the east of the kind that the German administration of the east has three times in the year, and it is going to touch the men of the offices and the officers of the staffs and the wives of the men of the offices and the households of the officers of the staffs; and there is a card with my name on the front of it and four words of pencil on the back of it in the index of the office of the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw, and the man who wrote the four words of pencil on the back of it is the one man in the German administration of this city who does not do what a German administration does at a moment of that kind, because he has spent the war doing the other thing, and the other thing is to check the card index of the population of a city against the paper of the offices of the district.*

She went up the marble stair with her hand flat on the rail.

At the head of the stair, in the corridor of the first floor, there was a captain of the ordnance of the district of Warsaw in a tunic with the collar hooked, with a of the papers of the office in his left arm, and he had been standing in the corridor for the time that the furier of the staircase had been on the stair, and the two of them looked at each other across the eleven metres of the corridor of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw in the middle of the afternoon of the twentieth of July 1944.

"Gnädige Frau."

"Herr Hauptmann."

"There is a business of the office," he said, in German, at the volume of an officer of the district reporting to the wife of his general on the state of a corridor. "The district is at the alert until the morning of the twenty-second. The the officers of the staff are at the building of the command for the night, and the men of the guard of the house of the General of the district have been doubled, and there is a NCO of the Feldgendarmerie on the garden gate until the morning of the twenty-second, and nobody is to come into this house or out of it between the eight o'clock of the evening and the six o'clock of the morning except the household and the officers of the staff of the district, and the telephone of the study is to be answered by the Ordonnanzoffizier of the district and by nobody else in the house for the night." He looked at her for four seconds, and in the four seconds the sentence that he did not say was in the corridor with the two of them: *the case, the laundry, the wall of the yard, the eleven men on the guard if it goes wrong tonight.* "The Frau of the household will be in the small room, and the Frau of the household will not be in the study, and the Frau of the household will not be in the garden, and the Frau of the household will not be at the window of the landing of the first floor for the night, and if the Frau of the household has a thing to say to a man of this house in the night, the man will be in the passage of the first floor at the eleven o'clock and at the one o'clock and at the three o'clock, and there is not one other thing in the world that the man is able to be for the Frau of the household in the night."

"Then the Frau of the household is going to be in the small room," she said, "and the man is going to be in the passage at the eleven o'clock, and the Frau of the household is going to be able to be asleep at the eleven o'clock, because the house has been in the ordinary state for the four days of the week and there is nothing at all in the house that is not in the ordinary state."

"The the house is in the ordinary state," said Phillip Brandt.

And then the bells of the churches of the city of Warsaw began to ring for the evening of the twentieth of July 1944, in the way that the bells of the churches of a city of the east ring at the end of a day, and there was not one Pole in the quarter who knew the reason that the bells of the city of Warsaw were ringing on the evening of the twentieth of July 1944, and the four days that followed, in the house of the General of the intelligence service of the district of the city of Warsaw, were the strangest of the war.

"And if it goes the other way, and we are both in this city in the autumn, then I am going to come and find you, on the ruins of the Nowy Świat, and I am not going to be Marion Herschlag, and I am not going to be the woman of the General, and there is not going to be a single man in the world who is going to be able to tell the two of us apart from the rest of the people of Warsaw."
