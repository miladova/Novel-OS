# Chapter 17: The First Frost

The spring of 1944 came to Warsaw in the third week of April, all at once, the way it did in that city: four days of wind, and then the chestnuts.

The city filled up with the ordinary of the extraordinary. The queues went on; the German papers went on printing the verbs of consolidation; and under it, in every tram and every courtyard, in a population of a million Poles who had spent five years learning to read the weather of an empire, Warsaw knew, in a way it had never known anything before, that the German army in the east was coming back.

It could be seen from the window of a house on the Bagatela.

The transports came through the Warszawa Gdańska station all day and all night, westbound: what the district called, in its paperwork, the *Rückführung*; and a woman at a window on the first floor of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district could, in an hour of an April evening, count eleven trains: the flat cars with the vehicles, the covered cars with the men, the horse traffic, the hospitals, the paperwork, the booty, the machines, the guns, and — on the third train of the eleventh hour — a an open flat car with the furniture of an officers' mess on it, strapped down, a piano on the top of the pile under a tarpaulin, going west in a Polish April.

She counted them for a month, from that window, and she never once wrote a number down, and by the middle of May she had, in the back of her head, the schedule of the German retreat out of the east, which she had been building the way she built everything: patiently, one train at a time, in the order of the clock.

---

Kruk came for her on the eleventh of May.

He came the way the men of that organisation came, which is to say with the entire apparatus of a country's conspiracy behind him and the appearance of nothing whatever: a motorcar of the city's gasworks, a driver in a cap, a route through the Praga side, and a flat on the fourth floor of a building on the Brzeska which had been a furrier's before the war and had eleven fur coats still hanging in the back room under a sheet, in a Polish May, because the population of the quarter had been taken out of it in 1943, and the flat was, in the fifth year of the war, the safest set of rooms in the city of Warsaw.

He was fifty-two, and she had known him before the war: Colonel Stefan Wilczek, of the 21st Lancers, who had been in her father's library in 1936 in a good double-breasted suit, arguing about the League of Nations with the confidence of a man who had never in his life had a conversation with a peasant.

He was in a bad grey jacket now, with a pair of pliers in his hand, because when she came into the room he had been doing something to a wireless set on the table; and he looked at her for a long moment and did not get up, and then said, in Polish, in the manner of a man of his class before the war:

"Panna Krystyna. Sit down. I have four things to say to you and no time, because I have to be in the Mokotów district by the four o'clock."

"Then say them."

"The first is that your father is dead and I know it and I have not visited the grave, and there is a reason, and the reason is that in the month of November there were four men of the organisation in the Powązki cemetery at that funeral, and if I had been the fifth, the city would have had the organisation in a room on the Szucha in the January." He set the pliers down. "I am sorry. In the ordinary sense of the word, which in this war has no meaning, I am the sorriest man in Warsaw."

"Say the second thing."

"The second is that I know what you have done for the last year." He looked at her with the grey eyes of a cavalry officer of the old Poland, and there was no warmth in them at all, and no cold either — only an assessment. "I know about the number of the safe and about the page of the schedule and about the boy in the chemist's shop in the Praga and about the manner of the manner in which you have been able to be in the house of a German General for a year and a half and not be a person; and I want you to understand that in the history of this organisation there has never been a woman in your position, and that there will never be one again, and that when this is over, the record of what you have done is going to be a page in a file, and the page is going to have your file name on it, and your file name is WESPER, and the page will say: *no information of the value of the third order was ever lost, and no false information was ever passed, and no member of the network was ever compromised.* And that is the second thing, and it is the only decoration that this city is going to be able to give you, and I am giving it to you now, in a room above a furrier's, because after June there is not going to be time."

"Say the third."

"The third is the demand, and I am not going to be polite about it, because there is no politeness in the thing. The the German line in the east is going to be in the Vistula by the last week of July. The the Red Army is going to be on the bank of the river of the city in the last days of July, and the government of Poland in London has decided that the Polish army of the underground is going to take the city of Warsaw from the Germans before the Red Army crosses the river, and that the operation is the thing that has been called the Tempest since the year of the 1940, and that it is going to be done, and that the reason is that if it is not done, then the government in the London is going to be sitting in a room in the 1946 with the government of the Soviet Union, and there will be no the card in their hand, because the city of Warsaw will have been taken by the Red Army without one Pole having been in the operation."

He stopped and looked at her.

"You do not have to say anything about that."

"I know what Tempest is," she said. "I have known since the January. The the wives of the German administration have been talking about the German line on the Vistula since the March, and the German line on the Vistula is a page in a safe in a study in the house on the Bagatela, and the page has eleven towns on it, and eleven dates, and the last date in the right-hand column is the eighth of August of the year of the 1944, and under the table there is a sentence in a pencil, and the sentence says that the line is to be held without an interruption until the date, and that there is to be no withdrawal without an order of the Führer."

The room above the furrier's went very quiet.

"Say it again," said Wilczek.

"The eighth of August," she said. "Nineteen hundred and forty-four. *Die Linie ist bis zum Datum ununterbrochen zu halten.* The line is to be held unbroken until that date. And that is not a plan for a retreat; that is a plan for a stand, and it is a stand that was decided in a room in Berlin by men who know what is coming down the road behind us, and there are only two things that a stand in a city can be: a fortress, or a grave."

Wilczek put both hands flat on the table.

"That," he said, "is the fourth thing, and you have just said it instead of me, and I am going to tell you the rest of it anyhow, because it is your orders and I am going to have the pleasure of the once." He leaned forward. "The the page and anything else that is in that steel box is to be in the hands of the staff of the district of the underground of this city by the twenty-fifth of July. Not later. And it is to be taken by you, because there is no one else, and the manner of the taking of it is your own business and I do not want to hear a word of it, and if you are taken while you are doing it, then the arrangement is that us do not know you, and your name is not on any paper in the city of Warsaw, and the manner of the last resort is the business of you and God."

"There is a fifth thing," she said.

"There is not."

"There is always a fifth thing. It is in your face."

Wilczek was quiet for a moment. Then he took the pliers up off the table and put them down again, and said, in a completely different voice, which was the voice of a man of fifty-two who had, in the September of the 1939, taken a squadron of the Polish cavalry into the fire of the German tanks at the Bzura and had lost three-quarters of it:

"There is a captain of the Abwehr in the district staff who was born in the Warsaw in the 1915, and whose the mother was a Pole of the name of the Zaleska, and whose the file has the seven weeks of the 1939 written over twice, and whose the file we have, and who has, in the month of the August of the 1943, signed a paper that kept a boy out of a German list, and I want to know the reason, and I want to know the reason before the twenty-fifth of the July, and there is only one person in the city who can tell me it."

She stood up.

"No," she said.

"Panna Krystyna."

"The the name of the captain is not going to come out of my mouth, and the reason is not the reason you think, and I am going to tell you the truth about it, because you are the second in command of the intelligence of the army of the underground of the city of Warsaw and there is not a time in the war when a truth is going to be worth more to you than a lie." She put her gloves on, which was a gesture of the pre-war. "He has known what I am since the thirtieth of the January of the 1943, and he has not said a syllable to anybody in the seventeen months, and he has risked his whole life and his whole career and his whole file for a woman he knew in a garden in the 1938, and the reason that he has done it is not a politics and it is not a cause; it is the fact that when he was a boy of nineteen years there was a girl of twenty-two at a piano in a house on the Mokotowska, and he has spent a war in a German uniform in the country of his mother, and the only thing he has ever had is the memory of that room."

The room above the furrier's was silent.

Wilczek looked at her for a long, long moment, with the grey eyes of a cavalry officer of the old Poland, and then he said the thing that she carried, in the nineteen weeks that followed, in the small room at the back of her head, next to the tailor's wife of the rue des Rosiers:

"Then the decision is yours, and it is the worst decision of the war for a woman of the intelligence, because the thing that you have just told me is the reason that the day is going to come, in the next three months, when that man is going to find the truth about you, and on that day he is going to do one of the two things: he is going to save you, or he is going to kill you. And you cannot know which of the two, and I cannot know it, and the only thing about it that I know for certain, is that on that day, Panna Krystyna, the war is going to come down to you and one man and one room, and there is not going to be a single thing in the world that an organisation is going to be able to do for you."

---

The Colonel came home that evening from the Blue Palace with a leather folder, and he came up the marble stairs two at a time, which was a thing he had not done since Paris, and he came into her room without knocking, which was a thing he had not done since the beginning.

"Pack," he said. "Two trunks, the winter, and the good silver, and the papers. To the west."

She was at the window with the evening on the chestnuts.

"To the west."

"To the west, on a train, on the twenty-eighth of the June, which is the train of the families of the officers of the staff of the district, which is going to go to the Breslau, and from the Breslau to a house in the Pomerania that has been arranged for the month of the March." He dropped the folder on the bed. "The the order came through on the Tuesday. The the civil administration of the district of the Warsaw is to be moved west of the frontier in the course of the July, and the military staff of the district is to remain, and the city is to be prepared for a defence, and it is the operation Festung Warschau."

"And the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw?"

"The General of the intelligence of the district is going to hold his district," said Friedrich von Reuth, "and is going to send his household to the west on the twenty-eighth of the June, and is going to come to the west in the September, when the thing in the east has been decided, and there is a house in the Pomerania with a garden and a woman, and the woman is going to be standing in the hall of it with a lamp, and I have arranged my whole life to be able to look at that door."

She looked at him.

"No," she said.

"Marion."

"No," she said again, and she said it with her mother's face, and she said it in the tone in which she had said a sentence to him in the corridor of the Hôtel Majestic in the January of the 1943, and it was the only sentence of their whole two years that came out of the body of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna and not out of the mask of Marion Herschlag. "You are going to hold a district until a date on a paper, and you have been sitting in this city for a year and a half with a schedule in a box in the wall of your study, and it says the eighth of the August, and I know the number because you gave it to me in a bed in the November, and the only thing in the world that you have allowed yourself to be is a man who holds a district until a date." She stood up from the window. "So there is no train, Friedrich, and there is no house in the Pomerania, and there is no woman in the hall with a lamp. There is a city and a wall and a river and a date in the August, and there is the thing that the two of us have been for the last two years, and I am not going to get on a train and be a rumour that somebody has heard about in a house in the west."

He looked at her for a long, long moment.

"You will stay," he said.

"I will stay."

"And why?"

And in the room of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, in a Polish May, with the eleven trains a night going west under the window and the future standing in the room with its hat in its hand, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, of the house of Stankiewicz of the Mokotowska, file name WESPER, stood in the light of the evening and gave the answer that was the truth, and the truth was the only thing in the world that he could not check.

"Because you are the only man in the world who has ever needed me," she said, "and because the thing in the east is going to happen in this city, and because when it happens, I do not intend to be in the west, in a house, in a chair, with a photograph and a newspaper."

He crossed the room and put his hands on either side of her face, both hands, palms flat against her cheeks, the way he had never done, and looked into her for a long time from a distance of eleven centimetres.

"Then let it be this city," he said, "and let it be this summer."

Outside, in the dark of the north over the wall and the ruins, four whole stations away, the eleventh train of the night was going west under the load of the wreck of an army; and in the cellar of a building in the Praga district a wireless set was receiving the order that had come out of London in the Morse of the night; and in the house on the Bagatela, in the corridor of the first floor, in the dark, a man with four stripes of the front on the sleeve of his tunic stood at the top of the marble stairs and listened to the two of them through the door, and did not go in, and did not go away.

---

The families of the officers of the district of Warsaw left the city on the twenty-eighth of June 1944, at half past five in the morning, in eleven carriages at the station on the Ochota side of the town; and the order for the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw had been in a leather folder on the hall table since the twenty-fourth.

He put it there himself, on the Saturday, without a word, the way a man puts a bill on a sideboard: the transfer warrant of the household, the ticket of the railway administration for the line of the Upper Silesia, the pass of the military police for the crossing of the frontier of the Reich at the station of Katowice, and a sheet of the paper of the district with the list of the objects which a family of an officer was permitted to take out of the Generalgouvernement in the summer of 1944: two trunks, one case of the silver, one crate of the pictures, three hundred kilograms of the household goods.

And for three days the woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw packed the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw.

She did it on her knees on the floor of the small room with the paper of the district in her hand and the ink of it on her fingers, because there was nobody else in the house to do it: the cook from Bydgoszcz came in at seven and went out at eight; Anna Zaleska was in the kitchen with her hearing and her silence and a bag of the household, and the furier of the staircase was at the gate with his wife and his sister and the sum of his four years of service in a case of the cardboard; and so the two trunks of the household of the General of the German intelligence service in Warsaw were packed by the only woman in the house who was not going to be on the train.

The winter of the Colonel: the two grey tunics, the collar linings, the boots with the trees in them, the greatcoat with the fur of the year 1941, the four shirts of the evening, the photograph in the frame of the leather — she wrapped that in the grey paper of the district and packed it between the boots and the collar linings, so that if the trunk stood upright for the six hundred kilometres to the west, the photograph of Anneliese von Reuth in the garden in 1931 would be face up in the top of the trunk when a woman of the Pomerania opened it.

The papers of the family: the deeds, in the packet of the wax and the red seal; the two account books; the notification of the pension of the widow of the year 1918, which was his mother's; and the little book of the poems that had been in his case since 1915 and that she had never once opened, and that he had never once mentioned, and that she packed in the bottom of the second trunk with her own hands, in the paper, wrapped twice, because it was the only object in the house in which she had ever found the man in one piece.

And on the Tuesday at noon the two trunks stood in the hall of the marble stair, and the crate of the pictures stood beside them, and the case of the silver was in the corridor by the kitchen; and the furier came up from the gate with the porter of the building of the next street, and the four of them carried it down the marble stair and out through the door and along the garden and into the street, where the cart of the office of the administration of the district stood waiting, and the operation took the middle of the day.

She stood at the top of the marble stair and watched the last crate go out through the door of the house, and there was, at the bottom of the stair, in the hall where the driver of the General of the district would stand in the evening with the cap in his hand, a man with four stripes of the front on the sleeve of his tunic, and he had been standing in the middle of the hall for the morning and had not said a word, and now he said one sentence, in the German of a man of thirty-four from the town of Posen, looking at the door of the house and not at the woman on the stair:

"In the month of the September of the 1941, gnädige Frau, in the town of the Posen, the administration of the Reich evacuated the families of the officers of the garrison of the town of the Posen in the four hours, and there were eleven thousand people in the roads of the west on the first night, and there was an old woman of the age of eighty years in the middle of the road of the column of them, and the people of the column of it walked around her for the night, and in the morning the people of the road of the west were the people of a road, and the families of the officers of the garrison of the town of the Posen did not come back into the town of the Posen in the war." He put his cap on. "The the families of the officers of the district of Warsaw are going to the west in the morning of the twenty-eighth, and there is no place in the west, gnädige Frau; there is only the direction of the west."

"Ehlers."

"Yes, gnädige Frau."

"Are you going with them?"

"The driver of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw remains with the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw," said Ehlers, "and there is not one soldier of the district who is permitted to ask a question about the household of the General, and there is not one soldier of the district who is permitted to answer one, and I have been in this house for the year, and I have driven this car in the four streets of the German quarter of this city, and there is one thing in the year that I have seen and that I have never once spoken of, and I am not going to speak of it, and the Frau should know that, because in the war there is exactly one kind of a friend that a man of the Wehrmacht is able to be to a woman of the household of his whole of a General, and it is a friend who does not ask."

He went out through the door of the house with his cap in his hand, and the woman on the stair stood in the marble hall of the house on the Bagatela, in the silence of the middle of the day of the twenty-seventh of the June of 1944, and understood, in the eleven seconds of it, the arithmetic that she had been avoiding for a month.

---

They went in the morning, in the dark, at half past five, from the station on the Ochota side of the town.

She went with them, because the wife of the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw belongs to the administration of the district in the way that a chair belongs to a room, and because the wives of the staff of the district were at the platform of the station in the dark of the twenty-eighth of June 1944 in their good coats, with their cases and their children and the labels of the administration of the district on the suitcases.

Eleven carriages. The doors sealed by the men of the Feldgendarmerie with a wire and a lead seal on the handles; the windows painted white to the height of the shoulder of a man, in the way of the trains of the administration of the east in the year 1944, so that nothing of the country would be seen from the inside of the carriages of the families of the officers of the German administration of the city of Warsaw.

There were nine hundred and forty people on the platform and about two hundred of them were crying, and one hundred of them were laughing, and them were in the highest state of the efficiency of a German household in the fifth year of a war: the prams folded and labelled, the cases counted twice, the children in the coats of the morning with the bread and the chocolate in the pockets, the grandmothers of the officers' families — of whom there were eleven on the platform — carried up the steps of the carriages by the men of the railway administration of the district, one on each arm.

The General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw stood with his hands behind his back at the third carriage and looked at the train.

He had not spoken to her in the morning. He had come down the marble stair of the house on the Bagatela at half past four in the dark with his greatcoat on his arm, and had stood at the bottom of the stair, and had said, in the ordinary voice of a general of the army of Germany:

"Marion."

"Yes."

"One word."

"No," she said.

And he had nodded, once, at the foot of the marble stair of the house on the Bagatela at half past four in the morning of the twenty-eighth of June 1944, and had put on his greatcoat, and had gone out to the car; and the conversation of the two of them about the train of the families of the officers of the district of the city of Warsaw had taken the nine seconds of it.

It was the wife of the medical officer of the district who asked her, at the platform, at twenty minutes past five, with the noise and the steam of the station around them.

"Frau Herschlag. You are not in the train."

"I am not."

"The transfer of the household of the General of the intelligence service is for the Frau of the household of the General of the intelligence service," said the wife of the medical officer of the district of Warsaw, who was a woman of forty with a good face and a sister in Stuttgart and a husband who was at that hour in a room of the administration on the avenue of the Ujazdowskie. "There is a place in the third carriage for the Frau of the household of the General, and the paper of the place is with the administration of the train, and the Frau of the household of the General is standing on the platform of the station of Warsaw in the dark at twenty minutes past five in the morning of the twenty-eighth of June 1944 in a coat of the spring of the year 1944, and the Frauen of the administration of the district of Warsaw have been asking the question of it for the month of the June."

"Then the Frauen of the administration of the district of Warsaw are going to have the answer to the question for the rest of it," said Marion Herschlag, in the French of the woman of Strasbourg, with the courtesy of the avenue Henri-Martin on the platform of the station on the Ochota side of the town, "and the answer is that the woman of the household of the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw does not go to the west of the Reich in the month of the June of the year 1944, and the reason for it is that her husband is a general of the intelligence service of a district of the east, and that there is a of an east, and that between the two of them the woman is a matter of the arithmetic of a household, and the Frau of the medical officer of the district is the wife of a doctor of the administration of the district of Warsaw, and is going to the west in a of a carriage with the windows painted white, and the two of us are in the same station in the same morning and are not in the same war."

The wife of the medical officer looked at her for a moment, and did the small, terrible thing that a woman of forty does at the end of an exchange of that kind, which is to be kind: she put out her hand and pressed the arm of the woman of the household of the General for one second, and said, "God keep you, madame. We shall be in the west until this is over, and the west is a hole in the ground and a stove and nothing else, and it is not a life; and the women on this platform are envious of you, and I am the only one who is going to say it out loud."

The whistle went at twenty-five minutes past five.

The train of the families of the officers of the district of Warsaw went out of the station on the Ochota side of the town in the grey of the morning of the twenty-eighth of June 1944 with the hands at the windows painted white and the white handkerchiefs beside them, and there were nine hundred people on the platform of the station, and the nine hundred were the men of the German administration of the city of Warsaw and the women and the Poles who had carried the luggage, and the nine hundred stood in the dark and looked at the train and did not applaud, because there is nothing in the German administration of the east in the summer of the year 1944 that any man on a platform was able to applaud.

---

The frost came on the twenty-ninth of June.

It was not a frost of the winter; it was the thing that the Poles of the Masovia called the cold of the three nights, and it came, that year, in the last week of June, and it came down over the city of Warsaw in the night between the twenty-eighth and the twenty-ninth with a wind from the north-east; and in the morning the garden of the house on the Bagatela was black.

The tomatoes of the gardener of the district were black. The beans of the household, in the two beds against the wall of the kitchen, were black, and hung with the water of the night, and looked, in the light of the morning of the twenty-ninth, like a thing that had been burned; and the walnut tree of the garden was green, because the walnut of the Masovian plain has been in that country since the Sarmatians and knows the cold of the three nights; and the Italian garden of the house of the wives of the administration on the Ujazdowskie, with the roses and the cypress of the year 1938, was killed in a of a single night, and the wives of the administration of the district of Warsaw were on a train in the province of Silesia, and the roses of the German quarter of the city of Warsaw were dead in the morning of the last week of June 1944.

She stood at the drawing-room window of the house on the Bagatela at six o'clock in the morning of the twenty-ninth of June 1944, in a dressing gown, with the coffee of the household in a cup in her hand, and looked at the garden: the black beans, the black tomatoes, the green walnut tree with its leaves, and the furier of the staircase coming along the gravel of the path with a rake over his shoulder, and stopping in the middle of the path to look down at the ruin of the two beds of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and then going on with the rake over the shoulder, in the ordinary way of a man on his way to the day's work.

And the woman of the household of the General said, out loud, to the garden, in the Polish of the city of her birth, in the empty room of the drawing room of a German house in the city of Warsaw, the sentence that she had not said in the four years of the war, because there was nobody in the house to hear it, and because there had never in her life been a fact like the frost of the last week of June 1944:

"Two months."
