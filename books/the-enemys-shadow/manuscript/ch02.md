# Chapter 2: Dead Languages

It had begun, like most things in her life, with a lie that was almost true, told in a room with a good carpet.

October, 1942. A building on the avenue Kléber with eagles over the doors and a lift that worked because the Wehrmacht had kept the current on all over Paris for the buildings it needed. She had waited forty minutes in a corridor painted the same green as a hospital, and she had counted the doors, and the cornices, and the number of times the lift came up, because she had been told in Kraków to arrive with her eyes and her ears and nothing else — no papers she could not burn, no powder on her face, no gloves because gloves were what women who came to be hired wore, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna had come to the Hôtel Majestic to be looked at only once.

That was the arrangement. One interview, one hour, one irreversible yes or no. It had been explained to her by Lis on a bench in the Saxon Garden three weeks earlier, in the ordinary voice of a man discussing the price of coal.

*You are not going there to be liked, my little rabbit. You are going there to be misunderstood. That is it. They must look at you and see a woman and stop there, because everything after that is arithmetic.*

The door had opened. A Hauptmann with a face like a boy's had said her name — hers, the false one, Herschlag — and she had stood up, and walked in, and stopped in the middle of the room, and the man behind the desk had done her the courtesy of not standing.

He had been fifty-three that autumn. Grey at the temples but not silver; a face built to be looked at from below, and a body that had been a good one at thirty and had been maintained since by riding and by a discipline that no longer knew what it was for. He had not looked at her body. He had looked at her hands, and then at her shoes, and then at her face, for a long time, without any embarrassment at all.

"Herschlag," he said. "From Strasbourg."

"Yes."

"You were married to a man who made plate glass."

"Yes."

"He is dead."

"He was killed in the retreat. Near Abbeville. Before the armistice."

"So it says." He had turned the paper over with two fingers, the way a man turns over a coin of a denomination he doesn't trust. "You speak German."

"A little. Alsatian German. My mother's people were from Kehl."

"And French."

"And French."

"And you came to Paris in July and have been living in the rue de Bassano with a dressmaker." He had looked up. "Which dressmaker?"

"Madame Vaury. The couture house is closed. She keeps the flat because her brother writes her a certificate every month."

"Why does she keep you?"

It was the question she had been waiting for, and she had rehearsed the answer not in a room in Kraków but in a room in Warsaw, at a window, in the winter of 1940, in front of a mirror, with her mother's face on and her own hands shaking in the dark below the sill.

"Because I amuse her," she said. "Because I am beautiful, and I have no money, and every week I become a little more expensive to keep. There is nothing strange in it, Colonel. Women keep each other the way men keep dogs; it is only the collar that changes."

There had been a pause in that room like a stone dropping down a well, and she had heard the lift again, and a telephone ringing two doors away, and then Friedrich von Reuth had done the thing that decided the next two years of her life. He had laughed. It was a short laugh, a bark, a door closing; and he had stood up — the limp was there already, the left leg, from a wound she would not learn the name of for another month — and he had come around the desk and stopped in front of her, close enough that she could smell his soap and his cigarettes and the wool of a uniform that had been tailored in Berlin.

"You will be told lies about me," he said. "By my officers, who think I am a machine. By the French, who think I am a monster. By women, who think I am a widower with an open wound." His hand had come up and taken her chin between his thumb and forefinger, not hard — a man inspecting a horse, or engraving a coin. "I am none of these. I am a man who despises waste. You are not a wasted woman. That is all."

And that had been Paris. She had moved into the apartment on the avenue Henri-Martin nine days later, and she had lain down in the bed of a woman who had died in 1936, and she had begun.

---

The film went out on the twelfth of January, in a hat.

Odile came for it at nine in the morning with a workman's box and a bundle of tissue, and the two of them stood in the hall of the apartment with Kleist's coffee still on the tray and did the thing that every Frenchwoman in the rue Cambon could have watched without seeing anything at all: Marion Herschlag tried on a hat, and the hat did not suit her, and the hat was taken away to be altered.

"How many?" Odile said, holding the mirror.

"Nine names."

"Put your shoulders down, madame, you are doing something military with them." She turned the mirror, all attentiveness, and said, at a volume that would not carry past the door: "The boy from the quai was picked up on Wednesday. The police, or the SD, I do not know. He was at the market, buying bread, being seen buying it, exactly as he was told."

Marion looked at a hat in a mirror.

"Then he did what he was told," she said.

"He was twenty-three."

"I know how old he was."

She had gone over it nine hundred times in the week since the quai, and every version of the night ended the same way. She had done the correct thing nine times out of nine and it had cost a boy his teeth. That was the arithmetic, and there was no escaping out of arithmetic: the only thing she was permitted to feel was the shape of the mistake. The mistake, she had decided, was speaking Polish to him. He had gone away from her with something in his chest, a piece of knowledge, warm as a hand; and a man with something warm in his chest goes to a market and looks at people the way a man looks at people when he has a secret. He had bought bread and been seen buying it, and he had also, she would have bet her life on it, looked back over his shoulder at a woman in a grey coat.

"Tell me about the film," Odile said. "Nothing about the film. Never about the film. The film is a hat."

"The film is a hat. Tuesday, the shop. Red hatbox, black ribbon, second shelf, behind the two blue ones. It goes to Marseille with the millinery. The man at the Gare de Lyon wears gloves and asks for Madame Vaury's order, and if he asks for anything else, you are a hat shop that has been robbed twice and you do not keep a ledger."

"And afterwards?"

"Afterwards there is no afterwards. There is a train, and then a road, and then Warsaw, and the men there will do what men there always do, which is argue about whether the names are worth the paper." She took the hat off and held it out. "Nine names, Odile. One of them is a tailor who lives in a basement with his sister and three other families and has probably never in his life been west of the Vistula."

Odile put the hat in tissue.

"Then why does a colonel of the Abwehr keep him in a briefcase?"

"That," said Marion, "is the best question anyone has asked me all winter."

She saw Odile to the door, and the concierge did not hear them, and the two women kissed the air on either side of each other's faces, and a hat went out of a house on the avenue Henri-Martin inside a bundle of tissue paper, and the thing inside it was a hundred and ten centimetres of unexposed film, and the man who would carry it as far as Lyon had a wife and a hernia and a bad opinion of Poles, and would, in the end, in the wet spring of that year, be caught at the frontier at Modane because he stopped to help a woman with a pram.

There was no way to know any of that, that morning, in that hall. There was only the door, and the cold, and the day's work ahead of her, which was to be a woman of no importance at a table in the sixteenth arrondissement.

---

At noon the Colonel sent for her, which he did perhaps twice a month, and each time it meant something.

Ehlers drove her. He was thirty-three, with a face like a well-kept farm and hands that never went above fifty kilometres an hour, and he had been in Poland, and in France, and had a photograph of a girl named Marta in the flap of his map case, and in the four months since October he had never once said a word to Marion Herschlag that was not correct, and never once failed to open her door first.

"It is cold, Fräulein," he said, in the car.

"It is January."

"At home they say the same." He changed gear with the same gentleness he did everything with. "My Marta writes that in Posen they are burning the trees. The chestnuts on the avenue. Not for fuel — there is coal — but because the branches hang over the road, and the road is for the army." He said it in the voice of a man reporting the weather, and then, quietly, because a car is a confessional and a German driver in Paris has one place left to put his opinions: "There was a park where we walked in the summer."

"That is what they do," said Marion. "They take the branches, and the shade, and then they take the bench, and then they take the woman on the bench. It is all one thing, Ehlers. You cannot have the branches back."

The car went along the avenue with the blackout lamps off for once, because it was daylight, and she watched the city go by, and she thought about how easy it was, how fatally easy, to be kind to the people who were going to lose.

The Majestic had a courtyard with staff cars and a sentry, and inside, on the second floor, in a room with a map of the Atlantic coast and a photograph of a dead woman on the sill, Oberst Friedrich von Reuth was standing at the window with his hands behind his back, and there was a single sheet of paper on the desk.

"Come in," he said. "Shut the door."

She shut the door.

"Take off your coat."

She took off her coat. He did not turn around. That was the shape of it, the shape their private life took when it came into an office: he would look at her in a window, she would stand with her hands at her sides, and the sex that had happened the night before would come into the room behind them and stand there, patient, like an orderly.

"There is a leak," he said.

"I have heard you say that before."

"Not like this." He turned. "A man named Röhrig was killed on Tuesday. This morning the Militärbefehlshaber tells me that a document from his briefcase has been found, in a private house in Neuilly, in a bundle of papers belonging to a banker who has been in prison since 1941, which means that the document went out of the Militärbefehlshaber, into the street, into the hand of a courier, and back into the hand of the police, in five days." He came over to the desk and put two fingers on the sheet of paper. "It is a list of names in the southern zone."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below, in the courtyard, a car door slammed, and the sound came up through the window and lay in the room like a third person.

"I do not know any names in the southern zone," she said.

"No."

"I know a jeweller on the rue de la Paix, a hairdresser, a hat shop, and your orderly. If I were a spy, Colonel, I would be a very poor one."

"I know." He was looking at her with the expression he wore at the theatre, when he watched the room watching his woman. "I tell you because I have decided something, and I want you to hear it from me and not from the servants."

She waited.

"There is a leak in the Abwehrstelle Paris, and it is not a Frenchman, because the Frenchmen here are clerks and clerks do not steal photographs. It is somebody who has access. Possibly somebody in my own section. When I find him, I shall hang him in the rue des Saussaies and make the rest of them watch, and after that I shall be sent to the east, because a colonel who finds a traitor is a colonel who has failed."

"Then do not find him," she said.

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched the thought land, and she watched him decide to be amused by it, because if he was amused he did not have to wonder how a dressmaker's companion from Strasbourg had learned to say such a thing.

"There is one more thing." He went to the window again. "My adjutant has been promoted away from me — Schmidt, a fool, but loyal. I have asked for a replacement, and they are sending me a captain out of the staff at Nancy. He is half Polish, of all things; his mother was born in that city. He speaks the language." A pause. "He will be useful. I am told his file is excellent."

She stood in the middle of the room with her coat over her arm, and she did not move, and the room was suddenly full of a noise like the sea.

"His name," said the Colonel, "is Brandt."

There was a chair by the wall. She did not sit in it. She looked at the photograph of the dead woman on the sill — Anneliese, in a garden, in 1931, with a dog — and she thought, with the part of her mind that had been trained on a bench in the Saxon Garden to be quicker than her hands: *there are twelve hundred men in the Paris garrison, and there were eleven hundred thousand people in Warsaw, and there are no coincidences, there are only things that have not happened yet.*

"Half Polish," she said.

"Half Polish." He was still at the window. "You are not a Pole, my dear, so do not look at me like that. It is not a crime in my service to have a Polish mother. It is merely a thing that has to be written down."

"Perhaps he will teach me the language."

"He will not have time." He turned. "He arrives on Friday. On Friday I have the dinner for the Reichsbank people, and I want you at it, in the black, and I want the earrings, and I want you to be the only woman in the room who is not frightened of me."

"I am never frightened of you," she said, and she said it with her mother's face, and it was the first genuinely perfect lie she had told him in three months.

---

She did not sleep that night, and she did not count.

At two in the morning he came for her, which was his habit when a decision was working in him: he would wake her with his hand and take her without a word, as though the marriage bed were a drawer he was permitted to open, and she would do what she did, and afterwards he would talk — that was the whole mechanism of the man; his body was the door and his voice was the room behind it.

He turned her onto her back and put his weight on her, and she made herself soft under him in the way that made it shorter, and he said, against her neck, with the effort in his voice, "The boy from the rue de la Pompe—"

"Not now."

"They gave him to the French police and the French police gave him back." His hand closed in her hair. "He was a student. Two years of law at Caen. He had a mother in Rouen." He was moving now, steadily, and she lay under him and did the work, which was to be a surface, and to hear every word. "Before they shot him he told them a name. They always tell you a name. It is not courage that runs out, my dear, it is the *arrangement*—"

She lifted her hips into him because that was what made him finish, and because it made him stop talking about the arrangement, and he came with his teeth against her shoulder, and then rolled off and lay on his back in the dark, breathing, and she lay still and hated him in the correct way, which was without any movement of her face.

"Do you know what a name is worth?" he said, to the ceiling.

"Tell me."

"A name is worth exactly what it costs to check. That is why we lose. Every name we are given costs four men four days and a car and a telephone call to Berlin, and in that time the man is gone, because his organisation has moved him; and so the arrangement is always behind, and the only way to get in front of it is to pay for a name with another name, and then you are the kind of man who sells names, and such a man does not last." He put his arm over his eyes, the thing he did. "In Russia I had a man who sold me names. Ukrainian. Excellent instinct. I gave him a house in Kiev and a car and the use of a woman who had been the wife of a commissar; and in April I had to shoot him myself, in a wood, and he was surprised. He kept saying, *Herr Oberst, I did what you asked.* And he had. That is the thing about this war. Everybody is doing what somebody asked."

She lay beside him in the dark, and under her breastbone the small cold machine that she called her mind was running, and it was running on the word *Brandt*, and it had been running for four hours, and it was producing, every time, the same three results.

The first was Warsaw, 1938, and a boy of nineteen in the Botanic Gardens in June, with a book of poems and a shirt that was too thin for the evening, who had said, when she had asked him what he intended to do with his life, *I am going to be something my mother can be proud of in two languages*, and had laughed at himself the moment he said it, and had blushed, and had looked away over the glass roofs of the palms.

The second was the letter, and the letter was not a memory, it was a physical object: a square envelope with her name on it in a hand that pressed too hard, which she had found in the drawer of the hall table on the seventh of September 1939, when the family was packing silver into pillowcases, and had put in her pocket and carried through that autumn and never opened, and which was, as far as she knew, at the bottom of a suitcase that no longer existed, in a house on the Mokotowska that had been used for three weeks as a barracks by a regiment of the Reich.

The third was the one that was actually dangerous, and she lay in the dark and made herself look at it, because that was the rule she lived by.

*He is coming here,* she thought. *He will be in this house, in this corridor, at my table, and he will be the one man in Europe who can look at me and see a girl.*

And then, because the mind was a machine and machines sometimes did the thing you had not asked for, the thought went on without her, in the dark, in the shape of a question, and the question was this: *what if he is not sorry?*

---

The Friday dinner was in the long room at the Majestic that had been a ballroom in 1907 and was now a dining room with a portrait of the Führer over the fireplace and forty covers on linen.

She wore the black. She wore the earrings — she had put them on, in the end, in front of the mirror in the second bedroom, and she had looked at herself for four seconds and understood something about herself that she had not wanted to know, which was that the diamonds suited her, and that she liked them, and that a woman who has begun to like the price of her own work is a woman with an appointment in the rue des Saussaies.

The Reichsbank men were dull and frightened; there is nothing so dull as a banker in an occupied country, and nothing so frightened. She sat between a Generalmajor with a wine-coloured face and a secretary of the Militärbefehlshaber who tried three times to look down the front of her dress without moving his head, and she performed, for four hours, the entire repertoire: the laugh at the right moment, the pause before the answer that made men think she had considered them, the single glass of champagne drunk very slowly for the pleasure of watching her hands around it.

At the far end of the table, the Colonel talked about the Atlantic, and she watched him do it, and understood, as she always did on these evenings, how it was that the thing between them worked. He did not love her; he possessed her; and possession, in a people who had given themselves over to a war they were losing, was a religion with a full pew on Sunday morning.

At eleven the cars came. And in the corridor, going out, with her hand on the Colonel's arm, she turned her head because someone had said the word "Brandt" two metres behind her, and saw, in the middle of a group of officers, with a glass in one hand and his head bent to listen to a colonel of the Luftwaffe, a man of twenty-eight in a captain's uniform who was not, in any respect, the boy from the Botanic Gardens.

She had three seconds. She used them the way she had been taught in Kraków: she let her eyes pass over him the way the eyes of a woman pass over the furniture of a room she has been in before, and she took from the three seconds exactly the information that was there. He was heavier through the shoulders. His hair was shorter and there was a scar at the corner of his mouth that had not been there in 1938. He stood the way soldiers stood when they had been in the east, with his weight on the balls of his feet. And he had, in the way he held the glass, in the set of the wrist, something that had been trained out of everybody else in the room and had not been trained out of him, which was that he held it as though he were in a house that did not belong to him.

She was at the door. The Colonel's hand came onto the back of her neck.

"Ah," he said. "There he is. Brandt."

She looked at the far end of the corridor. And in that same half-second, at the distance of perhaps nine metres, the captain's head came up.

He looked at her.

It was not a long look. A second, a second and a half. It was the length of look a junior officer gives the woman on the arm of his colonel: blank, courteous, and placed carefully at the point in the middle distance where a well-bred man's eyes rest. He was too far away for her to see the colour of his eyes. She saw, instead, the small thing that would keep her awake for the next eleven nights: the half-beat of hesitation before he turned away — the interval, no longer than a breath, in which a man decides what he is going to be for the remainder of a war.

Then he bowed slightly, to the Colonel, not to her, and turned back to his conversation.

"Good," said von Reuth, at her ear, in the voice of a man appraising a horse. "He looks exactly like what he is, which is a very lucky young man. He was a lieutenant at Vyazma and had his whole company killed around him; they gave him a fortnight in Paris before they send him back to the east." His hand tightened on her neck. "You are cold. Get in the car."

She got in the car. Berlin was six hundred kilometres away and Warsaw was twelve hundred, and she sat in the dark of the back seat with Ehlers driving and the blackout lamps smearing the grey fronts of the avenue, and she did the only thing she was permitted to do with a fact, which was to file it: *he does not know me, or he knows me and will not say it, and both of those are worse than anything I have arranged for.*

The car stopped. She got out. In the hall the Colonel took off his cap and hung it on the chair, and turned to her with the earrings catching the light from the landing above, and for the first time in four months he did not take her to bed.

Instead he said: "You were quiet tonight."

"I am always quiet at those dinners."

"No." He came a step closer. "From the moment we left the table you were quiet in a different way. In the corridor. When I said the name."

The clock in the hall struck the half hour.

"I hate the corridor," said Marion Herschlag. "There is a draught in it, and a photograph of your wife on the wall by the door to your office, and every one of your officers looks at it and then at me, and I am a woman who is paid to be looked at, Colonel, but not to be compared."

It was cruel, it was cheap, and it was exactly the sort of thing a kept woman says when she is frightened: personal, spiteful, and about a dead woman. It worked. She watched the words go into him and do their work on his face, and she watched him decide, as he had decided a hundred times in five months, that the sound of her jealousy was the sound of the only thing in his life that was still intact — his property.

He took her to bed, and he was rougher than he had been since November, and afterwards, when he was asleep, she lay awake with her wrists aching where his fingers had been, and listened to the sound of the great cold city outside, and understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in cleanly, that she was now in the only position she had ever been warned about: she was going to have to do this with a man in the room who could see her.

She had told herself, in the winter of 1940, in a room in Kraków, with a small man and a bad heart explaining to her the four rules that would keep her alive, that the fourth one was easy.

*Never love. It is not a moral instruction, my little rabbit, it is arithmetic. You will be offered two or three chances, and each one will feel like the only honest thing in the world, and each one will kill you and everyone who gave you your orders. So: never love. Say it back to me.*

She had said it back. She had said it back, in the winter of 1940, in a room with a bad fire and a bad chair, and she had meant it, and she had said it back again on the platform at the Gare de l'Est in October, under her breath, because the cold had got into her chest and she had thought it was the cold.

Beside her, the Colonel of the Abwehrstelle Paris slept the sleep of a man with a clear conscience, which was the last clear conscience left in Europe.

Down the corridor, past the green marble bathroom, in the small room with the north light, there was a hatbox with a red ribbon on it and no hat in it, and in the study behind the photograph of a dead woman there was a sheet of paper with a heading in three languages, and in the west of the city, in a flat above a closed couture house, a milliner of thirty was asleep in a bed she shared with nobody, with her brother's wireless set under the floorboard and a train ticket in the lining of a winter coat.

And somewhere, in a hotel on the boulevard des Capucines, a captain who had been at Vyazma was sitting on the edge of a bed with his boots off and a cigarette burning down in an ashtray, not sleeping, doing arithmetic of his own.
