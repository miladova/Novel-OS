# Chapter 14: What the River Keeps

The funeral was on the eleventh of November, in the rain, in the Powązki cemetery, at two in the afternoon, and she watched it from the seat of a tram.

That was the arrangement, and she had made it herself the way she made everything: sitting at the desk of a colonel of the Abwehr at eleven o'clock at night with a pencil and a list of the district's movements, and working out, with her cold and excellent mind, what the distances were, and what the timing was, and which of the trams stopped. And so on the eleventh of November at a quarter to two, Marion Herschlag got onto the number 17 tram at the corner of the Marszałkowska in a good coat and a scarf, and paid her fare, and stood in the middle of the carriage with her hand on the leather strap, and rode through the north of the city to the terminus at the cemetery gate, and at four minutes past two the tram went past the gate of the Powązki cemetery at eleven kilometres an hour, and stopped at the halt, and the doors opened, and a man in a cap got on with a sack, and the doors shut, and the tram went on.

In the four seconds of the stop, through the window, at a distance of about ninety metres, she saw: the wall, the gate, the black crowd of about two hundred people at the graveside, eleven umbrellas, the priest's white, and a coffin on the boards in the grey rain; and among the crowd, in the front, upright, in a black coat with a man's collar, a woman of thirty with one leg shorter than the other from a childhood illness; and behind the crowd, standing apart on the path in a cap and a wool coat with his hands at his sides, a boy of twenty-two who worked in a chemist's shop on the Ząbkowska and who had been made indispensable in the month of August by a piece of paper in a German office.

She looked at it for four seconds, and then the tram went on, and the picture was out of the window, and she stood in the middle of the carriage with her hand on the strap and looked at a woman's back and a child's parcel and the rain on the glass.

And what she thought about, in the seven kilometres back into the city, was not her father.

It was the fact that a boy of twenty-two had been standing at the back of a crowd at his own father's funeral and had not been standing beside a woman in a black coat; that a woman of thirty with one short leg had been standing in the front of a crowd at the graveside of a man she had fed for four years with half an orange a day; and that she herself, the daughter of the house of Stankiewicz of the Mokotowska, had watched it from a tram with the stopwatch in her head running, and had done it correctly, and had paid her fare.

She got off at the Nowy Świat and went home and asked Anna Zaleska for tea, and drank half of it, and went upstairs, and slept the afternoon; and at six in the evening she came down with her skin burning and a blackness at the edges of the room, and Anna Zaleska put her to bed with a hot brick and a quilt; and by nine o'clock she was delirious, and by eleven the Colonel had telephoned the district's medical officer, and Frau Doktor Weingart came in a car through the rain at midnight with her instrument bag, and stood at the end of the bed in the room of a doctor's daughter and said the following, to the Colonel of the intelligence section of the Warsaw district, in the hearing of the household:

"Pneumonia, both lungs, right one worse; and I want the window open, and the room emptied of the noise of this house, and a woman who will sit with her through the night, because in this city in this war people die of the pneumonia of November eleven times more than they die of the eastern front, and I am not going to write out a burial certificate for a woman of thirty whose lungs are full of water in the fourth year of a war because a colonel wanted his dinner brought on time."

And Lotte Erler came at eight the following morning with a bag and a set of her own towels, and moved into the house for eleven days.

---

There is a particular kind of knowledge that only comes to a woman in a fever, and it is not a dream, and it is not the delirium of the children's stories; it is an inventory; and for the eleven days of her pneumonia in the room of a doctor's daughter in November 1943, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna did her life over, from the beginning, in order, with the accuracy of a woman who could not stop.

She did the house on the Mokotowska with the lamp in the hall. She did the piano, and the green dressing gown, and her mother's hands on the keys. She did the Botanic Garden in June 1938, in more detail than she had ever permitted herself since the day it happened. She did the platform at the Gdańsk station in the winter of 1940, and the room in Kraków with a bad fire, and the small man with a bad heart who had gone by a dog's name — and she did it four times, and each of the four times he was saying a different sentence, and in the fourth he was saying, in his careful, pedantic voice, *the arrangement is not a protection, my little rabbit; it is a postponement; and a postponement is that it always, in the end, has a date on it.*

She did the rue des Rosiers. She did the wall on the north side of the city of Warsaw, and the pair of boots on the coping, and the death-notice pasted on the outside where the Germans could see it. She did the garden in the dark with the wireless playing a fado in the courtyard of a Polish house. She did the funeral, which she had seen at ninety metres out of the window of a tram for the space of four seconds.

And on the ninth day, in the afternoon, when the fever had gone and the room had stopped moving, she lay in the bed of a doctor's daughter in a German house in Warsaw and found the thing that had been waiting for her at the bottom of the inventory; and it was not her mother and it was not her father and it was not the room in Kraków.

It was the fact that she had done it, from the wall to the tram, and had felt nothing at all.

Not numbness. She knew numbness: numbness was the thing in Paris in the bathroom with the velvet curtain, and it was a lining that came up between the woman and the world, so that the world came to her at a distance of a hand, and could be looked at, and could be worked on, and could be measured. What was in the bed in November 1943 was not a lining. It was a floor. There was nothing underneath the floor at all.

She lay in the dark of the room of a doctor's daughter with her hands on the quilt and did the last and worst piece of arithmetic of her life, which was to look at the whole thing from the outside, the way a stranger would look at it:

*There is a woman of thirty-one in a German house in Warsaw who takes her meals with the intelligence chief of the district; who has watched her father's funeral from a tram; who has had a family's death and a city's wall in her hands for four months and has done, for those four months, exactly what she was told, without one single hour of any symptom at all; and there is no such woman; a woman is not a machine, and a machine is not a person, and somewhere between the fourth of March 1943 at the Gare de l'Est and this bed, there was a moment at which me stopped being a person and became the arrangement; and the arrangement does not have a birthday, and the arrangement does not have a father, and the arrangement watched that funeral through a window at eleven kilometres an hour with its hand on a leather strap, and it did not cost it anything at all.*

And in the middle of the arithmetic, with the room going dark, she found that she was crying, at last, four days late and nine days deep, in the bed of a doctor's daughter in a German house, and she cried the way a person cries when the machine has stopped: without any shape to it, and without an end.

Lotte Erler heard her from the landing and came in and sat down on the edge of the bed without a word; and she did the correct thing, which was to sit and put her hand flat on the quilt over the top of the crying woman's head, and to stay there; and after four minutes, without any transition at all, in the manner of a woman of thirty-three from Hamburg who had been a legal secretary and had spent four years watching the wives of the administration of the city of Warsaw do the impossible, she said:

"When my husband is killed, and he will be killed, because he is a Hauptmann of transport at Minsk and they are killing the transport officers at a rate of a hundred a month, I am going to have a whole afternoon in which I do not do one correct thing. That is my plan. That is my plan for the rest of the war. One afternoon." Her hand stayed on the quilt. "You may borrow it if you like. I have found that the only thing of any value that a woman in this administration has to lend another woman is the permission to stop."

---

The Colonel came to her on the twelfth night.

The fever had gone; the house had, in the way of the houses of the important, returned to its ordinary: Anna Zaleska, the kitchen, the ordinary; Lotte Erler had gone home at six o'clock after a lecture in the drawing room on the necessity of the window being open; and at eleven the Colonel came up from the study, where the district had been telephoning since nine, and stood at the foot of the bed with the lamp on behind him.

He had been drinking. She knew it from the four steps he took into the room, which were a centimetre too long; and she knew, in the same four steps, the reason he had come, and she lay in the bed of the doctor's daughter and did the arithmetic that she had been doing since October 1942: *nine weeks of a district and a war, and this man has just been told by Berlin that a thing he promised cannot be done, and there is exactly one remedy for it in his world.*

He came to the bed and looked at her.

"You are better," he said.

"I am better."

"They have told me that I am not to disturb you."

"Frau Doktor Weingart said that to you on the first night, in front of the house. It was addressed to the house and not to you, and you are a colonel and I am a kept woman and the room knows the arrangement; so do not stand at the end of the bed and make me do the work of inviting you, because I am ill and I am tired and I would rather have it spoken."

There was a silence in the room.

"You are not a kept woman," he said.

"No. I am a woman in a house. There is a difference, and it is the difference that makes this possible, and it is now eleven o'clock at night and there is a war and a district and there is this bed."

He got into the bed.

And then he did what he had come to do, and in it he was not cruel, which was worse; he was slow, and enormous, and utterly without any question at all, and in the middle of it, with his whole weight on her, with his mouth against the side of her neck and a district of Warsaw on the other side of the wall, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna lay in the bed of a doctor's daughter with her eyes open in the dark and made the last and worst discovery of her life.

*I feel nothing.*

*Not disgust, which I have felt, in Paris, in the rue de la Faisanderie, in the room in the tower with the candle on the floor. Not hatred, which I have kept for the last hour of the night and used, all winter, the way a woman uses a coal in a stove.*

*Nothing at all: as though the body of Marion Herschlag had been handed over to a contractor; as though there were a woman in the bed who was being had by a colonel of the Abwehr, and a second woman, standing one metre back, in the dark, in the cold, watching the first one work, and the second woman was doing arithmetic about a safe, and neither of them was anybody at all.*

When he had finished he put his arm over his eyes, which was the thing he did, and lay there breathing; and after a while, in the dark, with the weight of what he had just done still in the room, he said, in the flat, level voice of a man who is not asking and is not confessing but is, in his own way, *giving*:

"In March, I am to be a Generalmajor."

"I know."

"You know?"

"The Frau Generalin has had a new hat for three weeks, and the wives' table has been speaking in the future tense about the house of the General of the infantry, and there is only one promotion in this district that would be worth a new hat in November." She lay on her back in the dark beside a man who had just been given a city, and looked at the ceiling with the bandage on it. "I am not a fool, Friedrich. I am a woman in a house. I hear the district over the soup."

He laughed: once, from the chest, the door closing, the bark, the man in one sound.

"March," he said. "And in March the matter of the east is going to be decided; and I am told that the wall in the north of the city is going to be cleared, and that the operation is to be conducted by the SS and not by the Wehrmacht, and that my section is to provide the intelligence and take no part in the physical operation, and I have read the order and I have signed my name on the cover sheet, which is what a colonel of intelligence does in this war: he signs the cover sheet and the rest is done by men with shovels."

She lay in the dark and did not move.

"Friedrich."

"Yes."

"The wall."

"Yes."

"There are a hundred and fifty thousand people behind it."

"There are," he said. And then he said the thing that she carried with her for the rest of her life, and that she heard again, in the dust and the noise of the summer of 1944, in the moment when her world came down: "There were four hundred and ten of my men in the Crimea, and fourteen officers who shot themselves, and the mothers of them, and I wrote the letters in my own hand, and the fact is that a man is given a district, and it has a hundred and fifty thousand people in it, and a wall, and an order; and there is no version of the rest of my life in which I am not the man who signed the cover sheet. So I do not intend to be the man who signed it *and* the man who talked about it in bed. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Good." He put his hand on her stomach, flat, the weight of it; the claim; the register. "Then we shall not say another word about it, in this room or in any other room, in the war."

---

The river took two days from her in February.

They went on a Sunday, in the car, to a place about nine kilometres downstream of the city, past the Praga bank, where the Vistula in the fifth winter of the war was a kilometre of ice and snow with a road across it that the Germans had made, and a row of black holes in it where the Poles had cut the ice for water the winter; and it was a German officer's Sunday, of the kind that the district had invented in the fourth year of the war: a drive in a car, and a walk on the frozen river, and a luncheon out of a hamper, and three other cars and eleven other officers and four wives, and it was photographed by a Hauptmann of the propaganda section for the district's illustrated weekly, which was printed in Warsaw in German for an audience of the German administration of Poland, and which was, in the four years of the war, the single most obscene object that the city produced.

She walked away from it after lunch.

She did it as a woman of the German administration does, with the appearance of doing nothing at all: she took a rug, and put it over her arm, and walked along the ice towards the west into the white, towards the place where the river bends away from the city and there is nothing on the horizon except the snow, the reeds, and a black hut on the bank that had been a fisherman's before the war.

He came eleven minutes later, on the ice, in a Wehrmacht greatcoat, without a hat, with his hands in his pockets.

"They will notice," she said, without turning round.

"They will notice a captain of the section walking on the ice for ten minutes in a Sunday and talking to the woman of his colonel at a distance of four metres. It will be in the wives' room by Wednesday, and on Friday somebody will make a joke, and by the end of the month it will have become a thing that the district knows and does not think about, which is the greatest protection that exists in this administration." He stopped four metres from her. "We have four minutes and then I must go back and be photographed."

"Then use them."

"I am on the list for Riga since September," said Phillip Brandt, "and in November they moved the list to a depot at Minsk, and in December the Minsk depot was bombed, and my file was in a room in that building, and it is possible, in this war, for a file to be burned; and I mention it because I have spent the winter deciding whether to burn it, which is a thing I could not do for any reason that would survive being written down, and I have decided not to, and I want you to know that I decided not to for the sake of one thing only, and it is that if I burned my file I would have to leave this city, and I am not able to leave this city, and there is no sentence in the German language that expresses that without being a confession."

"No," she said. "There is not."

"Now I have said it."

"You have."

"In the six months since the garden, I have thought about nothing else, and I want to tell you the thing that I have concluded, and I want you to hear it without any of the *arithmetic* in it, because the arithmetic is yours and I have no objection to it, but I am not able to live in it, and I am not a Pole in a cell; I am a man of twenty-eight who has been given a uniform and a city and a war, and I have decided that it is a loan."

She turned round, on the ice, in the white of a Polish February, and looked at him for a long time without one word.

"Phillip."

"Yes."

"I am going to tell you one true thing about what I am, and then we are going to walk back separately, and it is going to be worse than anything you have imagined, and I want you to hear it in the exact words in which I mean it, because if you hear it and turn away, then you will go back to the district and everything will be as it was in October." She stood in the cold with the rug over her arm and the wind off a kilometre of ice. "There is a room in the house on the Mokotowska with a photograph of a dead woman in it, and behind the photograph there is a steel box in the wall, and in the box there is a page about a line on the river to the east that is to be held until a date in August of next year, and there is a list of names of the men of the intelligence of the district, and there is a file about a captain who was born in Warsaw in 1915, and it is going to come out of that box in the spring of 1944, and there is exactly one person in this city who is going to take it out, and it is not you."

He looked at her on the ice.

"No," he said. "It is you."

"Say it plainly. I want you to have said it once, out loud, on the ice, in the daylight."

"You are an intelligence asset of the Polish underground, and you are in the house of the chief of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and you have been placed there the way the French network was placed there, and everything in your body has been the price that a woman pays in a war that men have made." His whole face, in the cold, was not the face of an officer. It was the face of a boy of nineteen in a thin shirt in front of a glasshouse holding a closed book. "And there is nothing in the world that I am able to do about it, because I am a German officer with seven weeks written over twice in my file, and the only thing I have ever been able to give you is the fact that I have never once, in the year in which I have known, said a single word."

"And what do you want?" she said.

And in the white of a Polish February, on a kilometre of ice, four metres from him, with the district at the tents behind them, and the city on the bank, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna heard a man of twenty-eight say the single most dangerous sentence that anybody had said to her in the war.

"The war is going to end," he said, "and when it ends there is going to be a city, and it is going to be the city of the men who have done what men do in a war; and through it, in the ruins of it, there is going to be a woman walking; and I want to be the man walking beside her."

"Then you are going to have to wait," she said, "until it is over, and there is not going to be a single day in the time between now and then in which I am able to promise you anything at all; and you are going to have to live with what is going on in a bedroom four metres from your desk in a house on the Mokotowska, and I am not going to protect you from it, because it is my work."

"Krystyna."

"Go back," she said. "Go and be photographed."

The middle of the river was the middle of the country.

She had crossed it four times in the war, always in the Mercedes on the bridge, with the flag of the district on the wing and the city going past the window of the car at the speed of an official of the German administration; and on the twenty-first of March 1944, in the fourth winter of the war, she crossed it on her own feet, at eleven o'clock in the morning, in the wind, in a black coat with the collar of a man's, with a rug over her arm and a pass of the district in her bag, because she had told the household that she was going to the shops of the Praga for a morning and there is no paper in the German administration of Warsaw that a woman of a household cannot get for a morning of the shops.

The ice was a road.

It had been a road since the middle of January, when the Vistula froze from bank to bank in one night, and the administration of the German army had put the little wooden huts of the ferrymen on the ice at the two ends and a line of the posts of the engineers across the middle and a signboard with the German text on it; and the city of Warsaw had looked at the signboard for one day and had then done the thing that a city of a million people does with an ice road: the men of the Praga came across it with the sacks of the flour and the potatoes in the sledge, the boys of the two banks came across it on the skates of the year 1938, the women of the Wola went across it to the market of the Praga with the baskets on their heads because the tram over the bridge stopped for the month of the February, and there was, along the middle of the Vistula between the Poniatowski and the Citadel, a village of the little houses of the fishermen, built up out of the boards of packing cases, with the smoke of the fires coming straight up out of them in the still air of the Polish March, and with the fish of the river on the ice in the front of the doors of them, frozen and stacked like the firewood.

She walked out into the middle of it at eleven o'clock, and stopped, and turned round, and looked at the city of her birth from the middle of the river.

There was nothing in the world like it, and there is nothing in the writing about that city that has ever been able to put it down: Warsaw seen from the ice in the fourth winter of the war, at the hour of the middle of the morning, in the light of a March in Poland that is the colour of the inside of an oyster. The city was standing in the smoke of its own chimneys, brown and grey and white, and along the southern bank there was the wall of the ghetto, and beyond the wall, where the street of the Gęsia had been, there was not a street and not a wall and not a chimney: there was a plain.

They had done it in the month of the May of the year before, and they had finished it and had levelled it with the labour of the men of the labour administration and the machinery of the engineers of the SS, and in the four kilometres of the plain there was snow and broken brick and, in three places, the corner of a street standing up out of it like the stump of a tooth; and along the southern edge of the plain, in the distance, there was a line of the houses of the Polish city with their backs to the level of the ruin, and there was washing on the lines of the yards of them, in the wind, white on white against the snow, and it was the smallest and the most terrible object in the view of a city from a frozen river.

She looked at it for four minutes.

And while she looked at it, a boy of about eleven came up the ice from the north with a sledge and a line, and stopped beside her, and looked at the city with her, in the way that the children of Warsaw in the fourth winter of the war had learned to look at things beside anybody at all; and after a while he said, in Polish, without any preamble, in the voice of a child stating the business of the morning:

"There is a man under the ice at the second post."

"What?"

"Under the ice." He said it without any drama, in the way of a person reporting the state of the river, and he pointed with his chin at the second of the posts of the engineers, twenty metres to the south. "He came across in the night in the dark from the other side, and the ice at the second post is bad, because the water of the sewers comes out there, and my father says he is a man of the camp on the Dzielna, and my father says that there is a of the month that the men of the camp have been coming across in the night, and the men of the German police have been coming across after them, and my father says that we are not to say anything about it to anybody in the world, so I am telling you, because you are a woman and you are looking at the city, and because my father says it to his own line of the fish and not to a person."

"Are you not afraid to be on the ice with the German police on it?"

"The German police do not come out on the ice," said the boy, in the tone of a man of sixty, "because the German police have the boots of the German police, and the boots of the German police have the nails in the heels of them and the ice of the Vistula laughs at the nails; and because there are two hundred men of the Praga on the ice in the morning and them are the men of the fish of the fish market of the Praga, and there is not one of them who is able to be asked a question about a man under the ice, because them say the same thing, which is that the ice at the second post is bad and that the man is under the ice and that the ice is the ice." He took up his line. "There are four more under the ice between here and the bridge. The girls of the school know the four. We count them."

Then he gave her the last thing, in the way that the children of that city gave things in that war: he took the smallest of the fish of his line, a thing of the length of a hand, frozen, and held it out to her, and waited, and when she did not take it he put it down on the ice in front of the toes of her shoes himself, and went off down the ice with the sledge towards the north, with his weight on the balls of his feet, in the way of a child of a river town, running and sliding wherever he could.

She picked the fish up off the ice and carried it across the rest of the river in the rug over her arm, because a woman of the household of a General of the district of the German army who returns from the Praga with the shops in a bag is a woman who has bought a fish at the market and is going home to cook it, and because the fish of a boy of eleven from the middle of the Vistula was, in the fourth winter of the war, the only object in her possession that had not been either requisitioned, bought, stolen, or given to her by a German.

He went back. She stood on the ice for eleven minutes with the rug over her arm, in the wind, and looked at the white of the river towards the east, and prepared, with the cold attention that she had, the exact words in which she was going to tell a colonel of the Abwehr at dinner that the afternoon had been beautiful, and that she had a slight headache, and that she wanted to go home early.
