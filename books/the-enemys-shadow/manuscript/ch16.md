# Chapter 16: Aleja Szucha

They made him a Generalmajor on the first of March, and on the fourth of March the district moved into the house of the General of the infantry, which stood on the corner of the Bagatela with three floors, a garden, a fountain that no longer ran, and a marble stair that every officer's wife in Warsaw had admired for four years.

The house on the Mokotowska went to an Oberst of the supply section who arrived with a wife, two children and a piano from Budapest. Anna Zaleska and Piotr Zaleski went with the house, as they had gone with it from one General to the next since 1940, because a Polish cook and a Polish caretaker were, in the accounting of the German administration of Warsaw, indistinguishable from the fixtures.

She said goodbye to them in the kitchen on the third of March, and she did it properly: a bolt of good cloth bought with the household money on the Nowy Świat, a month's wages, and a letter in Polish in her own hand, addressed to a woman who did not exist.

Anna Zaleska took it and stood in the doorway of her kitchen with her hands in her apron, and said, in Polish:

"The lady will need a cook in the new house."

"The new house has a cook. She is a German woman from Bydgoszcz, she cannot make a soup, and the whole district knows it, and there is nothing I can do about it."

"Then the lady will eat the bad soup on the Bagatela." She wiped the table. Then, without any change of voice at all: "My Piotr has the contract for the gardens of the German houses. He will be at the side gate of the Bagatela house on Tuesdays and Fridays with the tools." She wrung out the cloth. "The wall at the side of the Bagatela garden is the wall of the yard of the building in the next street. The yard belongs to a laundry. The laundry has three doors, and the woman who owns it is my Piotr's cousin's wife, and she keeps a horse."

She said it in the manner of a cook discussing a delivery; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the doorway of the kitchen and listened to a woman of fifty hand her the plan for the last week of the war, wrapped inside a paragraph about a horse.

"Anna."

"The lady has lived five years in this city and has never once asked me for anything." Anna Zaleska folded the cloth. "My Piotr and I have our lives, and the boy in the chemist's shop has his. The arrangement is the arrangement: we are the Poles of the house, and you are the woman of the house, and we do not say it to each other and we do not say it to anyone, and on the last day of the war, if we are all alive, we will have a coffee in this kitchen, and that is everything I intend to say about it."

---

The arrests came on the eleventh and twelfth of March.

They came the way the arrests in Warsaw came: at half past four in the morning, in the dark, with vans in the street; and the district learned about them over the following eleven days, in fragments. A Polish clerk of the transport office, forty-one years old, with a wife and three children. A Polish engraver who had done the letterheads. The Polish woman who kept the flower shop at the corner of the Bagatela. And, on the eleventh day, the Polish secretary of the supply section: a woman of thirty-four, unmarried, who had been in the administration four years and who was, in the opinion of every German officer in the building, the best clerk they had.

Her name was Halina Zawadzka.

Marion Herschlag had spoken to her perhaps eleven times in a year — the ordinary exchanges of women in a building: a form, a file, the address of a shop. Once, in July, they had waited out a General together in a corridor of the Blue Palace and had spent eleven minutes talking about the price of shoes, in the tone of two women who have nothing to say to each other. And in the middle of that eleven minutes, Halina Zawadzka had said one thing about the German filing system that had stayed with her: *the General files transport under coal and coal under transport, and one day the entire war is going to be filed in the wrong drawer.*

She was taken on the tenth of March at four in the morning.

On the fifteenth, at the officers' mess, over the soup, the district was informed that the Polish secretary of the supply section had, in the cellars of the Szucha, after four days, made a full statement, and that the statement concerned the Poles employed in the offices of the district and the Poles employed in the households of its officers.

That sentence put Krystyna Stankiewiczówna in the corridor of aleja Szucha 25 on the nineteenth of March at nine in the morning.

---

The building was a long, three-storey block of the nineteen-twenties that had been the Ministry of Education of the Republic of Poland, standing at the end of the most beautiful avenue in the south of the city: chestnuts down the middle, fashionable Warsaw walking it on Sundays in the twenties. In the fifth year of the occupation the avenue was a corridor of two buildings facing each other across the trees. On the west side, the German police. On the east side, at number 25, the Sicherheitsdienst.

Between them, in daylight, Poles went up and down the avenue in the ordinary way. Not one of them, in five years, ever walked on the west pavement, where the doors stood open.

She was driven there in the Mercedes with the flag on the wing, by the driver of the General of the intelligence of the district, on an errand that had arrived the previous evening on a printed card with a typewritten hour on it, handed to her at the dinner table by a German orderly.

She had eleven hours to prepare, and she spent them doing the only two things there were to do.

The first was to sit in her room in the light of the window for one hour and put her own head in order, item by item, from the beginning: the name; the birth date; the false province of Alsace; the uncle's boots in the flood of 1919; the husband killed near Abbeville before the armistice; the couture house on the rue de Bassano; the four names in Paris; the nine names on the rue des Rosiers; the carbon copy with the date stamp of 22 XII 42; the green door in the rue Cambon; and the eleven minutes of the twenty-eighth of March 1943 in a small salon on the second floor of the Hôtel Majestic with a man who polished his spectacles on a handkerchief.

The second was to build, deliberately, the one true story she owned.

Because there was a fact with her name on it, and it had been made in a corridor of the Propaganda Staffel on the evening of the eleventh of December 1942, when she had walked twenty minutes between a lavatory and a window while a General talked in a courtyard, and when a list of names in the south of France had moved from a briefcase into a matchbox camera; and if anyone in Europe was going to find that fact, it would be the man with the green notebook, because he had spent three days in the Hôtel Majestic in April 1943 writing down every one of the twenty minutes of a hundred and eleven people.

So she made the story short enough to be carried, and she carried it out in the open, in the daylight, on the theory that a guard looks for a bomb that is hidden.

---

The room was on the first floor and had one window on the inner courtyard, with bars painted the same cream as the walls. A table, two chairs, a portrait above the door hung at the height of a man's eyes, a telephone on the wall. And the corridor outside it, which carried, over the four hours of the interview, three sounds in rotation: boots; iron doors; and the gong.

The gong was on the second floor. It was an instrument of the police of the city of Warsaw, and in the five years of the occupation it was used whenever the men of the Sicherheitsdienst wanted the people in the cells to hear the people in the other cells.

Werner Kroll came in at eleven minutes past nine with two files under his arm and a green notebook in his hand. He did not sit down. He stood on the far side of the table and looked at her for a long moment, and then did what he had done in Paris: took off his spectacles and polished them on a handkerchief.

"Fräulein Herschlag."

"Frau."

"I said Fräulein, and this is the second time you and I have had this conversation." He put the spectacles on. "I will begin by telling you the thing that no man of my service would tell a woman in your position, and I tell you because it is the reason I asked for you and not for the wife of any other officer in this district."

"Tell me the reason."

"In April 1943, in Paris, I interviewed a hundred and eleven people in the administration of the Militärbefehlshaber. Of those hundred and eleven, exactly one was able, in an eleven-minute interview, to give me an account of twenty minutes on the ninth of December 1942 in the order in which those twenty minutes happened." He opened the green notebook and did not look at it. "Everybody in the world tells the story of a memory in the order of importance: the first thing, the biggest thing, the rest. You gave me a woman walking to a lavatory, and you gave me the twenty minutes in the order of the clock."

"Then you already have the answer."

"I have the fact. I want the reason."

And she gave him the reason, and it was the only true sentence she had built in eleven hours, and she gave it flatly, with the small shrug of a woman of the middle class of Paris explaining a professional deformity.

"The Conservatory," she said. "Seven years of the piano in Warsaw, before the marriage. A girl who has been drilled at a keyboard from the age of nine keeps time whether she wants to or not; and a woman who keeps time tells a story in the order of the clock, because that is the only order she has ever been able to think in. It has been of use to you, Hauptsturmführer. It has never once in my life been of use to me."

Kroll was quiet for a moment. Then he laughed — a small bark, the only time in two years she ever heard him do it.

"The Conservatory in Warsaw," he said, "and the school at Sèvres, and the mother's people from Kehl. You are the only person in this building whose file I have read four times, and I have read it four times because there is nothing in it that is wrong."

"Then there is nothing in it to read."

"There is nothing in it to read," Kroll agreed. "That is what I said."

He sat down.

"Now," he said. "I will tell you what this interview is for."

---

What it was for took the rest of the four hours, and it was not what she had come for, and it was worse: it was a recruitment.

In February there had been a leak.

A page of a schedule of the fortification of the city had reached the underground, and the Sicherheitsdienst had, over five weeks, arrested eleven Poles out of the offices of the district, and had got two names out of them, and both names were dead ends, because the Polish underground of Warsaw was a machine of cells of five, and a cell of five that has been broken is a cell of five that has been broken.

By March the SD had reached a conclusion, and the conclusion was that the page had not come out of the Polish offices at all. It had come out of the German households: out of the houses of the officers of the district, which had never once in five years been systematically searched, because a German officer's household is his private life, and the private life of a German officer in the fifth year of the war was the one territory in the occupied east where the Sicherheitsdienst needed an order from the Reichsführer himself to put its hand.

"So we shall make the search," said Kroll, "in the spring, with the consent of the officers, through the wives of the officers."

He opened the first file.

"In the twenty-two households of the staff of this district there is one woman who is not a German. She has been in the society of the officers in two capitals. She speaks four languages. She has been in Paris and she has been in Warsaw, and she has been in the apartments of the Militärbefehlshaber and she has taken tea at the table of the Frau Generalin, and she is, in the assessment of my service, the single rarest thing in this administration."

"And what is that?"

"A woman who is not, in any respect at all, a person of the war," said Werner Kroll. "Every other woman in this district is a wife, or a widow, or a mother, or a mistress with a grievance. All of them talk; that is what they are for; the whole administration is held together by the fact that the wives talk to each other in the mornings about what the men say in the nights." He put his finger on the file. "You do not talk. In eleven months in this city, you have not told me one syllable about that household. And a woman who does not talk is either a woman with nothing to say, which is not possible in a house with a General in it, or a woman who is being careful, which is the most useful object in my profession."

She looked at him across the table.

"You want me to inform on the German households."

"I want you to give me the weather," said Kroll. "Not papers. Not names, unless the names walk into your hands. The weather: which of the wives is drinking, which of the husbands is writing letters to Berlin that he should not be writing, which of the houses has a Pole who has been in it three years and has a cousin, which of the women at the Frau Generalin's table has stopped coming, and why. You will be paid a hundred marks a month through the district's miscellaneous account, and you will be given a card, and the card will be signed by me, and it will state that you are employed in the office of the district administration; and the card will do two things: it will make you invisible, and it will make you mine."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you will go on being the woman of the General of the intelligence of the district, and you will be watched for the rest of the war by a man with a notebook, which is what you have been for the last eleven months, and no harm will come to you at all, because you have committed no crime, and I do not arrest people for being careful." He closed the file. "I have told you the offer, which is unusual, and I have told you the price, which is unprecedented. I have done it because you are the only person in this building who will understand the arithmetic of it: I am not asking you to betray your house. I am asking you to become the only woman in the administration of Warsaw who has an excuse to be in every house in it."

She sat in the chair on the far side of the table, in the room with the bars painted cream, and listened to the boots go down the corridor, and looked at the man with the green notebook, and did the largest piece of arithmetic of her life.

Because it was on the table in front of her, in the open, in the daylight, and what was on the table was this: an official card signed by the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw; a reason to be in every German house in the city; a reason to be in every room, at every table, at every hour; a hundred marks a month; and a man to whom she would owe a small, regular, useful, verifiable supply of trivia about the wives of the officers of the district, which she could manufacture, out of her knowledge of them, for a year, without once giving him anything true.

And in exchange for that: she would be Kroll's.

Not his body, and not his woman, and not even, in the ordinary sense, his informant. She would be the object in his index: the one file in the two hundred and eleven that had a card of its own, with a signature on it, and that made her the single most protected person in the Generalgouvernement, because a man does not arrest his own instrument; and it made her, at the same time, the woman who in the last week of the war would be standing in a house on the Bagatela with a card in her bag, in her own false name, signed by a Hauptsturmführer of the Sicherheitsdienst.

She had been trained in Kraków to think in five moves.

She made the fifth move and never afterwards regretted it:

"Terms," she said.

Kroll's eyebrows went up half a millimetre.

"I do not inform on the Poles," she said. "I do not inform on the household of the General, and I do not inform on any man or woman who works in the house where I live. I will not put a name in writing in my own hand. I will speak to you once a month, here, and you will write whatever you like in your book, and if at the end of the war there is a court, then the book is yours and the handwriting is yours and I have never signed a thing. And in exchange for that: you will put me on the list of the persons attached to the staff of the district, which is the list that permits a civilian woman to be evacuated to the west in the event of the movement of the civil administration, and to be assigned a place in a train."

Werner Kroll looked at her for a long moment.

"The list of the civil administration," he said.

"Yes."

"That list is for the families of the officers."

"I have been the family of an officer for a year and a half," said Marion Herschlag, "and I am not able to be that for the rest of the war without something in my hand. I do not want the diamonds. I want the seat on the train. That is the price, and it is the reason I am worth a hundred marks a month to you: because in this city there are two hundred and eleven women who will tell you everything for nothing, and not one of them will ever be able to tell you when the cars are being loaded."

He wrote one line in the green notebook. She watched him do it. Then he stood up and put his cap on, and adjusted it, and it was the same gesture as in a room on the second floor of the Hôtel Majestic, in Paris, in April 1943.

"You shall have the card on Friday," he said. "And Fräulein."

"Yes."

"I said in Paris that you were available, and I told you that available is a different matter from suspected, and that it always will be." He opened the door for her. "I was wrong. It is the same matter. Everything is the same matter, in the end; that is the discovery of my profession."

---

They walked her out along the ground-floor corridor, because the office on the first floor had a telephone call upon it and the clerk had come up with the message, and the clerk was a Polish woman of forty in a green cardigan who kept her eyes on the floor, and who was the reason that Krystyna Stankiewiczówna ever saw the inside of the corridor of aleja Szucha 25 with her own eyes rather than through hearsay, and carried it, all the way to the end of the war and beyond it, in a place where it could not be spent.

The corridor was twenty-two metres long and it had, along its left side, eleven doors.

The doors were ordinary office doors painted cream, with brass handles, and each of them had a small window in it of wired glass, and each had a hatch at the bottom, and each of them, from the inside, had a different sound; and if a person walked the length of it in the middle of the morning, that person heard the twentieth century in the space of twenty-two metres, and the sound that stayed was not the screams, because in the fifth year of the occupation the men of the SD of Warsaw had learned what the men of the SD of Warsaw had learned, which is that the sounds of a corridor are the most careless evidence in the world, and that there is a gong on the second floor for that reason. What stayed was the smell: the smell of the corridor of the building at aleja Szucha 25, which was a smell of carbolic and cabbage and the wet of a hundred winter coats, and which had, in it, underneath, something else that the women of Warsaw in the fifth year of the war had learned to identify as surely as a farmer identifies weather: the smell of a building in which a great many people have been very frightened.

She walked its whole length with a clerk in front of her and a man with a notebook behind her, at the ordinary pace of a woman of the administration leaving an appointment; and at the eleventh door there was a man in a German uniform of the police standing with a file, and the door was open, and she looked, because her craft was the craft of a woman who looks at nothing, and the discipline of looking at nothing is that you look at everything, at the pace of a woman who is thinking about her shopping.

There was a room; a table; a chair; a chair not occupied; a radiator; a drain in the floor of the corner; a bucket; and a young woman, sitting on the chair, in the clothes of a clerk of the district administration of Warsaw, with her hair down, and both her hands flat on the table, and her eyes on the table.

Halina Zawadzka.

The door of the room was shut behind Krystyna Stankiewiczówna by the man with the file, without any particular attention, the way a man shuts a door in a building where doors are always being shut, and the meeting had lasted the length of about a metre and a half of walking, and the fact was this: she had walked past her, in the corridor, at half past one in the afternoon of the nineteenth of March 1944, and neither of them had looked at the other, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna had understood, in the metre and a half, the thing that she had been carrying since the fourth of March without ever once looking at it, which was that the woman who had said *one day the entire war is going to be filed in the wrong drawer* had been taken from her bed at four in the morning and had been brought to this corridor, and that the reason was a page of a schedule that a woman in a house on the Bagatela was going to have to take out of a steel box in a wall in the spring.

---

Outside, the light lay along the chestnuts of the aleja Szucha.

The Mercedes stood at the kerb with the flag on the wing and the door held open by the driver of the General of the intelligence of the district, and along the opposite pavement, against the wall of the building on the west side, in the ordinary way of a Warsaw morning, eleven people were standing in a line with their hands at their sides, guarded by two men of the German police: the ordinary consignment of the small hours, waiting to be called in one at a time through a door with a brass plate, to be identified, or released, or moved to the Pawiak, or taken to the Szucha across the avenue, depending on paper, and knowing nothing, as they always knew nothing, about which.

She walked past the line at the pace of a woman who is thinking about a hat, and she looked at every one of them, because that was the discipline, and on the fourth of the eleven there was a boy of nineteen with a filthy bandage on his left hand, standing with his shoulders against the wall, looking at the ordinary afternoon the way a man looks at a thing he expects to be taken away from him in the next minutes; and the moment passed in the length of four footsteps.

She got into the car.

The car went up the avenue under the chestnuts in the light of the middle of a Polish afternoon in the fifth year of the war; and there was a woman of twenty-seven in a good coat in the back of it, with her hands folded in her lap and her face arranged in the manner of the women of the German administration of Warsaw, and about the woman in the car there was, to look at, nothing of interest whatever.

And what she was doing, for the seven kilometres between the aleja Szucha and the house on the Bagatela, was not thinking about herself at all.

She was doing her inventory, and it had four lines in it.

*Item one: within a week there will be a card in my bag signed by the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw, and the card will say that I am employed at the office of the district administration of the city of Warsaw, and with that card I shall be able to be in every German house in the Generalgouvernement, and in the last week of the war I shall be able to get through a road block and onto a train, in a false name, with a General's safe in my arms.*

*Item two: that card is a claim on me that will be paid once a month in a first-floor room with a man who polishes his spectacles, and I will pay it out of my knowledge of the wives of the German administration, and it will be manufactured, all of it, for the year, out of fiction, and I will be so good at it that he will come to believe that he has chosen the one woman in Warsaw who is not a person of the war.*

*Item three: I have seen the inside of the corridor, and the corridor has eleven doors, and I walked it at half past one in the afternoon with a cardigan clerk in front of me and a man with a notebook behind me, and I will spend the rest of my life among people who will want to know what it is like, and I will never tell them, and that will be the last thing about me that is entirely mine.*

*Item four: the boy on the fourth of the eleven, with the bandage on the left hand.*

*And the boy on the quai in Paris, with a Polish book about the botany of the Kampinos Forest, and no hat, in a January, in a good grey coat, who came out to the river in the middle of the day to say something to somebody who might carry it home, and who was taken at a market in the second week, buying bread.*

The car turned off the avenue into the street of the Bagatela.

In the garden the fountain that had not run since 1939 stood in the middle of the gravel with a bird on the rim of it, and the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw was at the window of his study on the first floor, with his hands behind his back, watching the car come in; and he was, in the account of the world, a man of fifty-five who had been given a district, a wall, an order and a woman, and who believed, with the belief of his generation, that the arrangement he had made in the middle of the war was the last piece of his life that was still his.

She got out of the car in the light of the afternoon of Warsaw on the nineteenth of March 1944, and went up the marble stair, and into the house, and up to her own room, to take off her hat.

And in the notebook of a Hauptsturmführer of the Sicherheitsdienst, on the line under her name, there was a sentence in his fine clerk's hand, written in the room with the bars painted cream while she watched him write it, and the sentence was this: *Brauchbar. Prüfen im Sommer.* Usable. To be tested in the summer.
