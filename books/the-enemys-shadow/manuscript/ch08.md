# Chapter 8: The Transfer

Warsaw came to her in the wrong order, the way a face comes back in the wrong order after eleven years: not the streets, which she knew by heart, and not the sky, which was the flat white sky of the Mazovian plain, but the sound.

The sound was a tramline being repaired.

They had come down off the long embankment that carries the line in from the west, past the yards and the water tower and the sheds that had been shelled in 1939 and had never been repaired; and then the train was slowing into the city, and she heard it: the ring of a hammer on a steel rail, in a long irregular sequence, two men and a sleeper, in the middle of a war, in the middle of a Tuesday morning. It was a sound from her childhood. It was the sound of every summer of her girlhood, in the city, and it meant nothing to the Colonel — who had turned the page of a report on the seat beside her and said, "We are here" — and it meant everything to Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, who sat with her hands folded in the sleeve of a coat with a film in it, and did not let her face move, and listened to a hammer on a railway sleeper like a woman listening to her own life being nailed down.

The station was the Warsaw Główny: a big grey hall with a glass roof that had two hundred panes missing, and a platform that was one third German — the Feldgendarmerie with their gorgets, the district staff, a general in a car with a flag, the smell of coal and soup — and two thirds Polish, which had been compressed into the far end, beyond a barrier, and which was silent. There were notices in two languages. There were two hundreds of people standing in the far part of the platform with their parcels, waiting for the trains that stopped at the sidings and the trains that did not; and there were German soldiers writing at a table under a lamp, and there was a woman in a brown coat being asked for her papers by a Feldwebel of twenty-five, and there was a boy of about fourteen on the far side of the barrier, looking through it at the German platform with a stillness that was entirely political.

The Colonel was received by an Oberstleutnant with a clipboard and a Hauptmann with a car; there was a small ceremony at the door of the carriage, which consisted of three sentences and a handshake; and then the car took them out of the station through the north side, and Warsaw began.

The first three hundred metres were the station district: the sheds, the coal heaps, the wooden barracks of the Organisation Todt, the great brick building of the waterworks with its roof broken open to the sky and its pumps working in the middle of the ruin, because the pumps had been repaired in 1940 by Polish workmen under a German foreman, and were, in the third year of the occupation, still pumping; and then the car turned into the streets.

She had known it. That was the fact around which she had built her breathing all morning, and the fact did not help her, because knowledge and seeing are two different rooms of one house.

The streets on the north side of the city had been walled off, and the car went along the wall at eleven kilometres an hour, because there were carts and horses and pedestrians in the road, and the Colonel sat back and looked out at the wall with the expression of a man inspecting a boundary he had been told about in a memorandum.

"It is the ghetto," he said. "In April we are going to clear it. There is a plan, and it is not a good plan, but it is a plan," and he said it the way he said everything: level, and final, and with the tone of a man for whom a thing that is written in a memorandum has already stopped being a thing and become a duty; and she sat beside him with her hands folded and looked at the wall.

She looked at it for eleven hundred metres and she saw: the brick wall with its coping and its wire, three and a half metres high; a pair of boots standing under a coat with nobody inside it, hung on the coping at a doorway, with a belt, and the belt was a good belt, and somebody had taken the boots; two children sitting on a stone by a gateway in the wall with their hands in their laps, doing nothing, in the middle of the day; a woman on the other side, and a woman on this side, standing at a gap in a brickwork, talking through it quickly in low voices in Polish about a parcel going out; a death-notice pasted on the wall, in Polish, a small black-trimmed paper with a photograph of a man of sixty and a paragraph under it, and the death-notice was from inside the wall and it was pasted on the outside of the wall, where the Germans could see it, and nobody had torn it down; and the ghetto wall was the wall of a city that was dying in front of the whole world, and everyone had agreed about it, and the charade was so complete that the wall itself was a piece of the agreement; and she sat in a car with a colonel of the Abwehr at eleven kilometres an hour and watched it, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was this: *if I move my little finger now, today, here, I am dead, and no one will know why, and the film will go into a German safe, and Odile Marchand will have said the word "aunt", in German, on a telephone, in order to save somebody who was not able to save her, and it will turn out that she saved nobody.*

So she did not move her little finger. She looked at the wall and listened to the Colonel and folded her hands, and when he said, "You are quiet," she said, "It is a great deal of ruin," and he nodded, with a certain satisfaction, because a great deal of ruin was exactly what he had been assigned, and it had been assigned to him because he was the sort of man who could be trusted with it.

---

The house was on the Mokotowska, on the German side of the city, and it stood behind a garden with a gate and a chestnut tree, and it had been built in 1911 by a doctor who had taken the advice of a French architect and gone to Zoppot for the summer every year of his life; and it had been taken by the district administration in 1940, and had been used by a General of the Luftwaffe for two years, and the General had gone to Italy, and there had been a smell of German soap in the bathroom and German filing in the study and nothing else in it at all; and the district had given it, with the garden and the staff, to the new intelligence chief.

It had five rooms down and four up. There was a drawing room with a piano — an upright, German, 1898, with a good action and a bad bass — and a dining room with a long table, a study with a desk and a bookcase and a safe that had been installed by the Organisation Todt in 1939, and a kitchen with a huge iron stove and a copper for the laundry; and it had a garden with two chestnuts and a walnut, and a side gate that went through into the yard of a block in the next street, and that gate was, for the sixteen months to come, the most important object in Krystyna Stankiewiczówna's whole inventory of the world.

The staff were a family.

That was the arrangement she had been told about on the train, in the way that German administration explained everything, which was by telling it as a piece of the assignment: *the house has Polish staff; they have been there since the General, and the district keeps them because they are clean and the woman cooks, and the husband keeps the garden and the furnace and the boiler; and one of them is from the old staff of the house and I am told is a good sort of man, for a Pole. I do not want them replaced, because a Pole who has been in the house for three years is a Pole whose habits I know, and a Pole I do not know is a Pole I have to watch; and so you will be civil to them, and they will be civil to you, and no one will say anything that matters.*

The woman was Anna Zaleska: fifty, heavy, with a face like a good loaf of bread, a widow's black in her dress, a woman who cooked German food with Polish hands and had a way of moving through a room with her shoulders slightly forward as though she were always carrying something; and the man was Piotr Zaleski: sixty-one, one ear, kept the furnace and the garden and the boiler, and had the hands of a man who had worked with iron in his youth and had broken the third finger of the right hand in a machine at a factory in 1929, and had never had it set.

They stood in the front hall, at attention, in the way that Polish servants had learned to stand for German officers, and the Colonel did his eleven sentences: *good day; the house is satisfactory; the wine cabinet in the dining room is mine; I sleep in the big bedroom; the woman — the Fräulein — has the room next to it; there will be three at table, sometimes four; the coal is Bundeswehr property and will be counted each week.*

"Jawohl, Herr Oberst."

"Anna. The room upstairs, the one beside mine, will be the Fräulein's. She is not my wife."

"Jawohl, Herr Oberst."

And that was it. The Colonel went into his study with his case; and Piotr went down into the cellar, where a furnace of 1911 was waiting for him; and Anna stood in the hall for a moment with her hands in the folds of her apron and looked at the woman who had come in a good coat out of a German car in the middle of the afternoon.

"Does the lady want tea?" she said, in German, very slowly, with the accent of a woman who has learned six sentences off a wall.

"I would like tea," said Marion Herschlag, in German, with her face composed; and then, deliberately, in the language of the house, with the accent of a woman who had learned a language off a wall: "And I would like hot water in the evening, at ten o'clock, without being asked. That is all. The rest I will do myself."

The woman's face did not change by a millimetre, and something in the room did change: the balance of a pair of scales that had settled, imperceptibly, at the moment when one woman decided that the other was not going to be a nuisance and was not going to be a friend.

---

The first night, the Colonel took her in the big bedroom of a doctor's house with the doors open to the corridor and a Germany army blanket under her and the smell of a new country in the room.

He had changed on the train; she had watched it happen, between Frankfurt and Warsaw, as plainly as a change in the weather. Berlin had done it to him, or the standing at the window of the district staff had done it, or the wall had done it; and the change was this: in Paris he had been a man with a posting, and in Warsaw he was a mission. He had looked at the wall on the north side of the city with the level, final expression of a man reading the last page of a document, and something had closed in him like a fist; and he was more careful now, more particular, more suspicious, and hungrier, because a man who has been given a district to be frightened of also needs to be fed.

That night he was rough in a way he had not been in Paris since January, and she learned, with the clear cold attention she brought to everything, that the next year was going to be a different order of work.

He held her down on the blanket; and he did it with his hand over her mouth — not hard, not to silence her, but because the Poles in the house were two floors away and a German officer's woman is not permitted to be heard in a Polish house; and that hand over her mouth was, in the sixteen months to come, the single most accurate description of her life in Warsaw: a woman who was allowed everything except the use of her voice.

When it was over he lay for a long time with his arm over his eyes; and then he said, into the room, in the dark, in German, to the ceiling:

"There will be a reception on Thursday at the Blue Palace. There will be the General, and the SS will send somebody, and there will be the wives, and I have decided something about you which you will not like."

"Tell me."

"You are to be nothing."

She turned her head, in the dark.

"You will be beautiful, and you will be quiet, and you will be known to the wives as a Frenchwoman whom the Oberst keeps, exactly as a General keeps a canary, and you will not have opinions, and you will not be charming to anyone above the rank of captain, and if a man in this district ever says to me, 'the Oberst's lady is *unusually intelligent*,' I will hear it as a report of a fault." He turned onto his side and looked at her with the pale eyes, and behind them there was nothing at all of the man who had cried in a room in a tower with a candle on the floor. That man was gone; she had seen the last of him on the platform at Forbach; and what had come east in his place was the man who had passed an order down the line without reading it. "You will be nothing for a year. And at the end of the year, when this district has been held and the General has written his report, I am going to send you out."

"Out where?"

"To the west. To the house in Pomerania, where there is a garden, and a piano, and a woman who will sit in a chair and read, and a man who does not sign anything." He put his hand on her stomach, flat, the whole weight of it, and left it there, and closed his eyes. "That is my estate. It is what is left of everything I have done. I have decided to give it to one person, and you are the only human being in Europe who does not want anything from me, and so it is yours; and if you are ever false to me, in any way, in this city where there are two hundred thousand people who would cut my throat for a ration of bread, I shall not shoot you. I shall take you to the cellar of the Blue Palace and I shall sit in a chair and watch other men do it, and I shall not say one word in your defence, because I will have learned, in that hour, that I have spent the last of my life on a thing that was not mine."

He fell asleep holding her, the way a man sleeps in a house he has just decided to defend.

She lay awake in the dark of the doctor's house, in a strange bed, in her own city, listening to the small noises of a Polish house at night: a floorboard, and the wind in a chestnut, and, three streets away, a tram going along Marszałkowska with its windows blacked out, and she thought about the film in the sleeve of a coat that was hanging in the wardrobe four feet away, and about a page in a safe in Paris with a date on it — the eighth of August, 1944 — and about ninety days of doing nothing, which was the promise she had made to a man with a bad heart in the Jardin des Plantes, who was not yet dead, but was on his way.

*Ninety days,* she thought. *Ninety days of being nothing. I can do that. I have done worse.*

And the next morning she began, in a house with a garden and a gate, to be the most nothing woman in the Generalgouvernement.

---

On the fifth day she went into the study.

It was a Thursday and the Colonel was at the Blue Palace from eight in the morning until the evening, and Piotr Zaleski was in the cellar with the coal, and Anna Zaleska was in the kitchen at the end of the corridor with the radio of the household on and the door of the kitchen shut, as it was shut in every German house in Warsaw in the year 1943 between the hours of eleven and one, because the German administration of the city took its dinner at one and the Poles of the house of a German officer took their one hour of the wireless while the soup cooked.

She went in with a duster.

The study of the house on the Mokotowska had been the consulting room of a doctor of the old Warsaw, and it had kept the shape of it: the heavy desk with the green leather top, the shelves of the medical reviews, the two cupboards of the instruments — the instruments were gone to the Reich in 1940 and the shelves had been filled by the district with the grey files of the intelligence service of the Warsaw district — and the window with the blackout frame, and the armchair in which a patient of the profession had sat and looked at the wall on which the first owner of the room had hung the diploma of the university of Dorpat of the year 1904.

The safe was in the corner behind the door.

She had seen it on the third day, in the first hour of the house; she had seen the corner and the door of it and the green of the repainted panel out of the corner of her eye at the moment of the arrival, the way a woman sees the furniture of a room she is being taken through by a man who is talking about the garden; and on the fifth day she went and stood in front of it for the first time in her life with the duster in her hand and her heart going at a flat sixty, in the middle of the ordinary morning of the house, with the wireless of the kitchen coming down the corridor and the smell of the soup of the household in the ground floor.

It was an Organisation Todt pattern of the year 1939: a box of the height of the thigh, set into the plaster of a wall of the old construction, with the door of the steel in the colour of the wall and the dial of the brass with the knurled edge, and — at the height of the hand, under the dial — a small plate of the brass with four lines of the stamping on it.

She did not touch the dial.

She stood in front of it with the duster and read the plate, in the Polish of the room of a doctor of Warsaw, in the light of the morning, for four seconds, and then she dusted the top of the box in the ordinary way of a woman of a house and went out of the study and up to her own room and wrote the four lines down on the back page of a prayer book.

*Gebr. Möller, Berlin SO 36. Bauart OT 1939. Nr. 44117. Schlüsselwerk W 44.*

The manufacturer and the number. And she lay on the bed of the room of the first floor for eleven minutes with the prayer book in her hands and did the arithmetic that a woman of the household of a German general does with a manufacturer's plate and a safe, and the arithmetic was: *the number is the registry number of an office of the German administration, and the administration of the German army is an administration of the paper, and the paper of the German army is in the office of the district, and the office of the district is four streets away.*

---

The office of the district was in the building of the command at the Blue Palace in the avenue of the Ujazdowskie, and inside the office of the district there was an office of the procurement and supply of the district, and inside the office of the procurement and supply of the district, in the second week of May 1943, there was a secretary of the Polish administration with a loyalty file that had been in the papers of the Sicherheitsdienst since 1941, and a typist, and a shelf of the catalogues of the firms of the Reich.

The list of the office materials of the district of the intelligence service of the Warsaw district was a page of the catalogue of the firm of Gebrüder Möller of the Berlin SO 36, with the entries for the fiscal year 1943: thirty-one boxes of the paper clips, the size 2; four boxes of the carbon paper, the German pattern; the paste of the district, eleven jars; and at the foot of the page, in the section for the *Waffen und Geräte* of the internal service of the office: the safes, the class of the internal service of the district, the boxes of the pattern OT 1939, three of the boxes, with the serial numbers of the three, and against each of the three numbers, in the column which the procurement clerk of the district had ruled for the purpose in the table of the office of the year 1943, the number of the combination of the lock.

The secretary of the Polish administration who carried that page out of the building of the Blue Palace in the month of May of 1943 wrapped it in the *Nowy Kurier Warszawski* of the third of May and put it under the lid of the cistern of the third closet of the first floor of the building, and it was in the hands of the officers of the organisation of the district within the week; and the number of the lock of the safe of the study of the house on the Mokotowska was in the head of the woman of the household of the Colonel of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw before the middle of the month of May of 1943, and she did not touch the dial of the box, and she did not touch it for the eleven months that followed, and the woman of the household of the Colonel of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw went on for the first month of the arrangement with a prayer book and a piece of the arithmetic and the face of a woman who was nothing at all.

---

She wrote the letter on the seventh day.

There was a system for it, and she had been given the system in a room of the Hôtel Central in the month of November of 1942 by a man of the organisation with a bad heart and a brown paper parcel, and the system was: a page of an ordinary letter of a woman of France to a friend of the province of the Loire, written in the French of the ordinary correspondence of two women of the middle class, in which the weather, the health of the family and the price of the meat were all true; and in the fourth line of the second page, in the syntax of a Frenchwoman of forty who has been at the lycée of Tours, the position of the words in a sentence that no German censor of the office of the district had ever once had the training to see.

*Ma chère Odile, la maison est grande, et le monsieur est un homme de la vieille école; les voisins sont polonais, et la rue est tranquille, et je pense à vous le soir, quand la lampe est allumée et que je ne suis pas encore couchée.*

*The house is big, and the gentleman is an old-school man; the neighbours are Polish, and the street is quiet, and I think of you in the evening, when the lamp is lit and I am not yet in bed.*

She put the letter in the box of the post of the administration of the district, with the address of the rue Cambon, and the letter went out of the city of Warsaw in the pouch of the administration of the district of Warsaw in the second week of May of 1943, and it was in the flat of a milliner of the rue Cambon in Paris in the evening of the eleventh day, and the milliner read it standing up at the table of the kitchen with the shutters closed, and the sentences of a woman of the middle class went into the fire of the stove of the flat, and the fourth line of the second page went out of Paris by way of a hat of the spring collection to a man of the organisation at Lyon on the nineteenth of the month, and was in the hands of the room in London in the last week of May 1943, and was the first piece of paper to come out of the city of Warsaw with the signature of the woman called WESPER.

*The street is quiet,* said the fourth line of the second page.

There is no Polish city in the year 1943 in which that sentence is true, and the man in the room in London read it and understood it and said nothing at all, and wrote in the margin of the file in his own hand, in the pencil of the office, the date and one word: *arrived.*

---

She did the first reconnaissance of her own life on the fourth day, with Ehlers, in the car, on the excuse of shopping.

There was shopping to do: the district had a store on the Nowy Świat for Germans only, with coffee and sugar and soap and tinned fish and a counter of women's stockings, and Marion Herschlag was entitled to it by the stamp on the *Ausweis* the district had given her at the station, a document the size of a postcard with her photograph on it and the words *Begleitung des Oberst v. Reuth* under her name, which meant that she was permitted to buy stockings and coffee, and not permitted to buy a railway ticket, and not permitted to walk in the streets between ten at night and five in the morning, and required to carry the little card against the breast of her coat whenever she left the house, and to show it to any German in uniform, at any time, for any reason.

Ehlers drove her along the Nowy Świat and the Krakowskie Przedmieście, and she sat in the back of the car with her gloves in her lap and looked out of the window at her own city, and was careful, for the length of a kilometre, to move her eyes the way a stranger moves them, in the slow horizontal way of a tourist; and it was harder than the wax key had been, harder than the wall had been, harder than the hand over her mouth in the dark.

There was the shop where her father had bought her a fountain pen in 1930: the sign was gone; the window had two hundred tins of shoe polish in it. There was the church where her mother's cousin had been married in 1934, with its steps full of people as they always are, and a German soldier sitting on the third step eating an apple. There was the confectioner's on the corner of the Świętokrzyska where Odile had never been, and where Marion Herschlag had eaten a *pączek* in 1937 in a grey coat with a fur collar of her mother's, and where the shop now had a plate on the door in German and a sign in the window in German and, in the window, a display of toothbrushes.

And there was the placard.

It was on the wall of a building at the corner of the Marszałkowska: a big white paper in a red border, in two columns, one in Polish and one in German, headed *OBWIESZCZENIE* / *BEKANNTMACHUNG*, and under the heading, the sentences that had been printed ten thousand times in the four years of the occupation of Poland, and would be printed, in the single sentence, in the single city, in the two months of the summer coming, more than three hundred times.

*In reprisal for the attack on a German officer.. the following persons have been sentenced to death and shot..*

She read the names. Then she read the placard under it, pasted below, which was a Polish thing and not a German thing, and which was the reason that a crowd of about forty people was standing in front of the wall at eleven in the morning, in silence, in the cold, in the very middle of a working day.

The placard below was a Polish announcement, printed by the Underground with a hand press in a cellar somewhere in the city: a single sheet, forbidden, punishable by death, and pasted up in the middle of the day in a street with a German sentry twenty metres away, and it said, in a hundred and eleven words, that the men on the German placard had not been shot for an attack; that they had been taken at random from the cells of the Pawiak prison because the German administration had a quota to fill before the visit of a General from Berlin; that their names were known; that the city knew their names; that their families would be provided for by the Polish community; and that the German administration of the city of Warsaw was, in the eyes of history, a criminal organisation; and it was signed, in the corner, with the anchor that every child in the city drew in the dust and the snow of every street in Poland.

Ehlers, who had stopped the car because of the crowd, looked at the wall for a moment through the windscreen.

"That is the second one this week," he said, in the flat voice of a man from Posen. "It is not the sentence that is the problem. It is that they keep pasting their paper over our paper in the middle of the day and nobody stops them; and my Marta says—"

He stopped. He had heard himself; he was a driver, and he said no more, and put the car into gear; and Marion Herschlag sat in the back of a German car in the middle of her own city and looked through the glass at a crowd of forty Poles reading a paper in the middle of the day, and understood, with the flat, huge, almost peaceful certainty of a woman who has just found her own handwriting on a wall in a burning house, that she was not going to have to wait ninety days for anything.

Warsaw was not Paris. In Paris she had been a knife in a drawer. In Warsaw she was going to be a knife in a hand.

Behind her, in the car, the film was in a hatbox with a red ribbon on it, in a trunk, in a house with a garden and a side gate, and thirty-one hours to the west of her, a woman in the rue des Saussaies was in a chair, and a boy of fourteen was still walking up and down outside a green door in the rue Cambon; and ahead of her, in the city, in the barracks and the cellars and the convents and the tram depots, there were forty thousand people who belonged to the same organisation as her, and every one of whom could kill her by saying her name.

The car crossed the Marszałkowska. In the window of a shop on the corner, behind the glass, in the middle of a display of German soap, somebody had scratched, with a diamond, at the height of a woman's knee, a single small anchor.
