# Chapter 18: Tempest

The wives left Warsaw on the twenty-eighth of June, on a train of eleven carriages with the doors sealed by the Feldgendarmerie and the windows painted white to the height of a man's shoulder.

Lotte Erler came to say goodbye at seven in the morning with two suitcases and a hatbox, in the hall of the house on the Bagatela, in the ordinary manner of a woman of the administration of Warsaw who had been told to be at the station at nine and who was, therefore, at somebody else's house at seven, drinking somebody else's coffee, because there is never any reason in the world to be early for a train that is going to be late.

They stood in the hall. The German cook from Bydgoszcz was in the kitchen making a noise with the pans; and upstairs, in the study, the General of the intelligence of the district was on the telephone to Berlin, which he had been since half past six.

"Well," said Lotte Erler.

"Well."

"I have a piece of paper with an address in Hamburg on it, and a piece of paper with an address in Kassel, which is my mother, and I have a card for a doctor in Dresden who will give me something for the nerves without writing it in a book, and I have twenty-two kilos of luggage." She set down the coffee cup. "I have known two hundred women in this city and I am going to be able to write to eleven of them, and eight of the eleven will not answer, because their husbands are going to be taken prisoner by the Russians and they are going to be ashamed of it, and in ten years there will be no one left at all who remembers that we took the tea together on Thursdays." She looked at her own gloves. "I am not going to say any of the ordinary things, because you are not the ordinary woman of the administration, and the district has known it for a year, and nobody has said it, because that is what we are: we are a district of people who know everything and say nothing, and that is why we are going to lose."

"Lotte."

"Do not." She picked up the hatbox. "I am going to say one thing, because I am a lawyer's daughter and I always explain the terms. There are four kinds of women on that train: the ones who believe the papers, the ones who believe in God, the ones who believe in their husbands, and the ones who believe nothing in the world. I have watched you for fifteen months and I have never once been able to decide which of the four you are, and there is only one of the four that would explain a woman who stays." She smiled with the mouth that turned up at one corner, and she was, at that moment, thirty-four years old, and she had been four years in the east, and her husband was at Minsk. "So I have written down your name in a book with the others, and I have put against it, in pencil, in the column of the remarks: *knows what she is doing.* And if we are both alive when this is over, and there is a city standing somewhere, and you want a lawyer — and you are going to want a lawyer, my dear, and I am sorry about that, and I am not going to ask why — you will come to Hamburg, and you will ask for Frau Erler, and there will be a woman of forty in a bad coat who will recognise you at once."

She kissed the air beside her ear, and went down the steps with the two suitcases and the hatbox, and got into the district car and went to the station; and the house on the Bagatela was quiet for a quarter of an hour afterwards, and then the cook went on with the pans, and the General went on with Berlin.

Three days later, in the cellar of a house in the Praga district, in a room where there were eleven fur coats under a sheet, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna met the man who had come out of the sky.

---

His name in the organisation was *Sokół*. He was thirty-nine years old and he was one of the two hundred and eleven people who had, over the four years of the war, been dropped into Poland at night out of a Halifax of the Royal Air Force, out of a hole in the floor of the fuselage, with a parachute, a pistol, a hundred pounds in zloty, a sabotage manual and a cyanide capsule in a sealed tube, into a country in which every human being he passed in the street could be either the reason he was alive or the reason he was dead; and the organisation called these people the Quiet Ones, and there were so few of them that each of them was, in the whole accounting of the war, worth more than a squadron of aeroplanes.

He had come down on a field at night in the middle of May, and he had spent the six weeks since then in the forests of the Kampinos with a wireless set and a formation of the sabotage units, and he had come into the city the way everything came into the city, in a coal lorry, under the coal.

He looked like a schoolmaster: thin, forty, in spectacles with one arm repaired with wire, and a bad chest, and the palms of both hands blistered in the shape of the handles of a spade.

He had four things to say, and he said them standing up, because there was nowhere to sit.

"The first is that I know who you are and what you have done, and I have come from London with the whole file read, and I want to tell you the single thing that none of the men in this city is going to tell you, which is that the men in London do not think about you at all. They think about a page. There is a room in a house in a suburb where four people sit at a table with the photographs of the German line, and what they do is arithmetic about the Vistula, and you are an entry in the arithmetic, and you are doing your work very well, and if you are killed tomorrow you will be replaced on the table by a note in the margin reading *WESPER: lost*." He cleaned his spectacles on his sleeve. "That is not cruelty. That is the war, and I have come four thousand kilometres to tell you the truth about it rather than the version with the flag in it."

"Say the second thing."

"The second is that the men of this city are going to rise in the first week of August whether London likes it or not."

The cellar was very quiet.

"Say that again."

"The first of August, or the second, or the third; the hour will be fixed four days before, and it will be fixed by a man who has to make the decision in a room in the Mokotów district with a telephone that does not work and eleven reports on the table, all of them wrong. The Russian army is on the river in the last week of July; the German army in this city is the wreck of the army of the east; and the government in London has ordered the underground of this city to take Warsaw from the Germans before the Red Army takes it from the east bank. And there is one thing more, and it is the whole reason that I am in this cellar, and it is the thing I have been ordered to say to you as the officer of the staff of the commander-in-chief." He put the spectacles on. "The rising is going to last ten days. It will last ten days, and after that there will be no ammunition, and after that the men in this city will be fighting with the German rifles that they take off the German dead in the street. And the German defence of the river will depend on a schedule in a safe in a house on the Bagatela that says how long they intend to hold a line, and where, and with what, and when the reinforcements are coming, and we do not know the answer to a single one of those questions; and you are the only person in the world who can get the paper."

"Then say the third thing."

"The third is the date."

He said it, and it was the twenty-fifth of July, and it was eleven days from the second thing; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in a cellar in the Praga district of Warsaw in the first week of July 1944 with eleven fur coats on a rail behind her, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was: *eighteen days between the paper and the rising. Eighteen days between the safe in the study and the first hour of the rest of my life.*

"Say the fourth thing."

And the man out of the sky, with the blistered palms of a man who had spent six weeks in a forest digging a hole for a wireless set, took off his spectacles and looked at her.

"I have been ordered to tell you the arrangement for the day you are taken," he said, "and to be certain that you have understood it, because the organisation has never once had a person in your position, and there is no precedent for it, and the men in the room where this was decided were four colonels and a priest in a house in a suburb of London in the month of April, and none of them had ever been in a room with a German general's woman, and this is what they wrote down for you." He did not read it. He had learned it by heart, in a Halifax, over the North Sea, at eleven thousand feet. "You are authorised, in the event of discovery, to say anything at all, to any man, including the truth, so long as the truth buys one hour; and you are authorised to take your own life rather than to be brought to the aleja Szucha with the knowledge that you have. And you are not, under any circumstances, to be rescued. And if you are in the house on the day that the city rises, you are to stay in the house, and you are to do nothing, and there is no plan for you; there is no file with a name in it; there is no man with a motorcycle and a spare coat; you are a woman of twenty-seven in a German uniform's house, and on the first of August, when this city begins to kill the German administration in it, you are going to be standing in a room with the man who has been in your bed for two years, and no one is going to be able to get to you, and no one is going to be able to save you."

"Then why did the four colonels and the priest write that down?"

"Because," said Sokół, "they knew that you would read it and would not be able to be told anything else. You are not a soldier; you are a woman of twenty-seven with a false name and a real memory, and there is not one order in the world that will keep you out of the last room of this war, because your life has been the last room, and you are going to be in it, and the men in the world know it, and they have sent me four thousand kilometres to say so, and to say the only thing that is left, which is: *do the work, and be careful, and, when the hour comes, do the thing that the last hour requires.*"

"And what is the thing that the last hour requires?"

"I do not know," said the Quiet One. "Nobody knows; that is what the last hour is. I have been dropped into this country twice out of an aeroplane at night, and I have been shot at sixty-one times, and I can tell you the truth about the last hour, which is that it is always a room, it is always somebody you have loved, and there is never any time at all."

The twenty-fifth of July was the date of the paper, and she had fourteen days.

It was in the cellar of the Praga that she did the arithmetic of the fourteen days, walking back over the bridge in the light of the middle of the afternoon of the fourth with the basket of the household on her arm and the city going on around her in the way that a city goes on in the last month before the thing that everybody has agreed not to name: and the arithmetic was that the district of the rising wanted, by the twenty-fifth of the month of July, the page of the paper of the Fall **Wotan** — the page with the map of the line of the river and the table of the rolling stock and the four dates — and that the page was in the steel box in the wall of the study of the house on the Bagatela, and that the steel box was behind a door of the steel of the pattern of the Organisation Todt of the year 1939 with a dial of the brass and the number 9-4-36, and that the woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw was the only person in the German administration of the city of Warsaw who was able to open it.

Fourteen days of the household of a general, in the fifth July of the war, in a city of a million people that had begun to count: the wives' table of the Thursday, the two shops of the German quarter, the tram, the tram, the tram; and in the middle of the fourteen days the thing that the woman of the household was doing at eleven o'clock in the night of every night of that fortnight, in a bath of the temperature of the hand, with the water running, with the window of the room of the bath closed and the blackout blind down, in the house of a general of the German army in the city of Warsaw, in the fifth year of the war.

She developed it in the month of the June, because the water is the only sound in a house of the German administration that a man of the German administration is able to hear through a door without looking up from what he is doing; and by the second week of July the technique of it was this: the film in the Minox at the beginning of the evening, so that the roll of it was in the camera and the camera was in the lining of the bag; the document on the towel on the lid of the closed seat, in the light of the two candles that stood on the shelf of the room with the glass of the household over the flame of them for the German administration of the city of Warsaw had issued the order of the blackout and the women of the houses had put the glass over the flame of them; the shutter of the Minox at the setting of the year 1941, the distance of the page at twenty-two centimetres, the hands of the woman of the household steady for the eleven seconds of the exposure; and the candle out, and the document back in the folder, and the folder back in the box, and the box shut, and the dial turned twice on the last number, and the operation of the eleven minutes finished before the water of the bath was cold, in the room of the bath of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, in the month of July of the year 1944.

On the eighteenth of July, in the evening, in the corridor of the first floor of the house on the Bagatela, in the seven seconds in which the corridor was empty, the captain of the ordnance of the district of Warsaw said, in Polish, at the volume of a man saying the time to no one at all:

"The twenty-fifth. Where?"

"Where I can, and it is going to be in the hands of the district by midnight, and it is not going to be in the hands of the district by way of you, and the reason for that is that the man who is in the corridor of the first floor of a house of a general of the German army at nine o'clock in the evening is a man of the German army, and the two of us have been standing in the same corridor for the fifteen months of the war, and there is not one officer of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw who has ever looked at the two of us in the same seven seconds."

He looked at her for four seconds, and then the boots of the Feldgendarmerie came along the corridor from the room of the household, and the captain of the ordnance of the district of Warsaw said: "Zu Befehl, gnädige Frau," and went down the stair.

---

And in the bed of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, in the fourteen days of the month of July 1944, the thing went on.

It went on in the dark, with the window shut and the blackout over it, in the heat of the Polish July, in the way it had gone on in the winter of the year 1943 and the spring of the year 1944 and the month of the June: the man of fifty-five and the woman of twenty-seven, and the apparatus of the possession of a man of the old school in a house of the east — the hand on the back of the neck, the weight, the orders of the voice in the dark, the long silence afterwards with the arm over the eyes. And in the fourteen days of it, which were the last fourteen days of the arrangement in the house on the Bagatela, she did the work the way she had done it for the two years of the war; and in the middle of the work of the seventh night of the fourteen, on the twelfth of July, in the dark, with the weight of a general of the German army on her, she understood the thing that she had been refusing to understand since the month of the March, and it was not a fact about him.

It was a fact about her.

She had begun, at some hour of some night of the month of the June, to be in the bed. Not in the manner of a woman of the profession of the bed, and not in the manner of a wife; in the manner that a person is in a room in which there is a person and a lamp and a war and a window with the blackout over it, which is to say: present, in her own body, with her attention, and taking from it the thing that the body of a person takes from a body that has decided to hold it.

It had not happened to her in Paris, in the four months of the arrangement; it had not happened to her in the first year in Warsaw; and the reason that it had happened in the fifth year of the war in a shuttered room of a house on the Bagatela was the arithmetic of the fifth year of the war, which was this: that there was no longer any part of the woman called Krystyna Stankiewiczówna left in the room; and the two women that the arrangement had made — the woman of the General and the woman of the district of the rising — had come to the end of the two of them, and what was left in the bed was a woman of twenty-seven years of age in the dark of a Polish July, with a man of fifty-five asleep on her arm, and there was nobody left in the room to tell her which of the two of them she was.

She lay in the dark for the night of the twelfth of July 1944 with her eyes open and the ceiling of the room of the first floor of the house on the Bagatela above her, and the city going quiet towards the curfew, and she thought the sentence that was the last sentence of the dark part of her life, and she thought it in Polish:

*In six days he goes to Kraków, and in nineteen days the city rises, and the page of the paper is in the box in the wall, and the box has a number of four and four and four, and my life has been a paper and a box and a number, and tonight there is a man asleep on my arm who has never once in five years asked me for anything but the truth, and I have given him the truth in the only form in which it has ever been possible to give it: the bed, the lamp, the window, the blackout, the woman.*

*And there is a man in the corridor of the first floor of this house for seven seconds of an evening who has asked me for nothing at all.*

The General came home on the eleventh of July in the blackest humour that she had seen in two years, which was saying something, because she had seen the great unhappiness of that man in a cold room in a chateau in the Loire with a dead woman's dress in her hands and a candle on the floor.

He came in at nine at night, and went past her in the hall without a word, and shut the study door, and was in there for two hours with a bottle and a telephone that did not ring; and at eleven he came out and stood in the drawing-room door and looked at her, and said:

"They are going to cancel the evacuation of the administration."

"They have evacuated the wives on the twenty-eighth."

"Because the wives are a different matter from the administration, and the administration is a different matter from the staff, and the staff is a different matter from the men in the line, and I have spent the evening being told by a General in Kraków that the movement of the civil administration out of the east is a movement that must be *made to look* like a movement of the civil administration, and not like a retreat, and that therefore it must be done slowly, and in the daylight, and with the flags." He put his hand on the door frame. "There is going to be a rising in this city."

She was standing by the piano.

"Say it again," she said, in her ordinary voice.

"There is going to be a rising in this city, and it is going to be in the first days of August, and there are going to be a hundred thousand Poles in the streets with rifles and bottles and a home-made mortar, and it has been put together under our noses in a city in which we have twenty thousand men and a card index of two hundred thousand names, and the reason that we know it is the reason that everybody in Warsaw has known it for the month of the June, which is that the thing has been decided in London, and London talks, and the population of this city talks, and my whole section has spent the spring writing reports about it." He laughed, or made the sound of a laugh. "There is going to be a rising, and the Generalgouvernement has seventeen days to prepare, and the line on the Vistula is to be held, and I have been ordered to make a district of a city into a fortress, and I am a colonel of intelligence with two hundred and eleven Poles in my offices and a safe in my study with a schedule in it, and I have been in the east for five years and I know exactly what a city is: a city is a thing in which two hundred thousand people are going to die very slowly, in the cellars, one street at a time."

He came across the room and took her by both arms, and he was drunk, and he was not drunk in the way she had seen him forty times; this was the other thing, the thing underneath, the man in the ditch at Modlin, the man who had written eleven letters to the mothers of the officers who had shot themselves in the Crimea and had put *duty* in the eleventh letter and posted it.

"I will tell you one thing, and then we will not speak of it again," he said. "There is a train on the twenty-eighth of July, the last of the trains for the civil administration, and I have a place on it for a woman with a foreign passport and a paper signed by the district that states that she is an employee of the district administration. I did not tell you. I had decided not to tell you. It is the only lie I have told you in two years, and I am telling you about it on the eleventh of July because in seventeen days I am not going to have any control over anything at all, not over a street, not over a train, not over a room, and I want it on the table between us now, at nine o'clock on the eleventh of July, while the two of us are still people."

She stood in an occupied drawing room in a house on the Bagatela with her hands at her sides, in the light of a green-shaded lamp, and looked at the man who had put a district into a steel box in a wall, and answered him.

"Friedrich," she said. "I am not going to the west on the twenty-eighth of July; and you have known that since May; and your asking me now is not for the place on the train at all. So put the paper in the drawer, and don't insult the two of us with the train; and if there is a thing you want to say to me instead of the thing about the train, then say it, and I will hear it, and it will be dark and it will not be the truth, but it will be something better than a railway ticket."

He was quiet for a long time.

Then he said: "Come into the bedroom."

And what he did with her that night, in the house on the Bagatela, was the last of the dark things between them, and she understood that while he was doing it, which was the worst of it: that it was not appetite, not possession, not the ceremony of the district; it was a man of fifty-five holding on to the one piece of his existence that had never once, in two years, lied to him about the war.

---

He came to her at three in the morning, which was not a thing he did; he slept through, always, on his back, arms at his sides, chin up, and when he woke he was a man who had been given a district, and not a man who had been dreaming.

But at three in the morning on the twelfth of July, in the house on the Bagatela, he put his hand on her shoulder in the dark, and she woke the way she had spent four years waking, which was not at all, and lay still with her eyes open and her breath going, and he said, in German, into the dark, in a voice that was not the voice of the day:

"Say something in Polish."

There was a silence in the room.

"Why?"

"Because I am lying here in the dark listening to the city and there is not one single Pole in this district who is not tonight thinking about the first of August, and I can hear it, the way a farmer hears weather, and I want to hear the sound of it out of a mouth that is telling me something that is not a report." His hand stayed on her shoulder. "One sentence. Whatever you like. The Polish of the Frenchwoman, which is what you gave me in the third month, in the car, on the way back from the chateau, when you were tired and you said *dobranoc* to the driver and did not know you had said it."

She lay in the dark with her eyes open.

She had learned Polish at the age of three in a house on the Mokotowska. She had learned it in the nursery, and in the garden, and at a piano, and at a school, and in a childhood in a city of a million people who had been, on the first of September 1939, the same city as her. It was the language in which her mother had said *Krystyna, you are not playing the second movement slowly enough*; the language in which she had been told, by a priest in a confessional in October 1943, the thing that would be required of her; the language in which her father had said his last sentence that had been written down, in a book, in the margin, in pencil. It was her and everything she had ever lost; and it was lying in a bed in a German house at three in the morning in July 1944 inside the body of a woman whose hands had been made to be the hands of Marion Herschlag.

And what she understood, in the dark, with the hand of a General of the intelligence of Warsaw on her shoulder, was that it was not going to be done to her.

Nobody was going to take the language out of her mouth for a German's pleasure. Not her, and not her mother, and not the boy at the market, and not the woman in the corridor of the Szucha with her eyes on the table.

So she said something, because a woman in her position said something, always, and lying in that bed was work; and what she said, in the dark, in a an entirely flat and foreign and cheerful voice, was a sentence out of a phrasebook that a Frenchwoman of the administration might have learned from a Polish cook.

"Soup is ready, sir. The tram is late today."

The General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw laughed in the dark, once, from the chest, the door closing.

"Rot," he said. "The whole of it is rot. Come here."

And he had her again, at three in the morning, in the dark, on the twelfth of July, in the fifth year of the war, and she did what she had done four hundred times, and it was nothing, and it was worse than nothing, and what she was doing in her head, all the way through it, was not arithmetic at all.

She was counting. She was counting in Polish, one number at a time, in the language she had given away in 1940, in a room in Kraków, to a small man with a bad heart; and she got to a hundred, and then she began again at the beginning; and she went on counting through it, and it was the only thing she had left that she had never once in four years handed to anybody.

At four she got up, and went down the corridor to the room that was hers, and put on the light, and sat on the edge of the bed in a nightdress with her hands in her lap.

And she said, out loud, to the room, in Polish, in her own voice, for the first time since a corridor in Paris in April 1943:

"Nineteenth of July. Three days with the General at Kraków. Nine, four, thirty-six. The photograph of Anneliese. The steel box in the wall of the study."

And then she turned out the light, and lay down on her back in the dark with her hands at her sides, and slept, for four hours, the way a soldier sleeps.
