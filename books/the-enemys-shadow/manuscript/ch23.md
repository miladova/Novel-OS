# Chapter 23: The Colonel's Bed

The first hour of the Rising of the city of Warsaw, in the streets around the house on the Bagatela, was not a battle.

It was a sound with no shape: the flat crack of small arms from four directions at once, none of them close; the long rattle of a machine gun somewhere behind the Ujazdowskie; the heavy, dull, double thud of something being fired that nobody in the Śródmieście had heard in five years except in the distance of a night; the bells of the churches of the whole city ringing and stopping and starting again; and the noise of a city of a million people opening its windows all at once.

She stood with her hand still on the brass of the front door and the glass of the fanlight at her feet in a white drift on the marble, and outside, on the stone, there was one knock again — two knocks, quick — and then a voice through the wood, low and even, in Polish:

"Otwórz. To ja."

She pulled the bolt back and opened the door eleven centimetres, and Phillip Brandt came in off the step and shot the bolt behind him with his elbow, and stood in the marble hall of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw with a leather map case under his left arm and a service pistol on his right hip, in a captain's tunic with the collar unhooked and his cap missing, breathing as though he had run four hundred metres, which he had.

"There is nobody in the street at the corner of the Polna," he said, in Polish, fast and flat. "There are three Germans of the guard of the Sicherheitsdienst in the doorway of the building on the other side of the avenue, and they have not got a telephone, and there is a Polish boy with a rifle on the flat roof of the house at the corner of the Krucza, and he has just fired four times at the street and hit nothing, and in about eleven minutes there is going to be a lorry of the military police coming up the avenue and it is going to be fired on from the roof and it is going to stop in front of this house, because this house has the only stone gate on this side of the street and there are windows on the first floor."

"You came through the street."

"I came through the yard of the building in the next street," said Phillip, "over the wall, through the garden of the furier's, past a gardener who was standing in the gravel of the yard behind the wall with a rake in his hand in the middle of a rising and who told me, in Polish, in the plainest voice of a man giving the time, that the Frau was in the house with the door bolted and the General at the Palace, and who then asked me whether I was for it or against it, and waited for the answer."

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him I was for it," said Phillip Brandt. "And then I climbed over the wall of the general's garden and got to the door of the general's house, which is the second time in the war that a Pole in a German uniform has climbed over the wall of that garden, and the first time was in April and there was nobody in the yard."

The house was dark: the blackout blind down in the small room, the hallway dim behind the shutters, the clock on the stair ticking in the middle of the noise like a small machine in a workshop. He looked at the glass on the floor at their feet for a moment.

"You are a captain of the Ordonnanzoffizier of the district of Warsaw," she said. "You have a general's order not to be in this house; you have a an army at the Palace at this hour; you have a war that is going to be judged to the last man in a room in Berlin in eleven years; and you have climbed over a wall to stand in a marble hall and tell a woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district that you are for it." She looked at him. "Say what you have come to say, and then go out of the garden before the lorry comes, because you are the only person in the world who has ever walked into my whole of a life at a door, and I have spent four years of my life making sure that does not happen twice."

He said: "Give me the paper."

"What?"

"The Wotan paper," said Phillip Brandt. "The the folder. You opened the safe on the twentieth of July, and the safe has been opened once since and it was by me on the twenty-ninth, when the General sent me into the study to fetch the register of the personnel of the district, and there was a folder of the Fall *Wotan* in the safe with five sheets in it, and there is a folder of the Fall *Wotan* in the suitcase of a woman of this house that is behind the boiler of a laundry in the yard of the building in the next street, and I have known about the suitcase since March, because I carried it from the corridor of the house to the garden gate on the fourteenth of May in the rain, and if the folder is in the laundry when the Germans burn this street, then the year is worth nothing at all, and if it is in the hands of the district of the Rising, then there is a thing in the war that has been done by a woman who will never be able to say it out loud." He put the map case on the hall table. "The district of the intelligence service of the Wehrmacht of the district of Warsaw is at this hour in the building of the command at the Blue Palace with the files of the district; the Generalgouvernement is at this hour in the building of the Governor in the Krakowskie Przedmieście with the files of the Governor; and the Sicherheitsdienst is going to burn its the office in the aleja Szucha in the night, which the Poles already know because the smoke of it has been over the city since Tuesday. There is going to be a German perimeter around the government district of the city by tomorrow evening, and the perimeter is going to be on the line of the Bagatela, and at that hour this house is going to be the house of the front line, and there is not one place in the city that has a German safe in it on the first of August that is going to hold anything by the fifth."

"Which is why I am here," she said, "and not in the car."

"Then the paper is not in the safe."

"The paper is not in the safe," said Krystyna Stankiewiczówna. "There is a page of the report in the hand of a boy with a bandage on his right hand, and there is the folder behind the boiler of a laundry in the yard of the building in the next street, and there is a copy of the last four sheets on a film in the lining of the bag that is standing on the chair at the bottom of the stair behind you, and there is a woman in this hall who is at the middle of the fifth year of a thing that no one is ever going to write down, and there is not going to be any more."

He turned and looked at the bag on the chair.

"Then it is in this house," he said. "The film is in this house, and the house is in the middle of the German perimeter, and the perimeter is going to be formed in the next twelve hours, and there is one thing in the world at this hour that a German perimeter does not search, and it is a German house with a General's flag on the gate."

She looked at him for a long moment in the dim hall, with the small arms going in the streets, and the clock on the stair.

"They are going to put troops in this house," she said.

"They are going to put troops in this house in the next four hours," said Phillip, "and they are going to put the telephone of the district in the study, and an observation post on the first floor, and the Feldgendarmerie is going to stand at the garden gate and drink the coffee, and the German army of the district of Warsaw is going to be in the drawing room of a General's woman, and the film is going to be in the lining of a bag, and the bag is going to be in the wardrobe of a woman, and the woman is going to be the only person in the house who is not going to be searched, and that, madame, is the plan."

---

They came at half past six.

There were eleven of them, of the third company of a battalion of the garrison, and they came up the avenue in a lorry that stopped at the corner of the Polna and was fired on twice from the roof of the house on the Krucza and gave the street three bursts of a submachine gun in reply; and the officer of them was a lieutenant of twenty-three from Kassel with a face like a boy's, and he came to the door of the house on the Bagatela and stood there with his cap in his hand and said, in the correct German of a cadet: that he had the order to occupy the stone houses on the line of the Bagatela, that the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw had been placed on the list of the buildings of the perimeter, and that he asked the pardon of the Frau Generalin.

She came down the marble stair in the grey dress, with her hair up, and stood at the bottom of it, and looked at him the way she had looked at a hundred officers of the district at a hundred tables in Paris and in Warsaw; and she said, in German, in the flat voice of a woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw:

"A lieutenant of the reserve of a battalion of the garrison is standing in the hall of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, in the middle of a rising, and is telling a woman of the household of the General that the house has been placed on a list. There is a telephone in the study of this house. There is a line to the Blue Palace. You will use that telephone in the next four minutes and you will speak to the office of the district of the intelligence service, and the man at that office will tell you the correct answer; and until he tells you, your men are going to stand in the garden and not one of them is going to be in the house."

It took four minutes and eleven seconds, and it worked, because it was the fifth year of the war and the German army still worked on paper even with a city on fire: the office of the district of the intelligence service in the Blue Palace confirmed that the house was on the list of the buildings of the perimeter and the troops were to occupy it, and the same office, in the same conversation, ordered that the study on the ground floor of the house was to be occupied by the office of the district, and that the rest of the house was to remain at the disposal of the household of the General, and that the Fliegeralarm cellar was to be prepared for the household and the troops.

She let them in at seven.

They came into the house the way a company of infantry comes into a house: eleven men and their equipment through a hall with a marble stair, and in eleven minutes the drawing room of the house on the Bagatela had a machine gun on the sideboard of 1851 and a telephone on the writing table and a map of the district of Warsaw on the wall where the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska had sat for sixty years in a chair that had come from her grandmother's house; and the boy-lieutenant from Kassel sat down on the sofa of the family and began, in the ordinary voice of a boy who had been trained well, to put his company's quarter in the middle of a Polish general's drawing room.

And at half past seven, in the middle of it, Ehlers came with the car.

He came through the garden gate with the smell of the street on him and a scratch on his left cheek and his cap on straight, and he stood in the hall among the eleven men of the garrison and looked at the house; and he said, in the flat voice of the driver of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw:

"Frau, the car is at the corner of the Polna and it is not able to come into the yard because there is a lorry of the third company of the garrison at the mouth of the yard. There is an order from the General of the district of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, of the first of August 1944 at three o'clock, that the Frau of the household of the General is to be in the car at five o'clock in the afternoon with her case; and the hour was five o'clock, and it is now half past seven, and there is no road out of this quarter of the city in any direction, and the avenue between here and the Wilanów is in the hands of the Poles since half past six, and there is going to be nothing at all in the streets of this city by tomorrow morning, and the order of the General of the district of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw is not able to be obeyed her or anywhere else."

"Ehlers."

"Yes, Frau."

"Did you go out with the car this afternoon?"

"I did not, Frau." He took the cap off. He was a driver of thirty-four with a girl's photograph in the flap of his map case and a street in Posen that did not exist, and his hands were the hands of a man who had been driving for four years without an accident. "At half past four this afternoon the yard of this house was full of the men of the guard of the General and the staff of the office of the General were putting the files of the office of the General into the car of the General, and I was told to drive the files of the office of the district of the intelligence service to the Blue Palace, and I drove the files to the Blue Palace at a quarter to five, and I came back through the fire of the Poles at the corner of the Nowy Świat, and there is a hole in the windscreen of the car of the General of the district which a man in this house is going to have to explain." He looked at her. "And there is one thing more, and I am going to say it now, and then I am going to the kitchen with the men of the garrison, because a driver of the Wehrmacht in a house of a General in the middle of a rising sits with the men and not with the household."

"Say it."

"The Frau is in the middle of the German perimeter of the city," said Ehlers. "And the men of the perimeter are going to be in this house in the morning, and the Poles of this quarter are going to be on the roofs of the houses at the corner of the Polna with rifles, and this house is on the corner, and there is not one window in it on the side of the street that is not going to have a man behind it by tomorrow. And the plan of the General of the district of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw for the household is a car, and the car has a hole in it, and there is no road." He put his cap back on. "The Frau is going to be here at the end, and the men in the kitchen know it, and I have not said a word to any of them, and I am not going to, and that is the report of the driver of the General of the district."

---

At eleven o'clock that night the telephone rang in the study.

She was in the small room with the blind down and the lamp unlit, and Phillip was on the first floor with the map case and the eleven men of the garrison were in the kitchen and the drawing room, and the door of the study had been shut since eight, and the boy-lieutenant from Kassel had put his telephone on the writing table in the drawing room with an operator sitting at it, and it had rung nine times in three hours; and the tenth time it rang there was no operator, because the operator was in the passage with the men, listening to the firing at the corner of the Krucza; and she heard the ring, and she went out and looked at the door of the study, and then she went in.

"Frau Generalin—" said the boy from Kassel.

"Es ist für mich," she said, and lifted the receiver, and heard, on the line from the Blue Palace, over the crackle of the district line and the noise of a distant firing like a wind in a chimney, the voice of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw.

"Marion."

"Friedrich."

"Are you in the house?"

"I am in the house."

"Are you alone?"

She turned her head and looked at the boy-lieutenant from Kassel, who was standing in the doorway of the drawing room with the telephone operator's cap behind him, and she said, in German, in the flat voice of the Frau of the household of a General: "The house is full of the men of the third company of the garrison, and the study has been occupied by the office of the district since seven o'clock, and your study smells of the soldiers, and the man from Kassel has put the machine gun on the sideboard of 1851, and there is a map of the district of the city on the wall of the drawing room of the Countess Jadwiga, and this afternoon there was a glass of the fanlight over the door that came down on the marble of the hall and there was nobody in the street to sweep it up."

There was a silence on the line of four seconds, and then the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw laughed: one short, dry, terrible laugh, the laugh of a man of fifty-five in the middle of the first night of the end of the eastern front.

"I am in a room on the first floor of the Blue Palace with eleven officers and a map," he said. "The Governor has gone to the command post at the Bruhl Palace with the garrison of the SS and the police, and the district of the intelligence service has the sector between the Saxon Garden and the Vistula, and there is one of the eleven officers and he is weeping at the window and there is nothing at all in the room that anybody is able to say to him, because at four o'clock this afternoon the garrison of the city of Warsaw was at the alert and the intelligence service of the district knew the hour to the minute, and there was not one man in Berlin who would listen to it." He paused. "The Governor has given the order for the city. There is a word in the order, and the word is *Banditen*, and it is the word of 1943, and it is in the file of the district, and I have read the paper at half past nine with the staff of the district in the room, and the paper has the signature of the SS and the police and the civilian administration of the district and it does not have the signature of the Wehrmacht of the district of Warsaw, and there is one man in this building at this the hour who is able to refuse to put his name on it, and that is the general of the intelligence service of the district, and I have refused, in the room, at the table, in front of the staff, and the room has been quiet for a minute."

"Friedrich."

"Yes."

"What have you done?"

"I have done the thing that a general of the intelligence service of a district does at the beginning of the last week of the east," he said, "and it is nothing at all. There is going to be a report of the district of the intelligence service of Warsaw for the month of August 1944, and it is going to be written by the office of the district, and there is not going to be one of the sentences of it in my hand, and the men of the office are going to be writing it in September in a village in Silesia, and the thing that I have done tonight is to refuse a signature on a paper that no one in the history of Germany is ever going to read." There was another silence, longer. "I want to ask you a thing. It is the last thing that I am going to ask, and it is not about the house. Do you know what this city is doing tonight?"

"No."

"Neither do I," said the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw. "There are a hundred thousand men in the streets of this city with the rifles of 1939 in their hands, and there is not one aeroplane and not one tank and not one gun in it, and the German army of the east is at the Vistula, and there is going to be a river of blood in the next three weeks, and there is not one man in my building who is able to say the reason that it is happening. And I am a German general of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw, and I have spent the last four years of the war in the city that is on fire, and there is one person in the world who has ever said the truth to my face, and she is in the house on the Bagatela with a machine gun on the sideboard of the family of her childhood, and her name is Marion Herschlag, and she has a face that I have been looking at for the two years of the war, and there is a thing about that face that I have never once been able to say out loud, and I am going to say it now on a telephone at eleven o'clock on the first night, and then I am going to ring off the line and go down the stairs to the map."

"Say it."

"Come home," said the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and the telephone line, which had been hissing with the fire of the streets for eleven minutes, went dead.

---

The second of August came up grey and thick with smoke, and by the second of August the quarter around the house on the Bagatela was a fortress.

It had happened in the night, the way these things happen: eleven lorries and two cars of the military police and a company of the engineers had come up the avenue at three in the morning and had gone into the big insurance building at the top of the Polna, and at four o'clock there were wire and paving stones and a machine gun nest at the corner of the Bagatela and the avenue, and at five there were men of the garrison lying in the passage of the house of the furier's and two of them on the roof with rifles; and at six, when she came down into the hall with her hair up, there were Polish corpses in the road at the corner of the Novum, four of them, in the ordinary clothes of the boys of the quarter, and one of them was barefoot.

She stood at the window of the landing of the first floor with the lace held back and looked at it for a long time, and Phillip Brandt stood at her left shoulder with his hands behind his back and looked at it with her, and neither of them said a word, because there were five men of the garrison on the stair; and after eleven minutes he said, in German, in a voice for a corridor:

"Die Leitung zum Kommando ist unterbrochen, Frau Generalin."

And then, half an hour later, in the passage to the kitchen, in the four seconds in which the house was empty, he said, in Polish, in a voice that had no breath in it at all:

"There is a paper in the Wola. My mother's sister is in the Wola, and there are four hundred thousand people in that district, and there has been a German order of the night in the district of the command, and the men of the garrison of the city who are not in the perimeter are going there, and I have read the order this morning in the study of the district and it is signed by the SS and the police and it is not signed by the Wehrmacht of the district, and there is one man in the German administration of this city who refused to put his name on the paper, and he did it in a room on the first floor of the Blue Palace at nine o'clock last night, in front of the staff." He looked at her. "Do you know who that man is?"

"I know who he is," said Krystyna Stankiewiczówna.

"There is a thing that is going to happen in the Wola in the next week," he said, "and it is not a thing of the war of the armies, and it is going to be done on the floor of a room and in the air of a district of a city with a population of a quarter in it, and there is nothing at all that a captain of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw is able to do about the thing except to be in one room in the house on the Bagatela next to a woman in a grey dress, and to tell her the truth in the seven seconds in which there is no one in the passage, because in the year in this house there has never once been a minute in which the two of us were able to be in the same room and to say a thing to each other."

And then the boots of the Feldgendarmerie came back along the passage with a bucket and a an empty can, and Phillip Brandt stepped back from her by half a metre and said, in German: "Zu Befehl, Frau Generalin," and went down the passage with his map case under his arm.

---

By the evening of the second of August the perimeter had closed around the German quarter of the centre of the city, and the house on the Bagatela had become one of the three houses at the corner of the government district that were full of the German garrison of the city of Warsaw.

There were two officers in the house and one of them was sick, and there were twenty-nine men, and there were four machine guns, and there was a telephone that worked when the Poles were not firing at the line, and there was a kitchen that had a Polish cook in it by the evening of the third, an old woman who had come in through the garden wall with a bag of potatoes and who had stayed, because the house of the furier's had burned on the second of August at noon and the servants of the German quarter of the city had been looking for a floor and a stove all through that night.

At four in the afternoon of the third, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna went up to the roof.

It was possible, in the house on the Bagatela, to get to the roof of the building through a hatch at the head of the back stairs; and the men of the garrison had been up there in the morning, and the boy-lieutenant from Kassel had put a man on it with a pair of field glasses because the roof of the house was the highest point of the quarter on the southern side, and the man with the field glasses had gone down at three for his dinner.

She stood on the roof of a house in the city of her birth on the third of August 1944 and saw her whole city on fire.

There is nothing in the world to be said about it that has not been said, and she never afterwards said anything about it to any human being; and what she saw, standing on the flat roof of the house with the lead of the gutter hot under her shoes, was:

The smoke. It was not one smoke; the city had six of them, each of its own district, and they stood up over the flat of the roofs in the white August light in columns that were yellow at the bottom and grey and brown at the top, and they leaned south-west in the wind of the afternoon, and the sky over the Vistula was one vast, smooth, brass-coloured haze with the sun in it.

The Wola, which was not visible from the Bagatela but whose smoke was: a brown-black curtain at the western edge of the city, standing up straight in the still air, with a red foot to it, and it standing over the district of the city where four hundred thousand people lived, and standing over it without moving for the afternoon, because the fires of the Wola were not fires of houses: they were the fires of a district of a city that had been raked into the streets by the troops of the SS and the police with their hands.

The Old Town, which was in the north: a tower of the smoke over the cathedral; and the Nowy Świat, which was on the next street but one; and the Śródmieście, in the middle, where the smoke was not the smoke of a district but of a hundred different fires in a hundred different streets, some of them standing straight up out of a single house, and them rising and falling in the day as the wind changed.

The Poles on the roofs of the buildings of the quarter, who were firing at the German positions with rifles; and the German positions, which were firing at them; and between the two, on the ground, at a distance of two hundred metres from the roof of the house on the Bagatela, in the afternoon light of the third of August, a girl of about sixteen in a white blouse coming along the middle of the street with a bucket in each hand and a white cloth on her left arm, in the middle of the firing, at a walking pace, delivering the water of the city of Warsaw.

And on the roof, in the middle of the smoke, alone, with the two buckets going along the street below her and her city in its last week, a woman of twenty-seven stood in a grey dress with her hands flat on the hot lead of the gutter and said the thing that she had never once said out loud in the four years, in Polish, to the smoke, in the ordinary voice of a woman of the city of Warsaw:

"Warszawa."

---

The General came to the house on the night of the fourth of August.

He came in a car with two motorcycles in front of it and a second car behind it, in the dark, with the quarter under the fire of the rifles of the roofs, and the gate of the garden was opened for the car by the sentry of the guard of the district of the intelligence service, and the car came up the gravel of the garden path three metres from the wall of the house and stopped under the windows of the drawing room with the mud of the south of the city on it; and Phillip Brandt was standing at the head of the marble stair in a tunic with the collar hooked, with the boy-lieutenant from Kassel at the bottom of the stair and four men of the third company of the garrison in the passage and all of them standing straight.

Friedrich von Reuth got out of the car in the gravel of the garden of his own house and stood for a moment in the light of the open door with his cap in his hand, and looked at the house with the flat exhausted attention of a man who has been in a room with a map for seventy-two hours; and there was the dust of plaster on the green of his collar and a small tear in the sleeve of his tunic at the elbow, and his face was grey with the grey that comes off a wall in a bombardment.

"Herr General." The boy from Kassel saluted.

"Lieutenant. Report."

"The third company of the third battalion of the garrison, twenty-nine men, four machine guns, occupying the house of the General of the district since the first of August at nineteen hundred hours, in the position of the line of the Bagatela, with one observation post on the roof, and two men at the garden gate, and the street under the fire of the Poles from the roof of the building at the corner of the Polna, and the house of the furier's burned on the second, and the household of the General is in the small room of the ground floor and the household of the burgher of the opposite house came through the garden wall on the third."

"Casualties."

"One man, Herr General, of the party of the roof, wounded in the arm by the splinters of the parapet on the second."

"The Frau Generalin."

"At the disposal of the General, and the household of the General is at the disposal of the command," said the boy of twenty-three, and it was, in the five years of the war, the most German and the most decent sentence that anybody in that house had produced; and then he stood aside, and the men of the third company who were in the passage of the ground floor came to attention as well as men come to attention in a passage with rifles and cooking pots and a bucket, and the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw walked through the middle of them and went up the marble stair.

He stopped at the head of the stair.

"Marion."

"I am here."

"Send the men to the kitchen," he said, to the house, in the flat voice of the district of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw, "and give the lieutenant the house on the ground floor for the night, and let nobody be on the first floor above the landing, and let the telephone be on the table in the hall and the calls be brought up by the orderly, and there is going to be no one in the garden after the eleven o'clock, and if the Poles come over the wall of the yard in the night the house is to be at the windows of the ground floor and nobody is to be on the stair." He turned and looked at her. "I have not slept for four nights, and I am going to be in this house until five o'clock in the morning, and there is not one other house in the city of Warsaw in which a general of the German army is able to be, and there is not one other person in this city of a million people whom I am able to be in a house with."

He went up the rest of the stair.

---

The room on the first floor of the house on the Bagatela had its windows shut and blinded, and the heat of the day of the fourth of August was standing in it at midnight like a person in a doorway; and the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw took off his tunic and hung it on the back of the chair, and put his belt and his pistol on the table beside the water jug, and sat down on the edge of the bed of the room in his shirt with his hands hanging between his knees and his back bent, and stayed like that without saying anything for four minutes while a woman of the household of the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw stood in the dark on the other side of the room and watched him.

"At four o'clock this morning," he said, to the floor, "the battalions of the police and the SS of the district go into the district of the Wola of this city, and there is an order with a number in the office of the Governor at this hour, and the battalions have been given the order, and there is not one line of the order in the hand of the Wehrmacht of the district, and there is not one general of the Wehrmacht of Warsaw who is able to say in the history of the war that he did not know what the order was for." He looked at his hands. "I have been in the Eastern Front for two years, and I have seen the villages of the Ukraine in the winter of 1941 and the things that the formations of the army did in that winter, and there is not one man who was in the east who is able to say that the army of Germany has clean hands, and there was a time in my life when I was able to look at the morning of the war and say to myself a sentence about the things that I was not able to prevent, and I have said that sentence in a room in this city at the hour of 10 o'clock in the evening of the first of August with the staff of the district in the room, and I have refused to put my name on a paper, and there is not one man in Berlin who is ever going to read the record of that refusal." He straightened, and looked at her. "And I have not done it for a principle, Marion. I have done it because there is a woman of France in a house on the Bagatela in Warsaw, and I have known since the first night of the rising that the investigation of this district is going to come to this house, and I have refused the signature in a room in front of the staff of the district in order to be able to say one thing on the day when they come to this house with a paper: that the general of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw is a man who has refused it, and that a general who has refused it is a general who is able to be a witness, and a witness is the only thing in the criminal administration of Germany that a policeman with a file is not able to put in a ground." He looked at the room. "That is the sentence. I have spent my life in the army, and it is the only piece of arithmetic that I have ever been able to do, and I have done it for a woman of Alsace who has been in my house for two years, and there is not one man in the war who is ever going to be told it."

She came across the room and stood in front of him in the dark.

"Friedrich."

"There is a question," he said, "and I am going to ask it once, and I am never going to ask it again, and it is not the question of a general, and it is not the question of a policeman, and there is not going to be a single word of the answer in the file of the district. Do you know why I have been able to spend two years in a house in this city with a woman and never once to be in a doubt?"

"No."

"Because the world has been in doubt," said Friedrich von Reuth, "and there is one object in it that has not been. There is a room in a house on this street with a woman in a chair by the window in the evening with a lamp turned low, and there is a man in the war who is able to come to the door of that room from the district of the command and know that the object in the room is in the room, and I have been in the war for five years with a war of a hundred million men around me, and I have had one object." He took her hand off his shoulder and held it in the dark. "That is the question, and the question is: is there any piece of that, in the two years, that has been wrong?"

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the dark of the room on the first floor of the house on the Bagatela at half past midnight on the fifth of August 1944, with the hand of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw in her own hand, and did the arithmetic for the last time in the arrangement; and at the end of it there was one item in the column, and the item was not a lie and was not the truth, and she said it in French, in the flat, even voice of a woman of the avenue Henri-Martin, in the dark, in a house in the city of Warsaw with a war in the street:

"There has never been anything wrong in this room, Friedrich. Not one thing."

---

There was no tenderness in the bed of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw on the fifth of August 1944, and there had never been, in the two years, any tenderness in the four hundred nights of it; there was a man of fifty-five who had refused to put his name on a paper and had come to the only room in the world in which he had ever been a man and not an instrument; and there was the weight of it, and the dark, and the heat of a Polish August standing in a shuttered room with the war in the street, and the sound of the machine gun of the corner of the Polna coming and going beyond the wall, and behind the blackout blind the light of the fire of a district of a city of four hundred thousand people on the underside of the sky.

She took it, the way she had taken it four hundred times: with her face in the bend of his neck and her hand flat on the back of his shoulder, and her mind working the way it had been trained in a room in Kraków to work in a bed, on the level above the body, filing the evening: the ninety-five days since the November, the three bundles of the safe, the paper of the card and the pencil sentence on the back of it, the page of the eleven surnames in the floor of the laundry, the boy with the bandage on his right arm, the five sheets of the Fall Wotan in the case in the third door on the left, the man in the passage with the pistol down his thigh, the plan and the number of the day.

And at some hour of that night, in the middle of it, in the dark of a shuttered room in a house in a burning city, the thing that she had guarded in herself for four years and had written down in the four rules of a room in Kraków broke, and it broke without any noise at all, and she did not notice it at the moment of it: what she noticed, afterwards, was that the face that had been in her head was the face of the man in the passage of the house with the dust of a wall in his hair, and that the hand that she was holding was the hand of a general of the German army, and that her own body, in the middle of the second year of the arrangement, had been for a minute in the room, which was the thing that no one had ever told her in a room in Kraków was able to happen to a woman who had been used four hundred times by an instrument of the war.

She lay in the dark afterwards with the weight of a sleeping man of fifty-five on the right side of her body and the heat of the night of the fifth of August on the room, and looked at the ceiling and thought the sentence that was the last of the first part of her life, and she thought it in Polish, in the dark, with the city of her birth going up in a smoke of a hundred thousand people beyond the blackout blind:

*Rule Four is dead. And I am not an instrument any more; I am a woman in a war, and there is a man in this house asleep on my arm, and there is a man in the passage of this house with the dust of a wall in his hair, and there is a city of a million people in the street, and I have no idea at all what I am going to do, and I am not going to be able to ask anybody, and there is nobody left in the world who is able to tell me.*
