# Chapter 6: The Weight of Paper

The Colonel went to Berlin on the first of April and was away for four days, and for four days the apartment at 12, avenue Henri-Martin belonged to Marion Herschlag and a fifty-seven-year-old man with two children in a photograph.

Kleist did not know what to do with a house in which the master was absent. He polished things. He took the silver out and looked at it and put it back. He brought the tray at seven exactly as he did when the Colonel was there, and stood in the doorway with his cap in both hands, and Marion understood, the first morning, that the tray was not for the master, who ate nothing but dry toast in a flat in Berlin, but for the orderly, who needed the day to have a shape.

The other thing Kleist did was to talk about his son.

His name was Heinrich. He was twenty-four and he worked in a graves-registration office at Smolensk, and his job was to write down the names of German soldiers out of the identity discs recovered by the burial commandos, and to match them against the lists, and to send the numbered, printed, official notifications to families in Bremen and Kassel and Breslau. Kleist had never in his life said the word "graves". He said *the office*. He said, *Heinrich has been transferred to a position in an office*, and it was the truth, and it was also that a boy of twenty-four had been given the job of writing the names of his own generation into a ledger, in a country where the ground was frozen nine months of the year.

"He writes that the typewriters are good," Kleist said, on the second morning, standing by the sideboard with his hands full of plate. "Model of 1938. Almost new. He says they have a *Kollege* from Stuttgart who was a violinist."

"Then he is well."

"He is well." He polished a spoon. "Fräulein. May I say something to you."

"You may."

"When the Oberst goes away, in this apartment, I have to decide where I am standing." He turned the spoon over and looked at his own face in it. "I have decided to stand here, in the dining room. If the Fräulein requires anything in the night, that is where I am."

At ten o'clock that night she understood what he had done.

She came down the corridor in her dressing gown, and the light in the dining room was on, and Kleist was sitting at the table in his shirtsleeves with the two photographs of his children in front of him and a book, and when he heard her feet he stood up so fast the chair went over.

"Herr Kleist."

"Gnädige Frau."

"You have been standing in the dining room since the evening of the first," she said. "For three nights. On a chair that does not belong to you, on a floor that is cold, in a house in which the man who pays you is not present."

"It is nothing."

"It is it, and it is more than I am able to carry." She stood in the doorway of the dining room in a dressing gown, and there was a thing she had not said to another human being since she had arrived in Paris, and she said it, in the flat, unpremeditated, unrehearsed voice of a woman giving an order to a servant, because there was no other way to say it that he would be able to accept: "I do not sleep either. If you are going to be awake in my house, sit in the kitchen, where there is a fire, and leave the door open."

He looked at her for a moment with an expression she could not read at all; and then he righted the chair, and gathered up the photographs, and went down the corridor to the kitchen, and left the door open, and she went back to bed and lay in the dark with the door of her room open too, and across the flat there was a small bar of yellow light on the boards, and it was the first time since Kraków that she had slept more than four hours together.

She thought about it in the morning, and understood that she had done a very stupid thing, and that the stupidity was not in trusting him; it was in the fact that in a house that belonged to a colonel of the Abwehr, and in a corridor between two open doors, she had, for one night, been a person.

---

The safe was in the study, behind the photograph of Anneliese von Reuth in her garden in 1931, and the study was the one room in the flat with a good lock on the door and a heavy curtain at the window, and it was the room in which Oberst von Reuth sat in the evenings in a leather chair and read reports with a pencil in his hand.

She had been in it eleven times in five months, all of them legitimately: to fetch a book, to take him his cap, to sit in the other chair while he read aloud a paragraph from a Berlin newspaper that had made him laugh. She had never once been in it alone, and he had never once, in five months, been in it while she was in the flat and not in it with her; and so the room had, in her private map of the apartment, exactly the status of the locked room in a fairy tale.

On the night of the second of April, with the Colonel in Berlin and Kleist asleep in the kitchen with the door open, she did the arithmetic three times and then did it.

Barefoot, in a nightdress, with the duplicate key in her left hand and the camera in her right, with the door of the study closed behind her and a candle not lit — she did it in the dark by the window, letting the blackout curtain back from the frame by one finger's width, which gave her a grey stripe of Paris night the width of a knife, no more: enough to find the outline of things, not enough to read.

The photograph came off the wall with a scrape of plaster. Behind it, the steel door of a Mauer, pattern three, 1941, the size of a briefcase, set flush into the plaster of a wall in a room that had been built in 1904.

The key went in. It did not go in the way keys go in the films, with a click and a triumphant little music. It went in stiffly, half a millimetre at a time, and her wrist knew after four millimetres what she had spent three weeks and a wax cake and a stranger's courage in the Auvergne to buy: that Monsieur Kahn's nephew was a very good workman, and that the lock was a Mauer, and that a Mauer of 1941, pattern three, has five levers, and that a key cut from a wax impression of a bit in a candle's worth of light will open four of them.

The fifth gave.

She stood in the dark with her hand on the door of the safe and her forehead against the cold wall for exactly four breaths, and then she opened it and began.

---

She did not take papers. That was the discipline, and it was the thing she had been taught in Kraków in a room with a bad fire, by a small man with a bad heart who had once sold books on the Nowy Świat and who was at that moment asleep, or not asleep, three kilometres away in the fifth arrondissement: *you do not steal documents, my little rabbit, because documents are counted. You steal light.*

A camera with a flash is a grenade. A camera without a flash needs one and a half seconds of exposure in which the hand, the paper and the lens must be immobile, and there is no tripod in the world that fits into a nightdress; and so she did it the way it had to be done, which was on the floor, on her side, with the paper flat on the boards and the little camera braced between two folded corners of the Parquet, exhaling slowly, holding, and counting.

There were eleven papers in the safe. She did them in the order of a woman who intends to be interrupted: the smallest first, the ones that could be lifted with two fingers; then the thin file; then the two big folded maps, which she did not photograph, because two big folded maps are not a page, they are a problem, and the problem was for Warsaw.

It took her ninety-one minutes.

She learned, in those ninety-one minutes, four things that would govern the rest of her life.

The first was that the III b registry was not the paper of a list-maker but of a cartographer. There were forty pages of it, and the names on it — French, Belgian, Dutch, Polish — were laid out not by date and not by section but by *place*: a page per city, each page with a grid drawn by hand over a map, each grid square numbered, each name written into its square in three inks, meaning three separate hands, meaning three separate visits. It was, she understood with a cold, spreading horror that had nothing to do with her own danger, a map of the underground of western Europe, drawn by someone who had been *inside* it.

The second was a single page in a folder of brown card, typed, stamped, with a signature she could not read, which was headed *Fall "Wotan"*, and which was not a plan for a fortress or a river line: it was a schedule. It listed, in columns, eleven towns east of the Vistula with the railway junctions beside them, and against each town a figure, and the figures were not men and they were not guns; they were *dates*, and the last date in the right-hand column was the eighth of August 1944, and under the table there was one sentence, in pencil, which she read twice to be sure: *Die Linie ist bis zum Datum ununterbrochen zu halten. Kein Rückzug ohne Befehl des Führers.*

The third was a personnel file in a folder of the same brown card, and it was thin, and it was the file of Hauptmann Phillip Brandt, born Warsaw 1915, mother Jadwiga Brandt, née Zaleska, deceased 1937, father Oberst a.D. Konrad Brandt, deceased 1939; schools: Warsaw, German-language, then the Technical University; the Polish army, evidently, in the autumn of 1939 — there was a gap of seven weeks in the autumn of 1939 in the file, and the words *in Ordnung* written across the gap in two different hands, and above them, in pencil, the letters the puzzle-makers use; and there, at the bottom, attached with a metal clip, was a single photograph, four centimetres by three, of a boy of nineteen in a thin shirt standing in front of a glasshouse, holding a closed book.

She looked at that photograph for a long time on the floor of the study of a colonel of the Abwehr, in the dark of the second of April, nineteen forty-three.

The fourth thing she learned was about herself, and it came upon her in the eleventh minute of the second hour, when she had finished and the papers were back in the safe and the safe was shut and the key was out, and she was standing in the middle of the room with the film in her fist and her body shaking in the aftermath the way it always did; and what she understood was that the shaking had come earlier this time, and had lasted longer, and that when the shaking had been at its worst she had stood in the dark of a locked room in a stolen flat with her eyes closed and had said, twice, without any sound, a name.

She had said: *Phillip.*

---

---

She met Lis on the third of April at four in the afternoon, in the Jardin des Plantes, in the rain, between the two long glasshouses, where a man with a bad heart could stand under an umbrella and look at the skeleton of a whale through a wet window and be, for eleven minutes, exactly as invisible as a retired schoolmaster.

"You are late," he said.

"I am being careful."

"That is different from being late, and you are both." He did not turn his head. In the glass the whale hung above them with its ribs like the ribs of a shed. "The milliner?"

"Taken yesterday morning. They took four boxes of paper and the coffee-machine, and there is a boy of fourteen on the street outside her door, and the notice on the door is in a hand that is not hers. She got a call out to me last night — one word, the wrong language — and then the line went."

Lis was quiet for a while. He had a way of receiving bad news that she had never seen in another human being: he took it the way a man takes a bill at a restaurant, by reading it, twice, before he says anything at all.

"Then it is finished here," he said. "Not the milliner. Everything. When they take the woman who runs the hats, they have the roads, and the roads are the trade."

"How long?"

"Nine days. A fortnight." He moved the umbrella to his other hand; his fingers were going white on the shaft. "They have a card index, and when they have a card index the arrests come in the order of the alphabet, because they are clerks, and the clerks work in the order of the alphabet; and there is a woman in Modane and a man in Annecy and a boy in Lyon who are in the same index as me, and one of them will be looked at by a man with a lamp, sooner or later, and will be told that his family is going to be deported, and after that he will be very, very helpful." He looked at her sideways for the first time. "You have the film."

"I have the film."

"Then do not give it to me." He said it flatly, and before she could answer: "Everything that comes to me now comes to me through a door that has four men on the other side of it by Tuesday. The film is worth more than the rest of this city's paper, and it is going to Warsaw in a coat, and I am going to tell you how, and then I am going to ask you for the last thing I shall ever ask you."

"Lis."

"Listen." His voice did not rise, but something under it did. "The registry. The forty pages, the grids, the three inks. Do you understand what you have photographed?"

"Names in squares."

"A map of us," he said. "Somewhere in the west there is an office where a man keeps a map of the Polish underground in France in forty pages, and he does not get that from interrogation, because interrogation gives you a name and a room and a chair, and this is *structure*. A grid per city means that somebody has been drawing our organisation the way a surveyor draws a hillside, from the inside, over years. There is a man at the top of that map, and he is ours, and he has been ours since 1940, and I do not know his face and I have never shaken his hand, because men like that never come *in*; they only go *out*; and when the film reaches Warsaw, the first thing they will do there is put that page under a glass and find him."

"Or her."

"Or her." He smiled, without any pleasure. "You are learning."

The rain came off the roof of the glasshouse. On the other side of the glass two hundred orchids of a dead republic stood in wet light; and sixteen metres away, on a bench, a German soldier sat with his arm round a girl of nineteen who was laughing at something, in French, too loudly, because the Parkordnung permitted it, and a man of forty in a mackintosh was walking his dog slowly along the front of the enclosure and looking at the small world of the Jardin des Plantes without appearing to look at any of it.

"The last thing I shall ask you," said Lis.

"Ask it."

"When you are in Warsaw, you are to do nothing."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"You will be inside the district staff," he said. "There is a colonel who will have papers in his house and a safe in his office and a General above him who says everything twice. You will be able to take half the German intelligence for Poland out of a room in the middle of the night, and every month you are in that city you will be offered a moment in which it is *possible*, and I want you to promise me that you will not take it."

"That is a stupid promise."

"It is arithmetic." He was very calm; his hand on the umbrella was no longer white, and she understood that he had decided something in the last minute. "A courier cannot be replaced. A woman inside a colonel's house cannot be replaced. A film that goes out three times a year is worth more than a film that goes out in April and a corpse in a cellar in May. You will be in Warsaw for a year, and the men who run you there will be hungry, because Warsaw is where it is going to happen, and they will ask you for things, and I am telling you now, while I am still alive to say it, that when they ask you, you may say no."

"Lis."

"Say it."

She stood in the rain in the Jardin des Plantes with her hand in her pocket on a hatpin and her arm against the place where the film was, and she said it, because he had known her father, and because he had taught her everything she knew, and because she was never going to see him again and both of them knew it.

"I will do nothing in Warsaw until I am told to."

"Good." He handed her the umbrella. "Keep this. It was my sister's and it is a bad umbrella, but a woman with a good umbrella in this city is a woman who is noticed, and you have enough of that." He put his hat on and pulled the brim down, and then he did the thing she remembered at odd moments for the rest of her life: he put out his hand as though to shake hers the way he might have done at a bank, and then did not, and instead he touched two fingers to the brim of his hat, exactly the way he had touched it in her father's library in 1938, when he had come to argue about the Germans and had lost the argument.

"Your father's daughter," he said, "should have been born a boy."

"Then he would have been lonely," said Krystyna Stankiewiczówna. "There would have been nobody left to be a girl."

And Lis walked away along the front of the glasshouse with his hands clasped behind him, a small bald man in a wet hat at a quarter past four in the afternoon, and was at the end of the enclosure, and then out of the gate; and she stood under his sister's umbrella with the orchids behind her, and she did not follow him, and she did not look at the mackintosh, and she did not cry; and eleven days later she learned, from a paragraph in a German newspaper in Warsaw, that a Polish bookseller of sixty-two had been arrested on the Boulevard Saint-Michel and had died in the rue des Saussaies of a heart that had been of no interest to anyone for twenty years.

He came to Paris on the fourth of April, from Nancy, on the morning train, and she did not know he was in the building until she walked into the archive room on the second floor of the Majestic at eleven o'clock to collect a box of the Colonel's papers that had been sent over from the map section.

The archive room had a window with a wire mesh in it and eight thousand files, and he was standing at the far end of it, in a captain's uniform with his tunic off and his sleeves rolled to the elbow, with a file open in his hands.

He put the file down.

"Madame Herschlag."

"Hauptmann."

"The Colonel's papers for the Warsaw posting." He indicated four grey boxes on the table with a movement of his chin. "I have been through them this morning. There is nothing in them but the usual: requisition, discipline, three letters from a General about a horse. I am told you are to take the first of them to the house in Warsaw with your own luggage, because the Colonel will not have his notes in the baggage car."

"That is what he told me on the telephone."

"It is not correct procedure." He said it without any particular expression, and turned a page. "Baggage is searched at the frontier by the customs of the Generalgouvernement, and a captain's boxes are opened by a sergeant who has been told the boxes are private, and a general's boxes are opened by nobody. But the Colonel is a colonel, and colonels are permitted one irregularity a year, and it seems that his is you."

She stood at the end of the table with her gloves in her hand and looked at him, and the room was very quiet, and outside the mesh window there was a motorcycle starting in the courtyard and a man laughing somewhere below.

"You have an opinion about me," she said.

"I have an observation." He closed the file. "Officers' wives do not travel with the boxes. Officers' women travel with the boxes, and it is always noticed, and it is always forgiven, and it is always written down. You will be written down at the frontier as *Begleitung*, which is a word that means two things, and the man who writes it will look at you the way all of them look at you." He put the file on the table and began to button his cuff. "That is all. It is not my business. I am merely the man who carries the Colonel's papers."

"Then carry them."

"I intend to."

She should have left. She had been in the room for three minutes and she should have left in the first one, and she had a reason to stay that had nothing to do with her profession, and the reason was that there was something in the room that was not yet finished, and she was going to have to look at it to know what it was.

She looked.

He was standing at the table with his cuff half-buttoned, and he had stopped moving, and there was, on the whole surface of him, none of the machinery of a German officer. His hands were still. He was looking at the four grey boxes and not at her. And she saw, in the way he stood, that he had been standing in that room for some time before she came; that he had opened those boxes and gone through them one by one; and that the thing he had been looking for in them was not his colonel's requisitions and not his colonel's horse.

"You have been through them twice," she said.

"I have a great deal of time."

"You have been through them twice, and you have not been reading the papers, because the papers were laid back in the boxes in the order you found them, with the corners facing left, and a man who reads a file turns the corners." She put on one glove. "You were looking for something else. And now you are looking at me, Hauptmann, and you have been looking at me for five minutes with the face of a man who has a question he has decided not to ask, and I have decided to tell you the thing that every woman in this building would tell you, which is that I am nobody's business, and my face is my own."

He looked at her then. It was the third time.

It was not the look of a man to a woman, and it was not the look of an officer to his colonel's mistress, and it was not even the look of one Pole to another in a city of the enemy; and she had spent three years learning the whole catalogue of looks and she did not have a name for this one, and she stood in the middle of the archive room of the Hôtel Majestic with one glove on and one in her hand and understood that in another second she was going to do something irreparable.

He said, in Polish, without any change of expression, in a voice pitched for the two of them and no one else, in the exact accent of a man who had learned the language in the courtyards of a city that no longer existed:

"*Jesteś bardzo zmęczona.*"

You are very tired.

And then he picked up the file and walked out of the room.

She stood alone in the archive room for four minutes, holding one glove, and then she put it on and went down to the car, and Ehlers drove her home through the wet April streets of the sixteenth arrondissement, and she sat in the back with her hands folded and looked out of the window at the whole grey machine of the city, and did not cry, and did not speak, and did not once, in the four kilometres, think about the eleven papers in the safe.

---

There were two calls from Berlin that evening, one at six and one at nine.

The first was the Colonel, and he was in the middle of a dinner and could not talk; he told her that the posting was confirmed, that they were to leave on the ninth, and that Kleist would travel with the household goods and Ehlers with the car, and that she was to be ready at seven in the morning, and that he had spoken to Ilse about her, and that the railway from Paris to Warsaw takes thirty-one hours when it is unmolested.

The second call was at nine, and it was Odile, and she used the word *aunt*, which was the word for *something has gone wrong*, and she used it twice, and she used it in German, and she never once in three years used German on a telephone.

Marion put the receiver down and stood in the dark hall with her hand still on it. In the drawing room the wireless was on and a German announcer was reading the communiqué: in the east, the enemy attack of the previous day had been repulsed; in Africa, the positions had been re-established; in the west, nothing to report. Kleist was in the kitchen with the door open, and the light of the kitchen lay along the boards of the corridor, and in the study behind the photograph of a dead woman there was a steel door with five levers, four of which had given, and in the small north room of the flat, in the toe of a shoe, inside a hatbox, in a bundle of folded handkerchiefs, there was a roll of film the size of a thumb, with sixteen exposures on it, and the film was now the most dangerous object in Paris, because the man who was to carry it was dead, or taken, and the woman who was to hand it on had just said the word *aunt* on a telephone.

The parcel of books did not come.

It was due on the Thursday, and on the Thursday there was no parcel, and on the Friday there was no parcel, and on the Saturday morning the woman who did the floors of the apartment said that the shop in the rue de Rivoli was shut, and that there was a paper of the German administration on the door of it, and that the paper said nothing that a woman was able to read in the street because a paper of the German administration on the door of a shop in the fourth year of the war means that nobody in the street wishes to be standing in front of it when the car comes round the corner.

A parcel late by two days is a parcel. A parcel late by four days is a signal, and the signal was in the agreement that had been made in a cinema in the fifth arrondissement in the February: if the channel is not closed by the fourth day, the channel is gone, and the woman at the counter is to be considered as the woman at the counter, and the man behind her is to be considered as a man, and there is to be no enquiry of any kind made by anybody in the block of the rue de Rivoli for a period of eleven weeks.

On the Saturday the Colonel came home to lunch and was in the good mood of a man who has been told something at an office.

"Eleven," he said, at the table, with the arithmetic of an appetite.

"Sir?"

"The Dulag in the rue des Saussaies has taken eleven of them since the Monday. Two of them are of no importance. One of them is a printer of the rue du Temple with a wife and four children and a medal from 1916, and the printing of a paper of the resistance of the north is in the cellar of his house behind the coal, and my colleague of the rue des Saussaies will have it by the Tuesday, and he will be very pleased with himself for a fortnight." He cut the meat. "This is the difficulty of it, Marion, and you will understand it better than the wives of the administration: a man of fifty-four who has spent his life in the intelligence service of an army and has a war of Europe in his hands is required, by the arithmetic of the service, to be pleased about eleven men in a cellar of the rue des Saussaies, and there is not one man of the service in the German administration of France who is able to tell me what it is for."

"Perhaps it is for the paper."

"The paper is a page," said the Colonel of the intelligence service of the district of the army of Paris, "and the page is worth the four men who print it, and the four men who print it are worth eleven arrests, and the eleven arrests are worth the attention of the office of the Militärbefehlshaber, and the attention of the office of the Militärbefehlshaber is worth a line in the report of the month, and the report of the month goes into a file in Berlin where it is read by a colonel of the general staff who has never been in a street of the fourth year of the war. And the printer of the rue du Temple is a man of fifty-three with a medal from 1916, and the difference between his life and mine is one page of paper, and there is not one thing in the world that either of us is able to do about it."

She ate her lunch.

She asked him, in the flat voice of a woman of the household, whether the eleven included a bookseller, and he said no, the eleven were a printer and a man of the post and nine of no importance at all, and she said that she did not like the closing of the shops of the rue de Rivoli, because the parcel of books was late, and he said that a shop of the rue de Rivoli that was closed on a Saturday in the April of the year 1943 was a shop of the rue de Rivoli that had been closed by the administration for the sale of the paper of the black market, and that the wife of the house should send the orderly to the rue de Rivoli on the Monday and let the matter be arranged in the ordinary way of the office of the district.

And the woman of the house ate the meal, and drank the coffee, and talked to the man about the price of the butter and the question of the piano of the Vasseurs, and went up the stairs at half past two to the room with the hatbox in it, and locked the door of the room, and stood in front of the wardrobe with her hand on the door of it for four seconds, and then took out of it the coat with the film sewn into the lining of the right sleeve, which had been in the wardrobe since the day of the sewing, and laid the coat on the bed, and stood looking at the coat the way a person looks at a coat, and did the arithmetic of the eleven.

*The parcel is four days late. The channel is closed. The cinema in the fifth is closed. The milliner is in the country, and there is a second man who comes on the pavement of the rue Cambon for a quarter of an hour in the evening, and there is an aunt who has been told to say what she was told to say. The bookseller of the rue de Rivoli is not one of the eleven of the rue des Saussaies, and a bookseller of the rue de Rivoli who is not one of the eleven and whose shop is shut on a Saturday in the April of 1943 is a bookseller who is in the wind, or a bookseller who is in the cellar, or a bookseller who is a mile under the street of the rue de Rivoli in the hands of a man with the light over the door of the cell and the list on the table, and there is exactly one way for a woman in this city to know which of the three, and the way is a coat with a film in the sleeve and a hatpin in the pocket and a blacked-out city of Paris between the avenue Henri-Martin and the house of the bookseller of the rue de Rivoli.*

She put on her coat. She put on the coat with the film sewn into the lining of the right sleeve, which she had done at four o'clock in the afternoon with a needle from the sewing box and eleven stitches, and she went out into the corridor and told Kleist that she would be an hour, that she was going to the dressmaker's, that the dressmaker was a woman of seventy and did not have a telephone, and that he was not to tell the Oberst on the telephone in the morning, because the Oberst did not like her walking in the streets after dark.

Then she went out of the door of the apartment at 12, avenue Henri-Martin, with the film in her sleeve and a hatpin in her hand in her pocket, into a blacked-out city, to find out which of her friends were still alive.
