# Chapter 21: The Photographer's Window

The studio was on the Nowy Świat, four doors from the corner of the Świętokrzyska, in a building with a marble stair and a brass plate that read *Fotografia — Lichtbildnerei* in two languages, and it had been the studio of a Polish photographer named Wierzbicki since 1922, and it had been, since 1940, one of the eleven establishments in the city of Warsaw that were permitted by the German administration to make portraits of German officers.

The window of it was the window she had walked past four hundred times: a shallow bow of glass, with a black velvet board in it, and on the board, in the last week of July 1944, forty-two photographs in three rows.

She had looked at that window in every month of the last two years, and it had always been the same and always different, in the way that a window of photographs in an occupied city is always the same and always different: the officers of the district in their tunics, at the beginning of their service or at the end of it, before they had been promoted or after; and a woman of twenty-seven who had learned to walk past a window forty times a month had learned, in the course of two years, to read the window like a register.

In the spring of 1944 there had been a photograph in the second row, third from the left, of a Hauptmann of the intelligence section, in a tunic with the ribbon of the winter campaign of 1941-42; and she had never once, in the time it hung there, permitted herself to look at it in the daylight.

On the twenty-ninth of July, at four in the afternoon, she had an appointment with Wierzbicki about the portraits of the General, which the district had ordered in the month of June and which were, in the last week of the war, one of those pieces of German administration that still had to be attended to; and she came up the marble stairs in her grey dress at four minutes to four, and the Polish boy in the shop said that the Herr Photograph would be with the lady in a moment; and she stood in the shop with her gloves in her hand, looking through the glass at the street, and René Tissier came in at two minutes to four with his cap under his arm.

"Madame Herschlag."

She did not turn around.

"Where is the man who was following me on Wednesday? Or was it Thursday? The one with the bicycle who is too old to be a man with a bicycle."

"He is in the café, madame, and he will stay there, and I have come alone, because I want you to listen to me without the apparatus, and because I have an hour." Tissier put his cap on the counter. "I am not going to ask you about the train today. I am going to give you something, and then I am going to go out and be on the street before you have left the shop, and you will never see me again unless you look for me."

"Give me the thing, then."

"There is a card index," said René Tissier. "It is in the aleja Szucha, in a room on the second floor, and it belongs to a Hauptsturmführer named Kroll, and it has two hundred and eleven thousand cards in it, and there is a card in it with your name on it, and I have read it, because my office has the index consolidated for the civil administration, and I have a key to the cabinet where the consolidations live, which is what a Frenchman of fifty is in this war: a man with a key to a cabinet."

She kept looking at the street through the window.

"Tell me what is on the card."

"*Herschlag, Marion. Born 9.4.1919 in Strasbourg. French. Employee of the district administration.* And on the back, in pencil, in the Hauptsturmführer's hand: *Brauchbar. Prüfen im Sommer.*" He paused. "There is also, in the same cabinet, in the file of the department of the east, a page dated the thirtieth of April 1943 with a list of four names from the Militärbefehlshaber in Paris, and one of the four names is the name of a woman who was in a corridor on the eleventh of December 1942, and there is a question mark in the margin next to it that was made in a pencil that does not belong to the man who typed the list, and the pencil is the pencil of the Hauptsturmführer who keeps the cards."

The shop was quiet. Behind the curtain of the studio, in the back room, somebody was moving a lamp.

"So you have come to tell me that you can be my death," she said.

"I have come to tell you that the Hauptsturmführer is at this hour reading a report about a page of the fortification schedule that got to the underground in February, and that in the last week of July a man with an index like his is not looking for a spy; he is looking for a hole in a house; and that when the city rises on the first of August, there is going to be a period of about eleven days in which nobody in this administration is going to have the time to read a card in a cabinet. And after the eleven days, there will be either a German administration or a Russian one, and in either of those the card is a piece of paper with a name on it that nobody will ever read again." He picked up his cap. "I have not come for the train today. I have come to tell you the thing that the man on the bicycle could not tell you: that I am able to put a piece of paper into a fire, madame. In the last week of the world, that is the only trade that I have left, and I am offering it to you without any price at all, because the man who has read your card is the only person in Europe who knows what you are, and when the cities are in ruins and the two of us are dead or alive, I would like there to be one thing in this war that I did out of my own smallness and not out of my fear."

He put his cap on, and looked at her for a moment, and there was, in his face, nothing of the foyer of the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées: he was fifty, and he had spent four years in the middle of the things that men of his kind did, and he was going to die in the month of August 1944 in circumstances that no one would ever write down.

"At nine tonight, the cabinet on the second floor is open for the consolidation of the files of the civil administration. I will be there. There will be a man with me, because there is always a man with me. So if you want the card in the fire, madame, you must be the one who tells me that; and you may tell me at any hour before midnight on the thirty-first, by the ordinary post, in an envelope, with one word in it."

He went out, down the marble stairs, into the Nowy Świat, at eleven minutes past four in the afternoon of the twenty-ninth of July 1944; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the shop of the photographer Wierzbicki with her gloves in her hand, looking at the window of the street, until the boy came to fetch her.

And the photograph in the window, in the second row, third from the left, which had hung there since the spring of 1944, was of a Hauptmann of the intelligence section of the district of Warsaw in a tunic with the ribbon of the winter campaign; and the lady in the grey dress looked at the window for a long moment as she went out of the shop, the way a woman of the district looks at the photographs of the officers of the district, with her attention, as though the window were nothing at all.

---

At half past seven that evening she went to the Sicherheitsdienst and paid her monthly instalment.

It was the sixth time she had done it, in the first-floor room with the bars painted the colour of the cream of the walls, and in the six times she had established the thing that was going to save her, at the last, and it was not an affection: it was a *habit*.

She had taught Werner Kroll the shape of her usefulness the way a woman in a salon teaches a man of fifty the shape of an evening: a piece of gossip about the wives, always true in the facts and useless in the substance; a warning about a colonel of the supply section who drank and talked in the officers' mess at the Blue Palace about the movement of the trains — which was true, and which she had given him in June, and which had cost that colonel nothing, because he had been transferred to Kraków in the following week; the price of butter; the arrangements of the Frau Generalin's table; and the small, exquisite, perfectly true item that she had brought him in July about the wife of the district's medical officer, who had been writing letters to her sister in Stuttgart about the hopelessness of the German position in the east.

The letters had been nothing: a woman of thirty-eight writing to a sister. But Kroll had put the sentence in his notebook with the fine clerk's hand, and had looked up at her the way a man looks up at a good piece of work, and had said: "You have an ear, and you have no cruelty, and it is a combination that my service does not have and cannot make."

That evening, at half past seven, she gave him Tissier.

She gave him the man, exactly as it was, in the flat voice of a woman of the administration of the district who had been frightened by something in a shop; and it was true, every word of it, and it was the truth arranged in the order of the clock, which is the order in which the man with the green notebook had once, in a room in Paris, told her that she was unique.

"On Wednesday and on Thursday a man followed me, an old one, on a bicycle. On Saturday, at eleven in the morning, a Frenchman of fifty, René Tissier, who has the uniform of a Hauptsturmführer of the office of the liaison, came to the house on the Bagatela and presented an order of the district concerning the evacuation of the civil administration and told me that he intends to be on a train. On the twenty-ninth, at four o'clock, the same man was waiting for me at the studio of the photographer Wierzbicki on the Nowy Świat and told me that he has a key to the cabinet of the consolidation of the files of the civil administration and that he had read the card index of this office, and that he knows that a woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district has a card in the index with a pencil note on the back of it, and he told me the words of the note."

She said it without a pause, and she looked at Kroll across the table while she said it, and she finished with the sentence that she had built, in the tram, on the way there:

"And then he offered to put the card in a fire, and he asked me for nothing in exchange; and the reason he asked me for nothing is that he is going to be in the city on the first of August, and he is fifty, and he is one of the four hundred thousand German civilians in the east, and he wants the one thing that every man in the German administration of the east wants in the last week of July, which is a place on the train; and the way he is going to get the place on the train is by holding a card over the head of a woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district, so that she takes him to the station in a German car, and says one sentence to the officer of the train, and puts a stranger into the civilian administration of Warsaw in the last week of the war."

Werner Kroll put his pen down.

He was silent for eleven seconds, which was a long time for him, and in the eleven seconds she watched him go through the arithmetic that she had built for him in the tram: a Frenchman of fifty with a key to a cabinet of the civil administration, reading a card index of the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw, and offering to a woman of a German General's household the removal of a card from that index, in exchange for a place on a train; and a man of that description in a room of that kind, in a district that was about to be the scene of the last German defence of the east, was not a man who was holding a card over the head of a woman of the district.

He was a man who was holding a card.

"How long has he been in this city?"

"Since February. He is the liaison of the office of the civil administration with the office of the district; he is French; he was in Warsaw before the war, as a teacher of French, in the twenties, and he has been in Paris through the occupation, and he sat at the table of the Hôtel Majestic in 1943 at the invitation of the Militärbefehlshaber." She put her gloves on. "His name in Paris was on the list of the journalists who were permitted to the press conferences of the Propaganda Staffel. He is a man with a newspaper, Hauptsturmführer. He has been a man with a newspaper for eleven years."

Kroll wrote one line in the green notebook, in the fine clerk's hand, and closed it.

"Madame Herschlag."

"Yes."

"You have brought me a Frenchman of the civil administration who has been reading my index," he said, "and I want to tell you what the transaction looks like from this side of the table, because I have never once lied to you, and I do not intend to begin on the twenty-ninth of July." He stood up and put his cap on and adjusted it, and looked at her with the flat, patient, terrifying courtesy of a man who had spent eleven months writing down the names of the population of a city. "A woman whose card is in the index of my office has come to me and informed against the only man in this city who has ever read that card. There is a reading of the transaction that is loyal: the woman has brought me a leak in my own office. And there is a second reading of the transaction, and it is a reading that a man with my profession must always make, and it is that the woman has taken the one man in Warsaw who holds her life in his hand, and has put him in my hand, in the last week before the city burns, which is the most elegant piece of work that anybody has done in this district in the four years of the war." He opened the door for her. "Both of the readings are true, or one of them, or neither. I have decided to act on the first, because the man with the key is a danger to the office and a nuisance, and because the second reading requires me to believe that a Frenchwoman of the avenue Henri-Martin has been able to spend two years in this district under my nose for a purpose that nobody has ever been able to demonstrate; and I am a policeman, and in my life, I have never once arrested a person for a possibility."

She stood on the threshold of the room with the bars painted cream.

"And what will you do with him?"

"I will take him tonight," said Werner Kroll, "in his flat, with the key in his pocket, and I will take his cabinet, and I will put him in the Pawiak, and on the first of August the population of the Pawiak is going to be a very large problem for the German administration of this city, and I have every intention of being on the other side of the river when the problem is decided." He held the door. "This is the last time we shall speak in this room. There is not going to be a consolidation of the files after the thirtieth; my office goes to the west; and you have the card that I promised you in March, and you have something better, and you have had it for four months, and I might as well tell you, because it is a matter of arithmetic and not of the heart: there is a list of the persons attached to the staff of the district that is permitted to leave with the civil administration, and there is a second list of the households of the officers of the district, and every name on the second list has been checked, and there is one name on the second list that has been checked four times, and the name is yours, and the checks were made in Paris, in Strasbourg, and in the office of the Militärbefehlshaber, and the reason I have checked it four times, madame, is that in my career I have never once seen a file that was as clean as yours."

She looked at him.

"Then it is not a file," she said. "It is a woman who has been very careful."

"There is no difference," said Werner Kroll, "and there never has been."

She walked home from the aleja Szucha at twenty to nine in the evening of the twenty-ninth of July 1944.

It was the hour of the blackout and the hour of the curfew for the Polish population, and the street belonged to the patrols: two men of the German police at the corner of the Bagatela with their torches down, a car of the field police standing at the mouth of the Polna with the motor running, and the Poles walking in the middle of the roadway in the way the Poles of Warsaw had learned to walk in the fifth year, close to the wall and fast and with their eyes on the pavement eleven metres in front of them. She walked in her own street the way she had walked in it for fourteen months, in the grey dress and the good shoes, and the men of the patrol looked at her and looked away: the woman of the household of the General of the district was a fixed object in the quarter, like the sentry box and the chestnut tree.

The house was empty of everybody but the cook and the furier.

She had a bath, and ate the cold supper that had been left for her under a cloth, and stood for a while at the drawing-room window with the blind down and the lamp off, listening to the city: the tram on the Marszałkowska, the radio of the German officers' mess at the corner of the Ujazdowskie, the bell of a tram in the direction of the Wola, the flat crack of one shot somewhere north of the wall, and then nothing. It was the sound of the last week of the occupation, and every Pole in the city was listening to it the way a farmer listens to the wind.

The telephone rang at eleven.

"Marion."

"Friedrich."

The line from Kraków was a long-distance line of the military administration, and it came with the apparatus of the year 1944: a hiss, a hum, and the voice of the operator of the exchange at Częstochowa asking for the number and the rank of the subscriber in the flat tones of a woman of the post, and then the connection, and then the voice of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw coming into a dark study in a house on the Bagatela out of a room six hundred kilometres to the south.

"You should be in bed."

"I should be anything you like."

"That," he said, "is the first thing you have said to me in two years that I have not been able to hear and answer in the same minute." There was a pause on the line, and a noise like the sea, which was the crackle of the province of Poland between the two of them. "I have been in a room with eleven men since four o'clock. There is an order coming, and it is going to be in my hand on the morning of the thirty-first, and it is an order about the line of the river, and the line of the river is drawn on the map on the table in front of me at this hour with a coloured pencil, and it goes from the bridge of the north of the city to the bend at the Wilanów, and I am a general of the intelligence of a district that is going to be asked to hold a front of twenty-six kilometres with a battery of twelve guns and a battalion of the railway."

"There will be more."

"There will be nothing more," said Friedrich von Reuth. "The line of the river is a map, and the map is a piece of the arithmetic of the east, and the arithmetic of the east was decided in the month of June at Minsk, and I am a man of fifty-five in a room in Kraków reading a map on which the German army of the east is a line of coloured pencil." He was quiet for a moment. "I have a question for you, and it is the question that I have been asking myself since the month of the May, and I am going to ask you across the province of Poland on a telephone, because on a telephone a man is able to say anything."

"Ask it."

"Why are you in that house?"

She stood in the dark of the study of the house on the Bagatela with the receiver against her ear, and the question, which she had been waiting for since the ceiling of a room in May, was in the room with her, at the end of a line of six hundred kilometres, in the voice of a man of fifty-five who had never once asked her for the truth and was never going to be able to hear it.

"Because in my life," she said, "there has been exactly one house in which I have been able to be in one piece, and it is the house on the Bagatela, and the man in it is on the telephone in a room in Kraków asking me the question, and the answer is: because it is the only one."

The line was quiet for eleven seconds.

"On the morning of the first of September," he said, "there is a train of the district of the intelligence service of Warsaw going to the west with the records of the district, and I have signed the paper of it this evening in the room with the eleven men, and there is a place on that train for the woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and the paper of the place is in the pocket of my tunic, and I am going to put it into your hand in this study on the night of the thirty-first of the August 1944, and we are not going to discuss it, and you are not going to look at me in the way you look at me when you are doing the arithmetic."

She held the receiver with both hands.

"Come back," she said. "On the thirtieth, at nine in the evening, at the door of the house on the Bagatela, in the Mercedes, with the mud of the province of Poland on it; and there is going to be a bath and the glass of the Silesian and the woman in the chair by the window with the lamp turned low, and the rest of the arithmetic is going to be able to wait for the morning."

"Marion."

"Yes."

"There is one more thing," said the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, "and it is the last thing I am going to say on this telephone, because the operator of the exchange at Częstochowa is a woman of the post of the Reich, and she has been on this line for four minutes, and it is the last thing that I am ever going to say in the war that is going to be in a record in the records of it: whatever happens, there is not one thing that has been in this house that has not been mine; and of the objects in it, the one that is in the chair by the window with the lamp turned low is the only object that the war has not been able to take, and I have been in the war for the five years of it."

The line went dead at eleven minutes past eleven, and the house on the Bagatela was silent, and the woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw stood in the middle of the study of the house with the receiver in her hand and the dial of the telephone of the district in front of her, and the conversation was in the room and was going to be in the room for the rest of her life.

---

She went upstairs and did the arithmetic of the morning.

The dress for the station: the grey, with the coat of the spring of 1944, and the shoes that were good enough for a platform and used enough not to be looked at twice by a man at a barrier, and the hat that the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska had given her in May with the four rules in it.

The bag: the Minox loaded with the last film of the arrangement, the document of the district in the name of Marion Herschlag, the Kennkarte of the administration, the money — eleven hundred zloty and four hundred marks — the two photographs of the household, and the little book that the woman of the Countess had left at the flat of the Krasińskiego in the month of the June and that was, in the property of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna in the year 1944, the only object that had been in the house of her mother.

The coat: the winter coat with the parcel of the Krasińskiego in the lining, on the hook in the wardrobe, where it had hung since the third of July, and which nobody in the house had looked at since the third of July, because in Warsaw in the last week of July of the year 1944 there was not a man in the German administration who was able to look at a winter coat.

And she stood in front of the open wardrobe in the dark of the room on the first floor with the inventory in her head, and did the last piece of the arithmetic of the twenty-ninth of July 1944, and it was this:

*Tomorrow at half past five in the morning, the last train of the civil administration of the district of Warsaw leaves the station on the Ochota side of the town, and the woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw is expected on it, and the paper of the place is in the pocket of the tunic of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and at half past five in the morning the German administration of the city of Warsaw is going to be standing on the platform of that station and looking at the two of us; and the thing that I am going to have to do on the platform at half past five in the morning of the thirty-first of July 1944 is the last piece of work of the four years, and it is not a lie of the mouth and it is not a lie of the face, and it is a lie of the body of a woman who is going to stand in the middle of a station in the dark and refuse to get into a carriage in the manner of a person who is telling the truth.*

*And then there is going to be one more day.*

The General came back from Kraków at nine o'clock on the evening of the thirtieth of July, and he came back a different man, in a way that she saw from the window of the first floor before he was out of the car.

He was thinner in two days. He stood at the door of the Mercedes and looked up at the house with his face grey; and he was carrying, in a leather folder, an order of the army group, and he came up the marble stairs with it the way a man carries a document that has already killed eleven people.

"They have given me the line," he said, standing in the hall. "The Vistula line. From the bridge at the north of the city to the bend at the Wilanów: the district's intelligence, the engineers of the garrison, and eleven trains of materials that are not going to arrive. I have eleven days, twenty thousand men, no fuel, no aeroplanes, and a city of a million Poles in the middle of it." He put the folder on the hall table and looked at her. "And there is the order about the civilian administration, which is what I came back from Kraków with, and which I am going to ask you to obey at half past five tomorrow morning, at the station, when the last train of the German civil administration of the district of Warsaw pulls out."

She looked at the folder on the table.

"You are going to ask me."

"I am going to ask you," said Friedrich von Reuth, "because in two years I have never once given you an order, and because in my life I have never once asked a woman for anything, and because the arithmetic of the east is on the table in that leather folder and I have looked at it for four hours in a car from Kraków and I know what it says, and it says that on the first day of August the city of Warsaw is going to burn, and that the burning is going to be in the north and the centre and the Wola, and that the house on the Bagatela is four hundred metres from the first barricade, and that the woman of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw is going to be, in the first week of it, a name on a door of a German house with a light on in it."

"Friedrich."

"One word," he said. "At the station, at half past five in the morning, in the dark, on the platform, with the Feldgendarmerie and the administration of the district looking at the two of us. One word, and it is over, and I will put you on the train with my own hands."

And in the hall of the house on the Bagatela, on the thirtieth of July 1944, with the tray of the German cook standing on the sideboard and the walnut tree in the garden and the city going quiet outside the windows in the blackout, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna did the last piece of arithmetic of the first five years of her war, and it took four seconds, and at the end of it there was exactly one answer, and the answer was the one she had known since the fourteenth of May in a May, at the end of a conversation in a room above a furier's in the Praga district.

"Yes," she said. "Tomorrow, at half past five, at the station. I will be ready."

He came across the hall and took her face in both his hands, the way he had done in the room on the first floor in May, and looked at her for a long time from a distance of eleven centimetres.

"Thank you," said the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw.
