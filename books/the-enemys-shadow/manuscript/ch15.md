# Chapter 15: The Man Who Knew Her

The market at Kercelak was held every day of the war on a waste ground the size of two city blocks in the Wola district, and it was the economy of the city of Warsaw in the fifth year of the occupation: five thousand people in a field of mud, in rags, in a January, buying and selling the German Reich's property back to the German Reich's officers through the hands of the Polish people, at eleven in the morning, on a Thursday, with a police operation at the gate and a division of the Wehrmacht four hundred metres away in the barracks at the corner.

She went once a month, and she went as the Frenchwomen of the German administration went: with Anna Zaleska, and with a basket, and with the license of a household of an officer to buy eggs and a chicken from a Pole at a price that had been fixed in the German ordinance at a level that had been arranged so that nobody could obey it, and so that the city would be, for the rest of the war, criminal.

They had been at the market for eleven minutes. Anna had gone to the egg women; and Krystyna stood at the corner of the second row of the stalls with the basket on the ground, looking at a table of boots, in the ordinary way of a woman of the household of an officer waiting for a cook to finish a piece of business, when a man of thirty-one in a blue police uniform stopped two metres away and looked at her and said, in Polish:

"Panna Krystyna."

There is no way to describe what happens in a woman's body at a moment like that. There is the training, which is instantaneous: the face does not move; the eyes do not widen; the breath goes on; and there is the second, deeper thing, which is not training at all, and which is the animal at the bottom of a person noticing, at two metres, in the middle of a crowded market, in a city where the people she loved were dead, that it has been seen.

She looked at him.

He had been a boy of twenty-two in the summer of 1938, with a crush on her that the city had known about, and she had pitied him; and he had a patent on a design for a lamp, and a set of ambitions, and a mother who had made the introductions; and he had asked her to dance four times at a *bal* in the month of February 1939, and on the second time she had said yes out of pity, and on the fourth he had told her, in the corridor by the coat room, that he intended to marry her; and she had laughed, in the way of a girl of twenty-two who has been raised to be laughed at by nobody, and had said a sentence that she had forgotten within an hour and that he had carried for five years.

He was fatter. He had a moustache. He had the blue uniform of the Polish police of the Generalgouvernement, with a rank on the collar; and he had, in his eyes, the exact expression of a boy of twenty-two in a corridor at a February ball.

"Panna Krystyna," he said again, taken aback at his own voice, and then, in the French that a Warsaw man of the educated class spoke before the war: "It is you. I have looked at you for four minutes. I could not believe my eyes."

"Monsieur Groński."

"I live here, in the Wola; I have a post in the sixth district and an office," he said. He was talking too fast, and he had the look of a man who has been given a present and does not know what is in the wrapping. "And you — they told me the family was gone. In 1940 the Mokotowska building; your father, in the summer, there were the rumours and nobody would say it — and here you are, at the Kercelak, with a basket."

"I am with a household," she said.

"With a household." He looked at her coat, which was the coat of a household; and then at the basket; and then, in the way of a policeman of the city of Warsaw in the fifth year of the war, at the middle distance, at the row of the egg women, at the woman with the good face who was coming back along the aisle with a chicken — and he did the arithmetic that a city of Poles did twenty times a day in that war, and it took him four seconds.

"Panna Krystyna," he said, in a completely different voice, "in whose household do you stand?"

She looked at him for a long moment, in the mud of the Kercelak, with the city around them; and she made the decision that she had been trained to make in a second and had never once been given time to think about: the decision between the lie that would end the conversation and the truth that would put a rope around his neck and hers.

"I am the woman of the Oberst von Reuth," she said, quietly, in Polish. "I live in the house on the Mokotowska. The the district knows my face. The the German administration of Warsaw knows me as Madame Herschlag of Strasbourg, and if you are seen speaking to me for another minute, in this market, in the daylight, then you will be taken to the Szucha by Friday and I will not be able to do a single thing about it, and I do not intend to lose a war over a man I danced with in 1939."

He went a colour.

And then he did what she had not expected at all, and what every single one of the Poles of Warsaw did, in the fifth year of the war, in the face of the occupation: he smiled at her with his mouth, in the manner of an old friend of the family, and kissed the air beside her ear, on both sides, in the Polish fashion, and said, loudly, in a Polish that could be heard at four stalls, "Pani, the eggs in this market are a scandal, and I will not have it; my wife buys at the Smocza and pays a third of the price."

And then, in a whisper, at her ear, in the same second: "On the third of February, at the seventh hour, at the church of St. Kazimierz in the Wola. If I do not come, I am dead; if you do not come, I will come to the garden gate on the Mokotowska."

He walked off into the market, and Anna Zaleska came up with the chicken, and the two of them went home along the Chłodna with the basket, and neither of them said a single word for eleven minutes.

At the corner, by the wall, Anna Zaleska stopped and looked at the trail of the smoke over the north of the city, over the wall, over the district which had been burning since the summer and which Warsaw could smell every day of the winter, and said, in the Polish, in a level voice:

"In 1940, before the wall, the women of this city went to the Kercelak with the basket, and the men of the blue police went with the pistol, and us were one people, and the difference between a blue policeman and a man with a basket was a paycheck." She shifted the chicken to her other hand. "Now the blue police take the people of this city at night out of the buildings, and my Piotr has a cousin in the Gęsiówka because of a blue policeman on the Chłodna, and I have the chicken and a boy in a chemist's shop and my silence, and I am telling you this so that you understand that in this city we do not have the luxury of the judgment, but we do have the memory, and the memory is what I am handing to you now, in the street, on the way home."

---

The weekly report of the household came to the hand of the Colonel on the first of February.

It was the office of the Ordonnanzoffizier that produced it, every Monday, in the neat small hand of a captain of the intelligence section: two pages of the movements of the house on the Mokotowska, the visits to the wives' table, the three shops, the tram lines, the film, the church on the two Sundays, and — at the foot of the second page, under the heading *Bemerkungen* — the notations of the officer of the guard at the gate. The remark on the first of February was one line, and it said, in the German of a young man trained to write everything down and to think about nothing: *Am 29.1. gegen 12 Uhr fragte ein Angehöriger der Polnischen Polizei (blau) am Tor nach der Dame des Haushalts; vom Posten abgewiesen.*

On the twenty-ninth of January, at about twelve, a member of the Polish police, blue, asked at the gate after the lady of the household; turned away by the sentry.

She learned that the remark existed because the Colonel read the report aloud to her.

He came home at a quarter past eight, which he did on the Mondays, and he came into the small room with the two pages in his hand and his cap under his arm and his greatcoat on, and he stood in front of the stove with the paper in his hand and read her the morning of Monday, the twenty-ninth of January, in a flat voice, from the first line to the last, and then he read her the line at the foot of the second page, twice, in German, and then once in French, in his own translation, which was better than the German, because the man had been a soldier of the intelligence service of an army for thirty-six years and had never once in his life read a sentence aloud without understanding it.

Then he put the paper on the table and took his coat off and gave the coat to Anna in the corridor, and came back in and sat down in the chair by the stove without taking the glass she had poured him.

"Now," he said. "The man in the blue police."

"I saw him," she said.

"You will tell me about him."

"I will tell you the thing as it is, and it is not a large thing, and then I will tell you the second part, which is, and the second part is about you and not about the man." She said it with her hands folded in her lap, in the French of the avenue Henri-Martin, in the tone of a woman who has thought about a question for two days and has the answer, because she had, in fact, thought about it for two days, since the moment on the Chłodna when Anna Zaleska had started to speak about the blue police and she had understood that the Kercelak was now a place with a witness in it. "On the Monday I went to the market with Anna, as I go on the Mondays. There was a man at the corn stall, a Pole, in the blue uniform of the city police, of about thirty years of age, and he knows the face of the woman of the German Colonel of the district, because the sixth district has been taking an interest in the German houses of the city since the autumn, and because my face has been in the *Nowy Kurier* twice, at the reviewing stand, and at the ball of the Winterhilfswerk in the room of the Regina in the month of November." She looked at him. "He came up behind me at the corn stall and said my name; he said it in the Polish fashion, the fashion of a man who has seen it written; and then he said a thing about the district, and then he went away into the crowd. He asked me for nothing, Friedrich. He followed me to the corner of the Chłodna, four metres behind me, with a chicken under his arm and his cap in his pocket, and at the corner he stopped and turned and went back into the Wola; and I came home; and there was no man at the gate, and he did not come to the gate, and it was not he who asked at the gate."

"Then who asked at the gate?"

"I do not know," she said. "And here is the second part, and I want you to hear it in this room and not in the Blue Palace, because it is not a question of the office, it is a question of the two of us, and there has never been a lie between us and there is not going to be one tonight." She took the glass he had not taken and turned it once in her hand and put it down. "Any man of the blue police of this city can buy the answer at the gate for a litre of the Silesian and a packet of the German cigarettes, and every sentry of every German house in Warsaw has been answering that question since the first week of the occupation, because the answer is worth money to the men of the market and the men of the blue police and the boys of the black market, and there is not one thing about me at that gate that is not for sale. That is the danger, and it is not a danger that came to me on the Monday; it is a danger that has been standing in the street with its hand in its pocket for five months, and the man at the corn stall is only the first man in the city who has been able to find my name and put it together with my face."

He looked at her for a long time from the chair by the stove.

"That is a very good answer," he said.

"It is the answer."

"It is a good answer and it is not the first part of the answer," said the Colonel of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, "and I am going to say the first part of it to you, because I have been reading the reports of the office of the Ordonnanzoffizier of this district since the month of the June, and there is one thing about the woman of my house that the reports of my own office have been telling me for five months and that I have been reading the way a man reads the weather in a window: she is not frightened of the city."

She did not move.

"Do you know what a German officer's wife is in this city?" he said. "She is a woman in a house with the curtains and a driver. She goes to the wives' table, and to the two shops of the German quarter, and to the church of the administration on the Sundays, and her Warsaw is four streets and the interior of the Mercedes; and there is not one of them who has gone into the Wola since the second year of the occupation, and there is not one of them who will walk from the Irena to the house on the Mokotowska on her own feet in the middle of the afternoon, and there is not one of them who knows the price of the eggs at the Kercelak. My wife, in 1931, in Breslau, was a woman of the province with a handshake like a farmer's and she would have gone to the Kercelak and fought with the egg women and won." He stood up. "And I have been watching you take the tram to the Krasińskiego and come home with the cabbage, and I have said to myself: it is a Frenchwoman, it is the way, it is the climate of the market and the two of them; and there is one thing in it that I have never once been able to make into a sentence, and it is that you have been walking in this city for five months with the face of a woman who is not in an occupied country."

He put the two pages of the report into the stove.

"Friedrich—"

"Do not." He held up his hand. "I have not asked you the question, and I am not going to ask the question tonight, because if I ask it in this room I shall have to ask it in the Blue Palace tomorrow, and there is not one man in the German intelligence of this district who is able to survive the asking of the question about the woman of his own house; and because I am fifty-four and there is a war and a line of the Vistula and an eleven thousand men in the east who are going to be in the east in the summer, and because there has never been a lie between us and I have decided to spend the rest of the war on that." He picked up his glass and drank it off. "But I am making an arrangement for the market, and the arrangement is this: from the first of the February, the woman of my house does not go into the city on her own feet. The car goes to the Krasińskiego on the Tuesdays, and the driver goes with the basket, and if the woman of my house wants the eggs of the Kercelak then the driver of the district buys them at the Kercelak and puts them on the seat of the car in the paper, which is what every German household in this city does, and which is what we are going to do until the matter of the east is decided."

"Then I am to be a woman in a house with the curtains."

"You are to be a woman in a house with the curtains," said the Colonel of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, "because I have signed the order of the day of the district that puts the German administration of this city behind a wall, and because I intend to spend the rest of the war in the position of a man whose house has not been burned down over the head of the one person in it."

He did not say anything more about it that night.

---

He came to the bed at eleven o'clock, and what he brought to the bed was the thing that she had spent five months learning to read, and it was not anger.

Anger she could manage: anger has a shape and a rhythm and an end, and a man in anger is a man who has already decided something and is saying it with the body on the way to the saying. What the Colonel brought into the room on the first of February was possession, in the sense in which a man can only possess a thing by proving that it can be taken from him; and it began with the door of the room and the lamps switched off and the two of them standing, and a hand flat on the back of her neck, and the voice of an officer of the intelligence service asking her, very quietly, in the dark, to say the name of a man in a blue uniform again.

She said the name.

He had her against the wardrobe first, and there was nothing slow about it, and she went with it the way she had been trained to go with it in the long room in Kraków in the winter of 1940, which is to say with her hands and her breath and the surface of the wardrobe and her face turned to the side so that the ear-clips would not be torn out of her ears, working: counting the open of the door, and the distance to the bed, and the fact that in the corridor the brown dog of the household was asleep at the head of the stairs and would not give her more than four seconds of warning if the door of the house opened.

And when it was done, and he had her in the bed and was lying with his hand on the inside of her thigh the way he lay when he wanted her to know that the night was not finished, she did the thing that she had never once done in the five months, and it was not calculation, or not only calculation, and it was:

"You are not going to ask me the question," she said, into the dark, in the French of the two of them.

"No."

"Then I will answer it once, and then never again, and I will answer it the way a woman answers a man at the end of a night and not the way a woman of the house answers an officer of the intelligence service." She turned her head on the pillow. "I did not choose this house. I was put in this house by a paper of the administration of the district and a place that a woman of Strasbourg takes in the war because there is nothing else in the world for her to take. And in the five months I have come to know what is in it, and I have found that what is in it is a man of fifty-four in a chair by the stove, in a house in a country that is not his, with the east in front of him; and there is not one hour of the five months that I have spent in this house calculating what the house is worth to me. Ask the office of your own Ordonnanzoffizier: there is not a piece of paper of the district of Warsaw that has ever been in my hand. But if you want the answer, then it is that the woman in this bed has been in the bed for five months, and that the man in the bed is the only person in the German administration of this city who has ever asked her for nothing but herself."

He was silent for a long time in the dark.

Then he put his arm across her and pulled her against his chest, the way a man of the province holds a child, and said the last sentence of the night, and it was the sentence of a man who had been given, by the woman in the bed, the exact amount of the truth that he was able to carry home to the Blue Palace in the morning:

"In the summer there is going to be an order, and the order is going to be about the line of the river, and when the order comes I am going to put this house and this bed and the woman of the house on the last train that goes to the west; and there is not going to be any discussion about it, and there is not going to be any argument in the small room by the stove, and the argument of it is the one that I have just been given in this bed, which is that a man does not take a woman out of a burning city because of a paper of the district, but because of the other thing, and the other thing has no name in the German language and is the only thing I have."

She lay in the dark with the arm of a colonel of the German intelligence service across her body and the brown dog asleep at the head of the stairs and February coming towards her, and she looked at the ceiling and did the arithmetic from the beginning, and the arithmetic had changed its shape since the summer, and the change in the shape of it was the thing that she was not going to be able to repair.

---

Wiktor Groński came to the church of St. Kazimierz on the third of February at seven in the evening, at the end of the afternoon service, in a good coat without the uniform, with a hat in his hands; and he stood in the nave with the crowd, and said the prayers with the crowd, and at a quarter past seven, in the aisle by the side door, he put out his arm and stopped her.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "In the porch. If we are in the church, the priest will see us; if we go to the street, we are two people on a corner."

"Pani Krystyna—"

"Eleven minutes. Begin."

And he began, and it was the saddest and most ordinary thing in the city of Warsaw in the fifth year of the war, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the porch of a church in the Wola district and listened to it with the attention that she had spent three years perfecting, and it was this.

He was not a monster. He was a blue policeman of thirty-one with an office in the sixth district, and his whole office was the registration of the property of the people who had been taken in that district: the flats, the furniture, the accounts; and he had, since 1941, taken the bribes that the population of his district offered the offices of the Polish police of the Generalgouvernement: for the release of a husband from a roundup, for the warning about a night raid, for the certificate, for the extra name in the register; and he had, in the two years, saved eleven human beings, and had lost, in the same two years, in the operations in which he had stood at the end of a cordon with a rifle and a list in his pocket, something on the order of four hundred.

He had, in the summer of 1943, been in the cordon around the district of the north, and he had put a family of six people into a courtyard wall, in the dark, on the Dzielna; and the mother of that family had said his own surname, out loud, in the dark, because she had known his mother; and he had gone home and drunk a bottle and come to the church of St. Kazimierz on the following Sunday, and had been coming ever since.

"And now there is you," he said.

"There is me."

"You are the daughter of Professor Stankiewicz of the Mokotowska, and you are the woman of a German colonel, in the house on the Mokotowska; and I have the last month thought of nothing else, and I want you to understand the position clearly, because I am not the blackmailer of a novel; a blackmailer of a novel is a man who wants the money." He looked at the hat in his hands. "I am the man who, in 1939, in a corridor, was told by a girl that he was nothing and that he should marry a woman of his own kind. And you were right. I married a woman of my own kind and she died of the typhus in 1940 and I have been nothing ever since; and now I have found you in the Kercelak standing next to a German colonel's cook with a basket; and the thing that I want, Panna Krystyna, is not your money and not your body."

"Then what?"

"I want to be the man who decides what happens to you." He said it in the voice of a man of thirty-one in a porch of a church in a Polish February, and it was, in its way, the most frightening and the most truthful thing that an ordinary man had said to her in her life. "I want you to come to me once a month and to tell me the truth about what you are, and to do the small things that I ask, which will be the small things and not the large ones; and in the exchange, I will give you the warnings from the office, which are worth your life and the lives of the men you work for; and I will never tell a single person a syllable, and if you ever fail me, I will go to the Szucha with a letter, and there is not one thing in the world that you will be able to do about it, because I am a blue policeman with a badge, and you are the woman of a German colonel, and nobody in the war is going to believe one syllable out of your mouth."

She stood in the porch.

"In the thirty days," she said, "for the first instalment, I will give you the following, and it is the truth, which is that I am not able to give you the truth about my work, and that the reason is not that I do not trust you. It is that the reason is the arithmetic of a war, which is that a man who does not know a thing cannot be made to say it."

"The the arithmetic," he said, and laughed, and there was the 1939 in the laugh. "You were always the arithmetic."

---

She reported it to the priest at the Visitation on the fourth of February; and the priest listened to it in the dark of the confessional without one single interruption, which was a thing she had never known him to do, and at the end, he said:

"I know this man."

"You know the city."

"I know this man, because in 1940, in the autumn, there was a case of the blue police in the Wola which came before the court of the underground, and the matter was a blue policeman who had put a family into a courtyard wall; and the court sat in a cellar for four hours and the verdict was a death sentence, and the sentence was not carried out, because the man's own mother came to the court, on her knees, in the cellar, at the age of seventy-one." The priest was quiet for a moment. "And now the man is standing in the porch of the St. Kazimierz with a hat in his hands and a letter with your whole name in his pocket, and the case is not the case of 1940 any more, because a man who knows one name is a man who will, in the end, in the fourth or the fifth month, tell it to the Szucha, and the Szucha has a card index, and a card index is the end of the underground of this city."

"Then what is my part in it?"

"The whole of your part in it," said the priest, "was to tell me it, and you have told me it; and now your part in it is over, and the rest of it belongs to the men in the cellars, and you will go home to the Mokotowska and drink the tea of the German administration, and you will not do the *anything* about Wiktor Groński; and if the man dies in the February, you will learn it from the German newspaper in March, in the same way that the city learns everything."

He died on the nineteenth of February, at half past seven in the evening, in the doorway of his own house on the Wolska, shot four times by three men of whom the population of that street would remember, to the end of their lives, precisely nothing, because the population of Warsaw had been trained, in the four years, to see nothing at the exact moment of a killing, and to be standing in the darkness of a blackout at the moment when they described it to the German police.

She read about it in the *Nowy Kurier Warszawski*, which was the German-controlled Polish-language paper of the city, on the twenty-first of February, at the breakfast table, in the house on the Mokotowska, with the German administration of the Warsaw district in the room; and the paper carried a paragraph of three lines under the heading *Mord an einem Polizeibeamten*, and she read the three lines and put the paper down and drank her coffee and went on with the morning; and at eleven o'clock, in the corridor by the kitchen, she stood with her back to the wall and did the thing she had been doing since the month of November without ever admitting it to herself, which was to take the memory of a man in a porch of a church with a hat in his hands, and to put it in the long room at the back of her mind, next to the tailor's wife of the rue des Rosiers, and next to the mother of the boy on the quai, and next to the man with a bad heart and a sister's umbrella who had died in the rue des Saussaies of a heart attack, and to shut the door of the room.

And in the week which followed, in the house on the Mokotowska, the woman of the colonel of the Abwehr was, in the opinion of the household, in the best of her health: she went to the wives' table on the Thursday, and took the tea, and discussed the price of the butter, and the question of the film of the week; and she went to the bed of the Colonel of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw on four of the seven nights of that whole week, and did the work, and was, in the assessment of the man who had been given a district, in the excellent form, in the way of a woman who had, in the five months of the autumn and the winter, become the one thing in his whole world that was not the war.
