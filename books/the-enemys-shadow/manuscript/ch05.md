# Chapter 5: What the Colonel Wants

The château was at Chémery, twelve kilometres south of Blois, and it had belonged to a family of paper manufacturers who had lived in it since 1698 and were now in the unoccupied zone, if they were anywhere at all.

In the eighteenth century one of them had planted an avenue of limes half a kilometre long, and the Wehrmacht had hung them with wire because the wire was needed for the telegraph; and the wire had gone, and the limes stood in the March rain with their bark slit at intervals of a metre and their pale wood showing like bone. There was a gatehouse, and a pair of iron gates that worked, and a courtyard. There were forty-two windows, three of which were broken. There was a marble bust of a lady of the family with her hair up, standing in the hall on a plinth, with a German soldier's cap on her head.

And there was a party of fourteen officers and their women, gathered for four days of shooting and bridge, because the Reich had decided, somewhere in the winter, that its colonels should be provided once a year with something that resembled peace.

They drove down on the nineteenth of March in a convoy: three cars and a lorry, and the Colonel's Mercedes with a wooden crate of champagne in the boot that had come, Ehlers explained in an aside, from a cellar in Reims that had been visited by somebody's cousin. Marion sat in the back with the Colonel's map case on her knees. It rained the whole way. She watched the black of the trees go by and the water standing in the fields like plates and thought about the fact that she had four days and the duplicate key in her hatbox at home and no way of using it until they got back.

"You are quiet."

"I am watching France do nothing."

"That is what France is for." He had his gloves off and was looking at his own hands. He was in an odd humour: he had been like a man carrying a parcel since the eighth, keeping something in view. "I was here in 1940. In June. I came up this road with the staff of the Sixteenth Army and there was a woman in the ditch with a birdcage and a clock, and she was walking south, and the clock was the wrong way up, and I have thought about her for three years." He turned his head. "The clock. Why would a woman run in a certain direction for three hundred kilometres carrying a clock and no food?"

"Because the clock was her mother's."

"Yes." He looked back at his hands. "That is what I decided."

The château had a west front and a park, and inside it was cold in the way that only French houses are cold, which is a political cold, a refusal. There was a drawing room with a parquet floor that boomed under the boots, a dining room with a table for twenty, and in the library, on the shelf where a book collector's glass case had stood before somebody took it in 1940, there were eight hundred volumes and a fire the size of a chair.

She was given a room in the east wing with the other women, and the Colonel was given a room in the west with the senior officers, and at eleven o'clock on the first night, after the bridge had ended and the last boots had gone up the stair, she walked the length of the corridor in her dressing gown with her shoes in her hand and came into his room and got into his bed, and it was the first time in five months that she had done it without being sent for, and it was, for the ninety seconds before he woke and understood, the most dangerous thing she had ever done.

He woke. He understood. He turned over in the dark and put his arm around her without a word, and she lay with her face against the shoulder of a man who had been at Verdun in 1916 and at Vyazma in 1941, and listened to the rain on the slate and the wood in the chimney making its small ticking conversation, and understood that she had just made an enormous deposit in an account she intended, eventually, to empty.

In the morning he said, "You will not be able to do that again. There is a Frau Oberst here, and she is the eyes of the garrison of Blois."

"I know."

"Then why did you?"

She lay in his bed with her hair loose on his pillow and looked at him, and told him, because it had the advantage of being true, and truth, in small quantities, in the right place, was better than any lie she had ever told.

"Because in Paris I am a woman in a flat that is not mine, and this is the first house I have ever been in with you where the door has a lock," she said. "That is all. I wanted one night in which I was not being watched while I was looked at."

He was quiet. Then he got up and did the thing she had not expected, which was to cross the room and fetch a chair and sit down by the bed with his elbows on his knees, in his bare feet, in the cold, and talk to her for an hour.

---

What she received that morning, on the second day, was the inventory of a man.

He had been born in 1890 at a garrison town in Pomerania, the son of a colonel and the grandson of a man who had been ruined by the building of a railway. He had married Anneliese Vorbeck in 1919, in a church with a leaking roof, because the war was over and everyone she knew was dead, and for seventeen years they had been, he said, in his flat voice, "content. It is a word you will not like. Content." She had miscarried twice, in 1921 and 1924. In 1936 she had died of a cancer of the stomach in a clinic in Berlin over a period of four months, during which he was in a garrison in Silesia and was permitted to visit on Sundays. On the last Sunday she had asked him to take off his tunic and lie on the bed with her, and he had lain there in his shirt with his boots on the coverlet, and she had told him a joke about a priest and had fallen asleep holding his hand. He had sat with her body for forty minutes before he told the nurse to fetch the doctor. He had not, at any point in the four months, been able to say anything to her that he would have wanted to be the last sentence.

He was in Russia in 1941 and again in 1942, at Vyazma the first time, and the second time in the Crimea, at the siege of a place whose name he did not say; and in the second winter he had been in charge of a sector in which, over eleven days, four hundred and ten of his men had frozen and fourteen officers had shot themselves, including a major of thirty-two who had been his student at the war academy; and in the spring the order had come for the treatment of prisoners, and the order had come to him, personally, by hand, from a General he had known at Cadet School, and he had passed it down the line without reading it.

"That," he said, "was the bad one. Not the guns. Fourteen men shot themselves and I wrote the letters to their mothers, in my own hand, with a pen, and it took me nine days, and in the eleventh letter I wrote the word *courage*, and then I sat with the paper in front of me for an hour, and then I wrote *duty*, and then I posted it, and it was a lie. It was not courage. It was a piece of paper in a room down a corridor." He straightened. "You will wonder why I tell you this."

"I don't wonder."

"You should."

"I knew a man once who told a girl everything before he had decided what to do with her," said Marion Herschlag, who was lying, "and it did not end well for him. You are not that man. You are telling me the things you would say to a priest if you were able to imagine one, and you are able to imagine one, because you do not believe in anything else, and I am the priest, and you have known that since December." She reached out and touched his knee. "It is all right. I can hear it. That is the only thing I can do in the world, Colonel, and I do it well."

He looked at her for a long time, in the cold room, in his shirt.

"I know what you are," he said.

The rain came down on the west front of the château, and for one second — one second only, and she never afterwards forgot the exact slope of the light on the floorboards at that moment, and the eight sounds of the rain, and the fact that she heard no sound at all — Krystyna Stankiewiczówna lay in the bed of an officer of the Abwehr and felt her whole body become a single sheet of paper on which something was about to be printed.

"Tell me," she said.

"You are a woman from a rich house that was broken," he said, "and you had a mother who was the only thing in your life, and she died, and after she died a school, and after the school a marriage of eleven months to a man who was killed, and then a couture house, and then a man of fifty-four who feeds you, who is not the worst man in the Reich, and who is going to die and leave you in a country where they do not know your name." He stood up, and crossed once to the window and back. "And you have decided not to be frightened of me, and you have succeeded, which nobody else in this house has managed; and it is my opinion, madame, that you have never in your life told me the truth about anything that mattered, and that it does not matter, because you are the only person in France who does not lie to me about the war."

She lay in the bed and breathed, and the entire weight of the last four months hung by one nail, and the nail held, because five months in this house had made her very good at nails.

"There is a difference," she said, "between a woman who lies and a woman who is careful. You have a wife in a photograph and a war on the eastern front and a list in your briefcase, and there is no room in that for anything that is mine. So I keep what is mine in a small room at the back of the house, and I do not lie about it. I just do not take you there."

He came and sat on the edge of the bed and put his hands on either side of her face, and for a moment she thought he was going to kiss her forehead, which he had never done, and she braced herself for it in the way you brace for a blow, and what he did instead was to look into her with his pale eyes and say, in the voice of a man reading out a sentence of a court: "Then I shall not ask you to."

---

On the third day they shot.

There were beaters from the village and two farms; there were three drivers and a boy of twelve who had been given a stick; there were fourteen shotguns and one professional, an Oberstleutnant of the Luftwaffe who had shot in Silesia before the war and shot calmly now, with a small head movement and a sound like a door closing in the next room. The Colonel shot badly and did not mind. His leg was bad in the wet, and after the first drive he stood at the edge of the wood with his gun broken under his arm and watched the others go into the second field, and Marion stood beside him in borrowed boots and a coat that had been the wife's of a French manufacturer, and eighteen metres away, in the turnips, a young woman in a headscarf was looking at her.

That was the thing about the third day, and it was the thing she thought about afterwards for years, in Warsaw, when the city was going up.

There was a woman of about her own age at the edge of the field with a rabbit in each hand, and she had been one of the beaters, and she was standing in the rain at eighteen metres looking at Marion Herschlag with an expression of pure, unmodified, bottomless contempt. Not hatred. Hatred is a thing between equals. This was the look of a woman from a village twelve kilometres south of Blois who had a rabbit in each hand and was watching a Frenchwoman in a good coat standing in a wet field with the men who had taken everything, and who had, in that look, already written Marion off: not as a traitor, which would have been a kind of respect, but as a thing that had happened to France, like the rain.

Marion made herself not look away. She stood in the wet field with the Colonel's elbow in her hand and looked back at the woman with the rabbits, and she did it deliberately, because she had learned in Kraków that the one thing that must never happen is the flinch: *the flinch is it, my little rabbit. Any man can bear to be hated. What a man cannot bear is to be seen to deserve it.*

The woman turned and went up the field with her rabbits. After eleven metres she spat.

"What is it?" said the Colonel.

"A woman looking at me."

"For what?"

"For a Frenchwoman on the arm of a German," said Marion, with her mother's face, "in a village where the men are dead."

He turned and looked at the woman's back with an interest that was almost professional.

"Do you know what I would do?" he said. "If the war ended tomorrow, and they held a tribunal, and I stood in front of it, I would be hanged twice: once for the rifles, and once for the shoes." He held out his arm. "Come. They are beating the second wood, and if I stand here any longer the Luftwaffe will have all the birds, and I have not shot a pheasant since 1939 and it is beginning to look like a policy."

---

That night, after dinner, he took her into the east wing.

The room was at the end of the corridor, beyond the room where the wives slept, in the tower; and it was not a room that was used, and it was cold enough that she could see her breath, and it had been the bedroom of the last woman of the family, and the Wehrmacht had not touched it, because there is no point in requisitioning a bedroom in a tower when there are forty bedrooms downstairs.

There were two wardrobes standing against the wall, and the Colonel opened the first one, and inside it, hanging in a long row under a sheet, were forty years of a woman's clothes: a ball gown from the twenties with the beading gone dull, a riding habit, a set of summer dresses in a blue that had faded to the colour of milk, a Russian fur with the shoulders rubbed bare, a black afternoon dress with jet buttons, and, on the left-hand end, four things that belonged to a different shape of woman entirely: a plain grey suit, a skirt, a blouse, and a dress.

He stood in front of the open wardrobe with his hands at his sides.

"Anneliese had the blue ones made here," he said. "In 1934. She had them made by a woman in Blois and then she had them copied in Berlin because she said the French cut was a lie that was worth paying for."

"Colonel."

"Put on the dress."

She looked at him. He was not looking at her; he was looking into the wardrobe the way a man looks into a well he used to draw water from.

"Which one?"

"The one at the end. The grey one with the belt." And then, because he was, in his own estimation, a man who did not make mistakes, and because he had told her in Paris that he would not ask her to open the small room at the back of the house: "I want to see it. That is all. I want to see it on somebody, in a light, on a cold night, in the last year of the war, before it goes into a crate with the silver and the clock."

She took the dress off the rail. It was wool, and it was good, and it was hers to take, in the way that everything in Paris had been hers to take for five months; and she went behind the screen in the corner of the cold room and took off her own clothes, and put on the dress of a dead woman, and it fitted her, which was the worst of it; and she came out from behind the screen and stood in the middle of the room with the candle-lantern on the floor between them, and the Colonel of the Abwehrstelle Paris looked at her for four seconds and then put his hand over his eyes.

"Say my name," he said.

"Friedrich."

"Not that one." His voice was perfectly level, and his hand was over his eyes, and she could see a man of fifty-three standing in a cold room in a house that was not his, in a war he had begun to lose, at the end of an evening in which he had drunk four glasses of a wine that had been the château's before it was his. "The other one. Only for a minute. Only while the light is on the floor."

There is a place in the mind of a woman like Krystyna Stankiewiczówna where the arithmetic stops. It is not a moral place. She had walked past the placards in the rue de Rivoli with her hand on the arm of the man who signed the order and she had not stopped there. She had knelt on the floor of a bedroom with her thumb on a wax key and a candle the size of a coin and she had not stopped there. She had put a boy of twenty-three on a train of words and had gone home and asked an orderly about his son in Smolensk, and she had not stopped there.

She stood in a cold room in a house in the Loire with a dead woman's dress on her body and looked at the man who had told her, in a bed at dawn, that his wife had died holding his hand, and who had never once in seventeen years been able to find a sentence for her; and she understood that what he was asking for was not degradation. It was absolution. And she understood, in the same second, that she was going to give it to him, and that she would give it to him because it cost her nothing, and that the fact that it cost her nothing was the most frightening thing that had happened to her since October, because it meant that whatever she had been when she came to Paris, she was not that any more.

"Anneliese," she said.

He came across the room and took hold of her. He did it with his mouth and his hands and the whole weight of him, and it was not like the bed in the avenue Henri-Martin, where he took her as a man signs a document; this was something older and worse, and it went on for a long time, in the cold, against the end of a bed in a room in a tower with the candle-lantern burning down on the floor, and he said the dead woman's name twice and her own false name once, in that order, which she would remember for the rest of her life; and when it was over he lay on the bed with his arm over his eyes, and cried, without any sound at all, the way a man cries who has not done it since he was a child, and his shoulders shook and his hands stayed flat on the counterpane, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, in a dead woman's wool dress, lay beside him in the dark with her eyes open and did not touch him, because she had done everything else and that was the one thing she could not do.

At three in the morning she got up and went back to her own room in the east wing with her own clothes over her arm, wearing a coat that belonged to a Frenchwoman, and stood in the corridor for a while listening to the wind over the limes, and then went into the bathroom at the end of the passage, which had a basin and a jug of cold water, and washed herself all over with a sponge and a piece of soap that was grey with age, standing in the dark, going over every part of herself, and when she had finished she did the thing she had done in the rue des Rosiers, which was to file it, and she filed it under the place where the arithmetic stopped, and went to bed and slept like a stone.

---

They came back to Paris on the twenty-third.

On the twenty-sixth, a Hauptsturmführer of the Sicherheitsdienst came to the Hôtel Majestic on the avenue Kléber to audit the Abwehrstelle Paris, and his name was Werner Kroll.

He was forty-one years old. He was a small man with the shoulders of a swimmer and the spectacles of a schoolmaster, and he wore his cap slightly back on his head, and he spent three days going through the section from top to bottom, and he did it the way a man does a jigsaw, without any hurry and without any noise: he sat in a room with the files and the clerks and the duty rosters, and he asked three questions where other men asked thirty, and he wrote in a small notebook with green covers, and he did not raise his voice once.

On the twenty-eighth, at four in the afternoon, he interviewed the Colonel's household.

That was the word he used, the Colonel told her afterwards: *household*. There was Kleist, who was questioned for twenty minutes and came out grey and went straight to the kitchen and did not speak to anybody for the rest of the day. There was Ehlers, who was questioned for forty minutes and came out furious, in the way of a man who has been asked whether he has ever written anything down. And at five o'clock, at the end of the afternoon, in the small salon on the second floor with a table and two chairs and a window with the blackout on it, Marion Herschlag was interviewed for eleven minutes.

He stood when she came in, which she had not expected, and held the chair, which she had not expected either, and then he sat down opposite her and took off his spectacles and polished them on a handkerchief, and looked at her.

"Fräulein Herschlag."

"Madame."

"Of course. Forgive me. The forms here say Fräulein." He put the spectacles on. "Alsatian."

"By way of my mother."

"So I am told by everybody." He turned a page in his notebook, which was already full of writing, and she could not read it upside down, which was a small disaster and not a large one. "How long have you been in Paris?"

"Since the July before last."

"Why?"

"Because the money ran out in the east and my husband's family had a business in Paris, and the business was closed, and I had a friend who was a dressmaker." She said it in the flat, slightly bored voice she used for the story; she had told it to eleven people and it had never once been checked, because in Paris in 1943 everybody's story was checked only as far as the next queue. "That is it, Hauptsturmführer. I am not an interesting woman. I am a kept woman in the sixteenth arrondissement, and the only thing I know about the war is what the officers say over dinner, and they do not say anything over dinner except what they want the ladies to hear."

"Yes," said Kroll. "It is the same in Warsaw." He turned another page. "You were in the household on the eleventh of December."

"I do not know what I was doing on the eleventh of December."

"Then I will tell you. On the eleventh of December there was a reception at the Majestic for the officers of the Militärbefehlshaber, and you were at it, at the Colonel's table, in a green dress, and the Colonel gave a speech about the eastern front that was reported to Berlin." He looked at her over the top of the notebook. "I am not asking about the speech. I am asking about the twenty minutes between nine o'clock and twenty past nine, when the Colonel was in the courtyard with a General and you were in the corridor alone."

The room was quiet. She felt the little machine in her chest do its work, and she let it, and she did not fight it, because in Kraków she had learned that the body must be allowed to be frightened, in the place where the frightened thing cannot be seen: the hands, the inside of the wrist, the soles of the feet.

"I was in the corridor," she said, "looking for a lavatory, and then I was in the lavatory, and then I came out and the corridor was full of the wives, and I stood by the window for a while and looked at the blackout, and then I went back. That is the twenty minutes. It is not a good story. There is no story in it at all, and if you have a man who tells you that he saw me put something in a vase in that corridor, then your man is lying to you for money, which is what men do, and I would like to know his name so that I can ruin him."

Kroll did not answer for a moment.

"I do not have a man," he said. "I have a schedule."

"A schedule?"

"In a building like this, everything is written down: who came, who went, who was at table, who was on duty, who signed the coal, who signed the petrol. So when something goes out of this building that should not have gone out, I do not need a man with an opinion. I need a gap." He closed the notebook and stood up. "There is no gap in your twenty minutes, Fräulein. I have checked them twice. Nobody came into that corridor while you were in it and you were seen by three people. That is why I am here: not because you are suspected, but because you are *available*, which is a different matter, and always will be, until I have found the actual hole in the building." He put his cap on, and adjusted it, and looked at her with the eyes of a man who had spent eleven minutes doing a sum and had got a result he intended to keep. "Herschlag. Marion."

"Yes."

"It is a good German name." He opened the door for her. "I shall remember it."

She went out along the corridor, past the photograph of Anneliese that hung on the wall by the door to the Colonel's office, and down the stairs, and out into the courtyard of the Hôtel Majestic, where the staff cars stood in a row and a sentry in a wet greatcoat walked his eleven paces and turned.

Ehlers was waiting with the car. He opened the door; she got in; and as the car pulled out through the arch into the avenue Kléber, she looked back once, through the rain-smeared glass, at the great grey pile of the building with its eagles, and she saw him standing at a second-floor window with his hands behind his back, watching the gate, taking his time, doing his sums.

And she thought: *you have my name in your little green book, and I have your face in mine, and only one of us is lying; and you are going to come to Warsaw in the end, because they always send the man who keeps the notebook, and when you come I will be somebody's kept woman again, and you will open your book, and you will read the name, and you will not find me, because the woman in the book has no second name, and there is no such person in the world as Marion Herschlag.*

Then she put her head back against the leather and closed her eyes, and Ehlers drove her home through the blackout at eleven kilometres an hour, past the queues, past the shut shops, past the placards, and the wet streets of Paris went by her like the pages of a book she had already memorised.
