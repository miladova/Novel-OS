# Chapter 9: The Blue Palace

The reception was at the Blue Palace on the Senatorska, in the rooms of a nobleman who had died in 1936 in Nice, and it was given by the district administration in honour of the transfer of the intelligence section, and it was, in the four years of the occupation of Poland, the largest collection of German officers ever gathered in one room in the city of Warsaw.

There were two hundred of them.

They stood about in their grey with their caps under their arms, in a long enfilade of rooms with parquet floors and crystal and a ceiling painted in 1780 with four allegorical figures and the monogram of a family that had stopped existing; and the Wehrmacht drank a Hungarian wine that had been requisitioned from a cellar in Buda, and the officers ate from the buffet with their gloves in their belts, and the wives — there were forty wives — stood in a separate gravity in a separate room by the windows, with their furs on chairs and their shoes already wet.

Marion Herschlag wore grey, and no diamonds. That had been her decision on the Thursday morning, in the bedroom of the doctor's house, and she had made it for a reason that she had to explain to a Colonel for the first time in seven months.

"The earrings in Paris," she said, standing in front of the mirror with the little box open on the dressing table, "were the jewels of a man who took me out of a couture house and put me in a flat in the sixteenth arrondissement, and every woman in the room knew it, and it was my position: I was a woman whose necklace told the room her price. Here, they must not know my price. Here they must not think about me at all. So I will wear grey, and I will wear no jewellery, and the wife of the most junior captain in the room will be better dressed than I am; and if you want a woman who will be looked at, then take one of the secretaries, because in this city the business of being looked at is over."

He had listened to that with an expression she could not read, and then had said, "You are the strangest woman I have ever paid for," and had gone down to breakfast.

The wives room was the room she had to work.

That was the thing about the Warsaw of the German administration, and she had learned it in eleven minutes and would use it for a year: the men kept the files and the wives kept the *index*. No officer in the district told his wife anything; every officer in the district told his wife everything, at eleven at night, in the dark, in a bedroom where he had drunk a glass of something to sleep; and the wives met each other at eleven in the morning in the drawing room of the Frau Generalin with the district's gossip in their handbags, and the gossip was the only current intelligence in the city that had not been written down.

She stood at the window of the wives room with a glass of tea in her hand and let it go past her for two hours, and she learned the district that Thursday, and she did it the way she had taught herself in Paris: by counting nothing and noticing everything.

Frau Generalin von Rohrbach was sixty-one and came from Kassel and had a photograph of a son on the eastern front in a leather case in her bag, and she was not, Marion decided within four minutes, a stupid woman; she was a woman who had spent thirty-five years being an officer's wife, which is a different species of intelligence altogether, and she ran the wives the way a good colonel runs a depot: with a rota, a rota for the coffee, a rota for the church, a rota for the visits to the field hospital, and no gossip whatsoever about anything that mattered, because gossip about things that matter is a form of theft.

Frau Oberstleutnant Altdorfer was fifty and her husband was in the supply section, and everything she said about the district was in the language of a woman who was hungry: there was no butter, there was no coffee, there was no white flour, and the Poles had everything and hid it, and the black market was everywhere, and if one more Pole was shot for it she would not be surprised, and what was the city coming to.

Frau Doktor Weingart was thirty-eight and was the wife of the district medical officer, and she said nothing at all for two hours and looked at Marion twice, and each time she looked at her, Marion looked back with her mother's face, because she had learned in Kraków that there is no possible defence against a clever woman except to be exactly the thing she is certain you are.

And then there was Lotte Erler.

She was thirty-three, the wife of a Hauptmann of the motor transport office; she had been a legal secretary in Hamburg before the war and had a mouth that turned up at one corner and a way of standing with her weight on one hip and both hands around her glass; and she came across the room to Marion at the beginning of the second hour, and sat down on the end of a sofa, and said, without any preface at all: "You are the Frenchwoman."

"I am the Frenchwoman."

"Good. Then we can talk about something other than the winter. The winter is the only subject in this city and I have been here since 1941 and I have heard the winter described four hundred times, and I have come to the conclusion that the Poles are right about us." She drank. "The Führer has a plan for this city and the plan is: no one shall ever write a novel about it. That is what they say, anyway. Do you play bridge?"

"Badly."

"Good. Nobody plays bridge badly in this administration; everybody plays it like a general staff exercise. What do you do?"

That was the question. It came at her at eight minutes past seven in the evening on the twelfth of April, 1943, across a sofa in a palace in Warsaw, out of the mouth of a woman of thirty-three with a turned-up mouth and both hands round a glass; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna looked at her, and did the thing she had taught herself to do in the place where the arithmetic stopped, which was to answer the question with the truest thing that could be said and could never be used.

"I wait," she said. "I am a woman in the house of a man who is important, and I wait, and I am very good at it. I am so good at it that I have begun to be able to tell the time to the minute without a watch, and I know the schedule of this district, because I hear it, every evening, over the soup." She set her glass down. "And in Paris I had a shop and a cinema and a friend who made hats. And now I have a garden with two chestnuts in it in a city where a hundred and fifty thousand people have been put behind a wall, and I am invited by the wife of a general to drink tea, and I drink the tea, and I am a Frenchwoman from Strasbourg, and there is nothing in my life here that I can say out loud except this: I am extremely tired, and I am thirty-one years old, and I am going to be nothing for a year, and the being nothing is going to be the largest piece of work I have ever done."

Lotte Erler looked at her for a moment over the glass.

"Thirty-one," she said. "Yes. It comes as a great surprise, doesn't it."

"It does."

"Then let us be friends," said Lotte Erler, "because I am also extremely tired, and I am also going to be nothing for a year, and you are the first person in this city who has said anything to me that was not about the winter. But I want you to understand one thing first, madame, because I am a lawyer's daughter and I always explain the terms." She leaned over and set her empty glass on a side table with a small exact click. "I am not nice. I have a husband on the eastern front, and when he dies I shall be a widow of an Oberleutnant with a pension of a hundred and forty marks a month and no children, and I have already worked out that in this war the only people who will be in a position to help women like me, when it is over, are women like *you*: women who have been in the rooms, who have heard the names, who know which of these men are monsters and which of them are merely clerks. So I am going to be your friend, and I am going to be useful to you, and I am going to make a note, in my own head, of everything I learn about you — because that is what people like me do, madame, and you should know that about me now, in April, rather than in October."

"Then we shall be friends," said Marion Herschlag, "on the terms."

"Good." Lotte Erler stood up and smoothed her skirt and looked down at her with the turned-up mouth. "Also, the Frau Generalin has noticed that you are wearing no jewellery and that you are not stupid, and she has decided that those are the two most dangerous things in this room; and if you want my advice, you will go and stand in the far corner by the fireplace for the rest of the evening and look bored. Nobody watches a bored woman. It is the only thing I have learned in this administration that is worth anything at all."

The Colonel's study at the house on the Mokotowska had a desk, a bookcase, a safe, a telephone, and, from the end of April, a woman.

It was Ehlers who had delivered her, in the sense that Ehlers delivered the post, and the arrangement came out of the ether of the household in the way that most arrangements in a colonel's house come about; there was a mass of paper, and no clerk, because the clerks were at the Blue Palace, and a colonel of intelligence who worked at home in the evening did not want to telephone for a clerk at nine o'clock at night and be heard by the exchange, because the exchange in Warsaw was Polish.

So at the end of April she began to sort the Colonel's private papers.

It was, in the two years of her work, the single most useful thing that ever happened to her.

She did it at the desk in the study, in the evenings, in a blouse, with a lamp, and she did it *badly*, in a very particular way: she sorted everything into three piles instead of two, and she made a list of the contents of each pile on a separate sheet, and she used a pencil, and the pencil was the key to it, because a pencil can be rubbed out and a woman who is seen to rub something out is a woman who is seen to be honest; and every evening, when the Colonel went through her lists with a pen in his hand, he found the same three things: a competent woman; a small number of errors; and one item per night that had been filed in the wrong place and put back in the right one.

Within eleven days he had stopped checking the pencil.

Within three weeks he was dictating to her.

There is no document in the world so valuable as a dictation, because a dictation is a man's head, out of order, at speed, with his hesitations in it: *no, strike that; start again; the general says that the figure was three thousand and I know it was three thousand eight hundred, but the general is writing a report for Berlin and I have learned that a general writes a report for the man above him and the man above him writes a report for the man above him, and that by the time a figure reaches Berlin it has been polished like a stone in a river; put in 'three thousand.'* She typed it on the Colonel's machine with two fingers, the way a Frenchwoman from a couture house would type; and while her fingers were moving, her mind was standing behind them, in a room in the dark, laying out everything that had been said in the order in which it had been said; and in the evenings, in the small bedroom next to the Colonel's, in the bed of a doctor's daughter, she would take it out and go over it, item by item, the way her mother had made her do arpeggios in 1931.

She kept nothing. That was the discipline: no paper, no list, no note. But a woman who has been trained at the Conservatory in Warsaw has a memory for sequences that is not a human memory at all, and by the middle of May she was carrying in her head the whole shape of the district: the order of battle of the garrison, the names of the four men who ran the work of the *Abwehrstelle* Warsaw, the fact that the General telephoned Berlin twice a week from his bedroom and never from his office, the location of the anti-aircraft batteries, the schedule of the trains bringing the *Wehrmacht*'s mail, and one fact that she came across in the third week of May in a file of railway timetables with which she did nothing for a year and which, in the end, in the summer of 1944, she used to kill eight hundred men.

The flower woman was at the corner of the Nowy Świat and the Świętokrzyska every Sunday morning, in the door of a dairy that was shut, with a bucket of nothing in it, because in the fourth year of the occupation there were no flowers in the city of Warsaw except the paper ones.

The paper ones were beautiful.

That was the thing she had forgotten, in Paris: that the Poles had taken the German economy of grief and made it into an art, and the paper flowers were part of it: made out of the tissue from the German packing cases, dyed with beet and coffee, wired with the wire from the German barbed-wire; and a woman with a bucket of paper flowers at the corner of a shut dairy was, in the city, an entirely ordinary sight, and half the customers bought them to put on the graves at Powązki, which was the largest single activity of the Polish population of the city in that spring.

She came on the third Sunday of May, in accordance with the arrangement she had been given in Kraków in 1940: the second and fourth Sundays, the woman in the black shawl, the phrase that was never written down, the refusal of the flowers, and the glass of water.

There was no woman in a black shawl. There was a girl of seventeen.

She was standing in the doorway with the bucket, with her hair in a plait, in a dreadful coat, and she looked at Marion Herschlag and her face did not change, because the face of a girl of seventeen at a corner in Warsaw in 1943 was not a face that changed for anything; and Marion stopped and looked at the flower bucket, and said, the way she had been taught: "Only in June."

"The woman with the shawl is my aunt," the girl said. "She has been at Oświęcim since February."

The the street went on around them: a tram, a queue, a boy with a handcart of firewood, two German soldiers looking in the window of the dairy because the window of the dairy was directly in their path, and the girl held out a paper flower and said, in the voice of a girl selling a paper flower, "Three zloty," and Marion gave her four and took the flower, and the girl said, in the same voice, without any pause: "The Fox's people say you are to do nothing. Nineteen more Sundays. Then a man will come with a book. He will ask you for a light, and he will have the book, and you will not talk to him in the street; you will walk to the church of the Visitation in the second week and he will be there, and after that it is his business and not mine." She took the four zloty and made change out of a purse, seventeen groszy, in the correct manner, with her tongue between her teeth, and she said, "It is a bad flower. Do not put it on a grave; the wire is the only real thing in it."

She got the change. She walked away with the paper flower in her hand, past the two German soldiers, who looked at her the way they looked at everything in the city, which was with their attention and their knowledge that no act of attention was innocent; and she went home along the Nowy Świat with the flower in her hand and her heart going like a bird in a box, and half a block from the house she stopped, and looked at the paper thing in her hand, and understood what it was.

Because she had given four zloty and it was in her hand, and any woman in the street could buy a paper flower, and so could any German: so it was nothing. It was nothing at all. The whole of a city was in the habit of buying the thing that was nothing, and the resistance of Poland was in the habit of putting the thing that was nothing into the hands of a woman in a good coat in front of two soldiers at eleven in the morning on a Sunday; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna walked home with a paper flower, and put it in a glass on the windowsill of the small room next to the Colonel's, and left it there, on the sill, in the sun, until it faded, and the Colonel saw it a hundred times and never once asked about it, because a woman in the house of a man who has been given a district needs a small useless thing in a glass on a windowsill, and that is the only invisible place in the world.

On the last day of May there was an inspection, and the district was in a fever from seven in the morning, and the house on the Mokotowska had two extra men in it for a whole day: a Hauptmann of the rail section and an Oberleutnant who had been at school with somebody and had come to Warsaw with the inspection from Kraków, and Anna Zaleska cooked for seven hours, and the Colonel, who was in his study with the door shut, telephoned the Blue Palace four times before eleven o'clock.

At four in the afternoon, with the house empty and the men still at the Palace, Marion Herschlag stood in the Colonel's study in front of the safe with the door open.

Not to open it. She never touched it in the first month; that was a discipline and she kept it. She stood in front of it with a duster in her hand, which was a woman doing the dusting, because Anna had been in the kitchen since dawn and the Fräulein had decided, in the way Frenchwomen in the German administration's houses did, that she would do the study herself; and she stood there looking at the door of a safe of the Organisation Todt pattern, made in 1939, with the manufacturer's plate on the front and a combination dial and, behind the dial, in a place that a man with a duster would be standing exactly in front of, the small brass plate with the serial number.

The plate had a serial number stamped on it in six digits, and she had seen the serial number on the safe in Paris, and she had seen, in the third week of May, in a file of procurement papers that went through her hands on the desk, a list of the serial numbers of Organisation Todt safes in the Warsaw district together with the numbers of their combinations, written by a procurement clerk in Berlin with a copy to the district administration, in a table, in a document concerning *Beschaffung von Büromaterial*.

*Office materials.*

She stood with the duster in her hand in front of the safe in the fourth year of the war and read the serial number off the brass plate, and put it into the long room at the back of her mind where the sequences went, and went on dusting.

That night, when the Colonel came to bed at midnight, grey with the day of the inspection, he found her sitting up with a book and the lamp on, in a nightdress, with her hair loose, and he stood in the door for a while and looked at her.

"You have been at home all day."

"I have been at home all day, and I have dusted your study, and I have had my tea with the Frau Doktor, who is a woman with the eyes of a person in a stationary shop; and I have heard from Lotte Erler that the wife of the Oberst in the supply section is going to have a baby in the autumn, which is a certainty because the Poles in her kitchen say so, and the Poles in a kitchen know everything that the German administration knows and one thing that it does not." She turned a page. "And I have been thinking about your garden."

"My garden."

"The chestnut is going to die. It has a fungus at the base of the trunk, and it will be dead in two years, and it is the only good thing about coming home to this house." She looked up from the book. "Have it cut in the autumn, and put in a walnut or nothing at all. Piotr will tell you the same thing if you ask him in Polish, which you cannot, so I am telling you in French."

He came over and stood at the end of the bed and looked down at her for a long moment, with the district in his head and the day of the inspection behind him and the wall three streets away, and he said, in German, in the voice that belonged to a bedroom and not to a district:

"There was a moment this afternoon, in the Palace, when a Hauptmann from Kraków said something to me about the Poles, and I thought: she would have said the exact opposite, and she would have been right, and there is no one in the east I can say that to except the wall of my own study."

"Then say it to me."

"No." He sat down on the edge of the bed and began to unlace his boots. "The garden is a good thought. I shall have Zaleski do it. And the safe in the study — there is a new combination on it since yesterday, and I have written it on a piece of paper in my wallet, and the wallet is on the chest of drawers, and you are lying in my bed in a nightdress, and both of us are going to go on being extremely careful with each other, because that is the only kind of trust that lasts, and I intend for this to last."

He got into bed, and put his arm over his eyes, and was asleep in four minutes, which was a man's ability to be careful with a woman: four minutes, and a sentence he thought was a joke.

The building of the command of the district was a house of four doors and two clocks.

She learned it on the fourth day of the month of June, on a Thursday, because the ration cards of the household of the General of the district were in the office of the administration, and the woman of the house of a General of the German army in Warsaw went for them in the Mercedes, with Ehlers at the wheel and a paper of the district in her bag, at eleven o'clock in the morning, in the way of a piece of furniture being moved from one room to another.

The first door: the guard room, where a Feldwebel of the military police and two men sat behind a table with a ledger of the visitors and a clock on the wall above their heads, and the clock was the German clock, and it was five minutes fast, and it stayed five minutes fast for the fourteen months, because the sergeant of the guard had set it in the month of October 1939 and nobody in the administration had ever been able to give him an order about it.

The second door: the corridor of the ground floor, which was given to the administrative section of the district, and where the walls were painted the colour of the cream of a hospital and there were eleven rooms on the left side and a wall of the pigeonholes of the internal post on the right, and there were two officers of the administration in the corridor who looked at her for four seconds and then looked away, and the wife of the medical officer of the district, who was coming down the corridor with a paper of her own, stopped and said good morning and took her by the elbow to the window and gave her the social law of the place in eleven sentences:

that the wives of the administration did not come to the building except on the second and fourth Thursdays; that the wives of the military of the district did not come at all; that the wives' table was at the house of the Frau Generalin of the administration on the Ujazdowskie on the Tuesdays; that it was understood in the district that the woman of the General of the intelligence service was a foreigner; that in the four years of the war there had been exactly one other Frenchwoman in the administration, the wife of a transport officer of the rue Rakowiecka, and that she had gone to the west in the year 1941 and had not been seen since; and that, on the whole, and speaking as a friend of the profession, the wife of the medical officer of the district thought that a woman of the household of the intelligence service ought to come to the wives' table on the second Thursday and sit in the window and say nothing at all.

The third door: the library of the old palace, which the district had made into the registry, and where there were two sergeants of the office, a Pole of the labour administration in a green eyeshade at the far desk, and a great many shelves; and where the papers of the district of Warsaw of the year 1943 went in and out of the wire baskets under the hands of men who had spent the war being accurate about the paper of a continent, and where a woman of the household of a General waited for eleven minutes for the ration cards and saw, in the eleven minutes, the size of the machine she had been put down inside: the baskets of the incoming and the outgoing, the ledgers of the numbers, the carbon copies of everything that the district was going to do to the population of the city of Warsaw.

The fourth door was at the end of the corridor and it was the door of the office of the General of the intelligence service, and she did not go through it, because a woman of the household does not go through that door in a building of the district; and on the wall beside it, at the height of the chest of a man, there was a plan of the district of the city of Warsaw under the glass of the frame, with the quarters of the city in four colours and the German administration marked out in red over the colours, and the name of the street of the Mokotowska on the south side of the red, and the quarter of the ghetto in a black cross-hatch at the north of the whole plan of the city, and the river along the east of it.

She stood in front of the plan for four minutes, in the noise of the corridor, with the ration cards of the household of the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw in her bag, and did the thing that she had been trained to do with a room since a winter in a house in Kraków, which was to put the plan into her head in one piece and in the order of the colours: the quarters, the streets, the bridgehead of the Praga on the far side of the river, the belt of the railway, the four red squares of the German administration, and the eleven German houses of the officers of the district that she was able to name from the wives' table.

Then she went out of the fourth door of the building of the command of the district and down the steps to the car, and Ehlers opened the door of the Mercedes, and the clocks of the building of the district of Warsaw were both behind her: the German clock on the wall of the guard room, five minutes fast, and the other clock, the one that was not a clock, and that she had been listening to since she came down out of the station in May, which was the city of a million people of Warsaw going about its day with its face down, in the way of a population that has agreed to be five minutes behind the administration in everything that can be seen and in nothing that can be counted.

She did not write down one word of the plan of the district. She did not need to, and there was nobody to give it to for eleven days, and she spent the way home in the car looking out at the streets of the German side of the city of her own childhood and making the inventory of the ruins that were in the plan and the ruins that were in the street, and the difference between the two of them was the instrument by which she had decided to measure her war.

She lay in the dark and listened to him breathe, and thought about the fact that in the house there was one man who had taken a district from the east and put it in a safe in a study, and one woman who had a serial number in her head, and one paper flower on a windowsill, and nineteen Sundays, and no one, in the city, who was permitted to know that the woman in the small room had arrived.
