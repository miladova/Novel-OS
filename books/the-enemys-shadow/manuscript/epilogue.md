# Epilogue: The Hour

The papers came on the third of October 1944, in the evening, in the cellar of a burned building behind the house that had been the furier's, in the hands of a colonel of the cavalry of the army of Poland.

She had been in that cellar for three days with eleven Poles of the Mokotów and a woman of seventy-eight and a child of about four whose mother had gone out of the city in the first column and had not come back. Twice in the three days she had gone up into the streets. The first time was at noon on the third, when she walked the four hundred metres to the ruin of the house on the Bagatela and stood in what had been the marble hall, and then in what had been the study, and looked at the wall where a safe of the Organisation Todt pattern of 1939 had been set in the plaster: the plate with the serial number was still in the wall, and the dial was gone, and the floor above had come down into the room below, and the room smelled of wet ash. She stayed four minutes, and then she took away with her a piece of the plaster with the paint of the study on one side, and kept it in the pocket of her coat through the winter, and lost it somewhere between Pruszków and Grodzisk, and never knew how.

The second time she went out was at four in the morning of the fourth. She went through the wall at the broken brick course and across the yard, which was a plain of wet ash with nothing standing in it but the boiler in the middle of the floor of the laundry, and she went down on her hands and knees in the wet and put her hands into the rubble where the third door had been. The page of the card index of the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw — eleven surnames of the population of the city, four ticks against four of them, one line in the hand of a general of the German army — was still in the floor. It was soaked through and wholly readable. She put it inside her coat, against her ribs, and carried it out of the city of Warsaw in October 1944; and it was in a tin box under a floorboard in a farmhouse in the province of Łódź in the winter of 1945, and in a flat in the Praga in 1946, and in a drawer of a flat on the Solec at the hour in which this book is written.

Colonel Stefan Wilczek came through the garden wall at nine o'clock that evening, in a greatcoat of the Polish army of 1939 with the badges taken off, with a pistol and a packet of papers, and stood in the light of a candle at the edge of the cellar with the eleven Poles and the child and the old woman looking at him, and said:

"Which of you is the woman of the German general's household?"

Nobody answered. He looked around the cellar in the candlelight for a moment, and then he looked at a woman of twenty-seven in a dark coat in the corner, and he spoke in the ordinary voice of a colonel of cavalry in a cellar in October.

"Krystyna Stankiewiczówna. I was in your father's library in June 1936. I have been in the underground of this city since 1939. There is not a man in the rising of Warsaw who is ever going to know your whole name, and there are four men in this city tonight who know what you have done, and three of the four are going to be dead before January." He held out the packet. "These are yours. A Kennkarte of the municipality of Warsaw in the name of Halina Wiśniewska, of the village of Grodzisk, thirty-one years of age; a card of the labour office; a baptismal certificate from the parish of the Holy Cross; and a ticket of the civil passenger traffic of the district of Warsaw for October 1944. None of it is a forgery. It is the paper of a woman who lived in this city — thirty-one years old, in a cellar on the Dzielna on the fifth of August — and every other person who was in that cellar is dead, and there is no human being in the world who will ever be able to say that she and the woman who walks out on the Grodzisk road on the fifth of October are not the same person."

She took the packet.

"Colonel. There is a boy of twenty from the Praga. His name was Janek and I do not know his surname, and he is lying in the middle of the Przejazd with his own cap over his face, and his Kennkarte is in the pocket of the coat of a woman of the general's household. There is a mother somewhere in the Praga who will be in the column on the fifth of October, and she is never going to be told, ever, by anybody, and I am the only person in Warsaw who is able to tell her, and I cannot do it, because I am going to be in the middle of a road with an old woman on one side of me and a child of four on the other, under a name that is not mine, and there is not one thing I can do about it. I want somebody to know that I know it."

"There is nothing to be forgiven," said Colonel Stefan Wilczek of the 21st Lancers. "There is the arithmetic and there are the dead, and no one in the history of Poland has ever got the two of them into the same column."

---

She went out of the city at four o'clock in the morning of the fifth of October.

It happened the way these things happen: eleven thousand people in a road five abreast, with the bundles and the prams and the old people in the carts, and the German military police at the mouth of the street with their lists, and the columns going west out of the city in the dark; and the city behind them was burning, so that for the first three hours there was a light over the road, and the people of the column walked out of the middle of that light in the ordinary way of people who have been in the fire of a city for two months.

They were stopped four hundred metres outside the line of the city at a block of the field police. The column was counted. The papers were checked under a lamp in the middle of the road, and the line stood in the dark and waited its hour.

Wilczek was forty metres in front of the woman with the Kennkarte of Halina Wiśniewska, at the head of the column, with his men around him in the greatcoats with the badges off, because there is no such thing as a rising that does not have a soldier at the end of it. There was a German of the field police at the barrier with a blue policeman of the city beside him, and a lorry of the army at the side of the road with its engine running.

At half past four in the morning a sergeant of the military police looked at the Kennkarte of a woman of thirty-one from Grodzisk for four seconds under the lamp, and looked at her face for four seconds, and handed the card back, and said, in the German of a man from Essen in the fifth year of the war:

"Es ist in Ordnung. Weiter."

And it was in those four seconds, behind the woman in the lamplight, at the head of the column, that a machine gun of the escort party at the side of the road fired eleven rounds into the group of men in greatcoats. An officer of the army in the lorry had been watching that group for eleven minutes and had said a word about greatcoats with the badges taken off. The men at the head of the column ran four ways. The column went down onto the road the way a column does, and the child of four on the right hand of the woman with the Kennkarte went down with it and was not hurt.

They carried Wilczek into the ditch.

He lived eleven minutes. He said three things in that time, in the ordinary voice of a colonel of cavalry with his own blood on his tunic, in a ditch at the block on the Grodzisk road at five o'clock in the morning of the fifth of October 1944.

"There is a paper in the inside pocket of the coat, for the men who come after the war. The men of the east are going to be in this city in January 1945, and not one of them will ever look at the paper of a woman from a German general's household.

"Frau. The wallet. Inside pocket.

"Tell them at the head of the column that no man of the underground of Warsaw goes into the column of the west. The people of this column are the people of the city. There is not a rifle among them. A rising of a column on the Grodzisk road is a dead man's arithmetic."

He died at eleven minutes past five, and the column went on west along the road, and a woman of twenty-seven with a dead woman's Kennkarte in her coat and a colonel's wallet against her ribs walked in the middle of it for eighteen kilometres to the transit camp at Pruszków, with an old woman on one side of her and a child of four on the other.

---

She was in the camp at Pruszków for two days.

It was a factory of the district administration with great halls, and there were something like a hundred thousand people of the city of Warsaw in it in the second week of October 1944, and they were registered, and sorted, and put into the trains: the people of Warsaw went out of that factory towards the Reich in covered wagons and towards the west of the Generalgouvernement in open trucks, and none of it was disorder. It was an office of a state in the fifth year of a war, doing its work.

On the first evening in the camp she saw the woman.

She was standing in the queue for the evening soup with the child of four on one side of her and the old woman on the other, and the queue was moving forward under the lamps of a factory hall; and eleven metres away, in the queue for the German staff of the camp, there was a woman of about forty in a good coat of the winter of 1942, with a suitcase at her feet.

It was the wife of the medical officer of the district of Warsaw. In July she had been writing to her sister in Stuttgart about the hopelessness of the German position in the east, and her letters had been in the green notebook of a Hauptsturmführer of the Sicherheitsdienst in the aleja Szucha, because a woman of the household of a general of the district's intelligence service had said her name out loud at a table in a room in Paris nineteen months earlier, and had put the sentence in the notebook's column, and had done it for money that was not money.

The woman looked at her across the two queues for four seconds.

Then the wife of the medical officer of the district of Warsaw turned her head away and looked at the floor, and said nothing to the officers of her staff, and nothing to any man of the commission in that hall, and nothing to any human being for the rest of her life.

She left the camp on the third evening in a transport of eleven hundred people for the west of the Generalgouvernement. The transport went in open trucks to Żyrardów. At Żyrardów station on the fourth morning, three of the eleven hundred were on the list for the road to the village of Grodzisk: a woman of thirty-one years, a child of four, and an old woman. No man of the escort looked at them twice in the confusion of that station, and the three of them walked out of it on their own feet into the wood.

---

The winter of 1944 and 1945 she spent in a farmhouse in the district of Grodzisk, eleven people in two rooms, a cow, a well, and a brick stove in the middle of the kitchen.

She worked. There was nothing else to be done: she milked the cow and carried the water and cut the wood and cooked for eleven people in the fifth winter of the war, and she was in the parish register as a woman of Grodzisk, thirty-one, a cook, and she wrote no letters to any human being, and for the first month she did not go out of the house.

On the first of January 1945, in the evening, she sat by the brick stove with a piece of paper and a pencil and wrote eleven lines, and she read them twice, and then she put the paper in the fire and watched it burn. The lines were a statement of account, and they said: that the folder of the Fall **Wotan** had been in the hands of the rising's district since the ninth of August 1944; that the men and the armour of the trains had been at the Wilanów on the ninth of August and not on the ninth of September; that a page of the card index of the Sicherheitsdienst lay in the floor of the laundry in the yard behind the Bagatela; that a card with the name of a woman of Strasbourg on the front and four pencil words on the back was in the lining of her coat; that eleven letters of a nurse in Berlin were in the wall of a burned house; and that in the Pomerania there was a man of fifty-five in a house with a walnut tree in the garden, in a room at the top of the stairs, in a chair by the window, with the lamp turned low.

There was an item missing from the column, and she had known for four months what it was, and she did not write it down: that no letter had been written, and none was going to be, and that the chair by the window was a chair in the head of a man who was going to sit in it for the rest of his life, in a village he had never seen, on a morning at which she had never arrived.

She put the pencil down. She went out into the yard and stood in the dark and looked west, where the city of her birth was, and where the army of the Soviet Union was going to be in seventeen days.

---

She came back into Warsaw on the nineteenth of January 1945.

She came in on the western road, on her own feet, with a bundle of the farmhouse bread and a dead man's boots; and the road for the last eleven kilometres was one procession of the people of Poland walking east towards the city — the carts, the prams, the province of the Generalgouvernement coming home — with soldiers of the Polish army of the east among them in the uniforms of the Red Army, and sledges, and horses, and snow over the plain of Mazovia.

The city was a field of brick.

There is no description of it that any human being has written and none that is needed here; what the men of the German army did to the city of Warsaw in October, November and December of 1944 they did with engineers and with the police formations of the district and with four months in which to do it, and in January 1945 the streets of Warsaw were fields of brick and ash and snow with the walls of houses standing in them one and two floors high, so that the ruins were like the ribs of a whale in the middle of a desert.

She walked in it for four hours. She went from the western edge across the brick and the ice, past where the Marszałkowska had been, and where the Nowy Świat had been, and where the house on the Bagatela had been — a hole in the ground in the quarter of the Bagatela with a fragment of the study wall in it and a hole in the fragment where the safe had been, and snow over all of it.

He was standing at the corner of the Świętokrzyska in the middle of the afternoon of the nineteenth of January 1945.

There was a line of people at a well on the corner of the square, with buckets and cans and pots, waiting for water in the way the city waited for everything that winter; and he was at the end of the line, in a coat of a man of Warsaw with the collar up, in a cloth cap of the kind the country made, with a can in his hand.

She stopped four metres from him.

There were four seconds of silence.

And Phillip Brandt — of Warsaw, twenty-nine years old, who had stood in the Botanic Garden in June 1938 with a book of French poems in his hand — put the can down in the snow and said, in Polish, in the ordinary voice of a man of Warsaw in the fifth year of the war:

"Krystyna Stankiewiczówna."

---

They were married in the registry office of the Praga on the eleventh of April 1946, at eleven in the morning, and there were four people in the room: the registrar, who had been in the army of Poland in September 1939; the woman from the village of Grodzisk, who had kept the child of the camp column, the girl of four whose mother had gone out of the city in the first column of the October of 1944 and had not come back, and who was eleven years of the age of eleven in the year of the wedding and lived at the farmhouse at Grodzisk as the daughter of the woman who had kept her; and a young man of twenty-four named Bolek Stankiewicz, chemist's assistant of the Ząbkowska in the Praga, with a scar of September 1944 on his left arm.

There was the flat of two rooms on the Solec, in the Powiśle, with a window on the Vistula and an iron stove; and a bicycle, which had belonged to a girl in Posen in 1942; and a tin box of English biscuits under a floorboard, in which there were: a Kennkarte of a boy of twenty from the Praga with a photograph in it; a card of the administration of the district of Warsaw, four centimetres by six, with the eagle of the Reich in the top left corner and the stamp of the *Ausweis* across it and four lines of German type in the middle and four words of pencil on the back; a page of the card index of the Sicherheitsdienst of the district of Warsaw with eleven surnames on it and four ticks; a Minox of the pattern of 1941; and a photograph of a girl of twenty with a bicycle, taken in a suburb of Posen in the winter of 1942 with a camera borrowed for the afternoon.

And in the four years of that flat she told the man everything — the corridor, the safe, the wax and the keys, the milliner, the priest, the woman with the horse, the boy with the bandage, the price of the paper she had given to the district of the rising, the sentence about the cap — everything, in the flat, with the door shut, in the way of two people who have decided that the war is not going to be a locked room between them.

Everything except one thing. And the one thing was the yes: the yes she had said in the room on the first floor of the house on the Bagatela at nine o'clock in the evening of the thirtieth of August 1944, with a hand on a railway warrant and a man asking her about a walnut tree and a chair by a window. It had not been an arithmetic. It had not been a performance. It had been the only thing in the four years that had belonged to nobody but her, and she kept it, and never spoke it, and it was still in her at the hour in which this book is written.

---

On the fourteenth of September 1951 a letter came to the flat on the Solec from the Federal Republic of Germany by way of the tracing service of the Red Cross, in the hand of a woman of sixty-eight, and it said:

*I am the sister of General Friedrich von Reuth of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw, and I have been looking for a woman of Strasbourg named Marion Herschlag since July 1945. There are two hundred and eleven women of that name in the registers of the west, and not one of them was in Warsaw in 1944.*

*My brother did not die in the war. He was in the west in 1945, in English hands, and from 1946 he lived in a village in Lower Saxony, and he died there on the fourteenth of March 1951, at eleven o'clock in the morning, in the room at the top of the stairs, of a disease of the heart, aged sixty-two. There is a clause in his will of July 1950, sworn before a notary at Hameln, leaving eleven thousand marks of the money of the German Reich to Marion Herschlag of Strasbourg; the bank was in Breslau and there is not a pfennig of it left in the world; and the clause is in his own hand and in his own signature, and the notary read it out in the presence of myself and of one woman of the village.*

*And there is a second matter, which I set down because I am sixty-eight years old and there is nobody else alive to whom it can be said. In September 1944 my brother telephoned me from the office of the district at Skierniewice, and he said one sentence, and then he said that he would never say it again, and he never did. He said: the woman is not in the column of the west; she is in the file, and I have looked into the eye of the file, and there was nothing in it; and I am not able to say this to any human being in the world.*

*I have one question for the woman who opens this letter. Was there ever a woman of Strasbourg named Marion Herschlag in the city of Warsaw in the years of the war? And if there was, and if she is holding this paper, then I want her to know that a man sat in a chair by a window in a room at the top of the stairs in a village in Lower Saxony for five years, and that the chair was empty, and that there was nobody in the room, and that he had been a general of the intelligence service of the district of the city of Warsaw, and that there is not one paper left anywhere in the world with the name of that woman on it.*

*Ilse von Reuth.*

She read the letter in the flat of two rooms on the Solec, standing at the window with the Vistula and the city beyond the glass; and then she sat down at the table and took a sheet of paper and a pen, and wrote, in German, to a woman of sixty-eight in Berlin, eleven lines: that she was not the woman of Strasbourg, that she had not been in Warsaw in the years of the war, that she knew nothing of any Marion Herschlag, that she was not able to help in the search for the brother's family, and that she wished her peace in the rest of her life.

And she did not send it.

---

She went to find Anna Zaleska in the spring of 1946.

It took a year and a half, and it took a year and a half because the addresses of the city of Warsaw in the year 1946 were not addresses but a set of directions given by a woman at a well: the office of the population of the district of the Praga on the Brzeska, where the registers of the year 1939 had been burned and the registers of the year 1945 were the letters of the people who had come back; the parish of St. Florian, where a priest of seventy with a bad memory kept the lists of the living and the dead of the Praga in four notebooks and where the name Zaleski appeared eleven times with four Christian names against it; and a courtyard on the Brzeska behind a workshop of the upholstery, where a woman in a headscarf sent her to the third floor of the house at the corner of the street of the Kijowska, and where there was a room of eleven metres with a window on the yard and a bed and a stove and a table, and a woman of fifty-three in the black of a widow with her hair grey and her hands the hands of a washerwoman.

Anna Zaleska looked at her for four seconds from the doorway of the room.

Then she said, in Polish, in the flat voice of a woman of the Praga at the end of the fifth year of the war: "The Fräulein has a coat of the German quarter of the city of Warsaw."

"That coat has been at the bottom of a well in the province of the Łódź for a year and a half," said Krystyna Stankiewiczówna. "I am asking whether I may come in."

"You may come in."

The room had a table, two chairs, a pine box, an image of the Mother of God over the bed, a lamp with a metal shade, and on the table a photograph of a man of about forty in the uniform of the Polish army of the year 1939: Piotr Zaleski, of the household of the house on the Mokotowska, gardener and chauffeur and odd man and the man who had come up the stairs of the house with a chicken in a paper in the month of the September of the year 1943 and had said nothing at all about the chicken, in a house where the chicken was a thing of the black market and the black market was a thing of the grave.

He had died on the fourteenth of September 1944 in the Praga, in the yard of the house of a sister on the street of the Brzeska, when a shell of a German battery on the far bank of the Vistula came over the river into the middle of the yard of a block of flats where there were eleven people drawing the water of the well and four children playing at a version of the war, and there was, in the forty years of the life of the man, exactly one thing that anybody ever afterwards said about him, and it was said by a woman of the third floor of the house of the corner of the Kijowska in the year 1946, in the voice of a person stating a fact of the weather: *Piotr Zaleski was a man who never said anything to anybody in his life, and he carried the water and the coal of the houses of the German quarter of the city of Warsaw for the five years of the war, and at the end of the fifth year of the war a shell of a German battery came up out of the river into the yard where he was standing, and there is not one person in the Praga who has ever been able to explain the arithmetic of the thing to the wife of the man.*

They sat at the table.

The two of them had, in the two years of the war, exchanged an average of eleven words a day, and those words had been about the soup and the linen and the trams, and neither of them had ever, in the two years, been in a room with the other without a third person present or a door open; and they sat in the room of the Brzeska with the afternoon of April on the yard and the window of the room open and the noise of the workshops of the upholstery coming up out of the courtyard, and the woman of the house said, first:

"He is alive?"

"He is alive. He was in the Wola on the first of August and in the sewers in the month of the September, and he was in the camp of the prisoners of war at Lamsdorf for the winter and he came back in the month of the March of the year 1945 on foot, and he is at the office of the reconstruction of the district of the Warsaw, and he is thirty years of age, and he has the two hands."

"His mother?"

"Dead in the year 1942, in the typhus of the district of the Praga, in the house of her sister."

"And the Bolek?"

"Chemist's assistant of the street of the Ząbkowska, with a scar of the month of the September of the year 1944 in the left of the arm of him, and he is to be married in the month of the June of the year 1946 to a girl of the district of the Żoliborz who was in the company of the girls of the rising of the city of Warsaw, and there is nothing more to be said about the boy."

"And the man of the General?"

She asked it in the way that a woman of the Praga asks a question about a person who has been in the house: without any preliminaries and without any pity, in the flat voice of the twenty years of the service of the German houses of the city of Warsaw.

"Alive, in the west, in the hands of the English, in a village of the province of the Lower Saxony, with a garden that he is not able to work and a pension of a general of an army that does not exist any more, and a sister in Berlin, and a chair by a window in a room at the top of the stairs of a house of a village, and a name that he is never going to be able to say out loud in the rest of the life of him."

Anna Zaleska nodded once.

Then she said the thing that she had kept for the four years, and she said it in the way of a person stating a fact of the weather, at the table, with the afternoon of the spring of the year 1946 on the yard of the house:

"The cousin went into the transport of the month of the March of the year 1943 and there was not one paper about the rest of the life of the man, and his wife has been in the province of the Kielce since the year 1944, and there is a boy of the age of seven years who is the boy of the cousin, and the mother has told the boy that his father went in the year 1939, and the city of Warsaw tells the boys of the city of Warsaw that the fathers of them went in the year 1939."

She took the photograph of the man of about forty into her two hands and put it, face down, on the table, the way a woman of a house puts down a plate she is finished with, and said the last of the exchange:

"In the two years of the house of the General of the district of Warsaw I asked the German's woman one thing, in the kitchen of the house, with the water running, and the German's woman said no to it in the Polish of the city of the two of us, and I have spent the four years of the war knowing the reason, and the reason was good, and the cousin went into the transport in the month of the March, and the two of us have been in the same city in the same war with the same account in the hands of the two of us." She got up and took the kettle off the stove. "There is no tea in the district of the Praga and there has been none in the year, so we shall have the water, and the two of us shall sit at this table and drink the water of the well of the house of the Kijowska in the spring of the year 1946, and after the water is drunk the Fräulein is going to be able to go out of the door of this room, and there is not one thing in the world that has been left over."

They drank the water.

And when she had gone out of the door of the room of the Brzeska and down the two flights of the stair and into the yard of the house of the corner of the Kijowska in the spring of the year 1946, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the middle of the courtyard of a house of the Praga for four minutes with her face in the sun of the month of the April, and understood that the account had been closed by the one person in the city of Warsaw who had the right to close it, and that the closing of it had been done in the ordinary Polish of the kitchen of a house of the Praga, at a table, with a glass of water, by a woman who had never once in the two years of the service of the German quarter of the city of Warsaw said one sentence to her about the war.

The hour is five o'clock in the afternoon.

It was the hour on the first of August 1944 in the city of Warsaw, and it is the hour in the city of Warsaw at the moment this last line is written: five o'clock, in October, in a flat of two rooms on the Solec, in the free country of Poland.

There are two hundred and eleven people in this city who know a part of the truth of the year 1944 and not one who knows all of it. There is a woman of thirty-four at the window of the front room with the street below her and the river beyond the roofs, and a man of thirty-five in the back room, and a daughter of four years on the floor of the front room, born in the second year of the peace, and a tin box of English biscuits under a floorboard, and a photograph of a girl with a bicycle on a shelf.

And at that hour — the hour at which a woman of twenty-seven once stood in a marble hall and heard the first shot of the rising of Warsaw come up out of the Wola — the woman at the window stops. It is a small thing, and nobody in the world has ever seen her do it: in the middle of a sentence, in the middle of a meal, in any room of any house in this city where she is able to be, at five o'clock in the afternoon of every day of the year, she stops for four seconds and looks at the window.

Then she goes on with it, the way the city of Warsaw went on with it. And the shadow of the enemy of 1944 is on the wall of that room at five o'clock in the afternoon of every day of the year, and there is nobody in the world who is able to see it but the woman in the room.
