# Chapter 20: The Quiet Ones

In the last week of July 1944, the city of Warsaw did the thing that a city does when it has been told, by its own sons, that it is about to be free.

It waited. It went to work. The trams ran on the Marszałkowska and stopped at holes in the track where the shells of 1939 had never been repaired, and the queues stood outside the shops with their coupons, and the German papers printed the verbs of consolidation, and on the bridges over the Vistula the columns of the German army went west in the ordinary way: the horse traffic and the hospitals and the carts of the paper. And under all of it the city was a machine, running: a hundred thousand people between the ages of sixteen and fifty, in cells of five, and each cell knowing nothing of the next, and it keyed, and oiled, and loaded, and waiting for a word that would come out of a room in the Mokotów district.

She saw it, in the last week, from the tram.

She rode the number 6 up the Marszałkowska on the twenty-fourth of July at four in the afternoon, in her Frenchwoman's coat, with a shopping basket, and watched the city at the last moment of the thing that had been its life for five years. Two Polish boys of seventeen were doing something at the corner of the Kredytowa with a bicycle whose frame had been cut in half and welded back; and a woman of sixty in an apron was standing on a doorstep with her arms folded, watching a German officer walk down the middle of the pavement; and at the corner of the Świętokrzyska a man of thirty in a worker's cap was reading a wall with his attention, in the way of a man reading the prohibited press; and a girl of about eleven was skipping, alone, on the pavement of the street of her childhood, with the rope going round and round, and the song she was singing was the one that the children of Warsaw had been singing for four years all through the streets:

*Sierpnia pierwszego, o piątej godzinie...*

She heard it through the window of the tram, at a distance of two metres, from a girl of eleven, in the open daylight, in the middle of a city in the fifth year of the German occupation.

*On the first of August, at the fifth hour.*

That was how she learned the date. There is no intelligence service in the world that can compete with a child's skipping song.

---

René Tissier came to the house on the Bagatela on the twenty-sixth of July at eleven in the morning, in the uniform of a Hauptsturmführer of the Sicherheitsdienst, with orders in his pocket and a neat little moustache and a curious, clever, exhausted face, and the cook announced him in German as an officer of the office of the district's liaison, and Marion Herschlag came down the marble stair of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw and stood in the hall and looked at the man who had bought her a book in the rue de Rivoli in March 1943.

He was fifty. He had been born in Lyon and had spent the twenties in Warsaw as a teacher of French and had gone back to Paris in 1935 and had made a paper out of other people's marriages; and he was standing in the middle of a German hall in a German uniform with his cap under his arm, and he looked at her the way he had looked at her in the foyer of the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, with his hands behind his back, smiling.

"Madame Herschlag."

"Monsieur Tissier."

"You are surprised."

"I am not in the least surprised. You have been a man with a paper and an ear for eleven years, and there is a war on, and there is a district of Warsaw, and there is one Frenchman in it who was in Paris in the same rooms as the German administration in 1943. I am surprised only by the uniform, and only because I did not think a man of your taste would find one that fitted."

He laughed, all the way through, for a long time, in the marble hall.

"Beautiful," he said. "Beautiful. I have thought about you, do you know that? Four hundred times. In Paris I sat in a room after the liberation of Africa and I listened to the Berlin wireless and I thought: the little actress's mouth is standing in the corner of a General's drawing room, and there is not one other human being in Europe who knows what she is." He put his cap on the table. "I am not a Hauptsturmführer. I am a civilian attached to the office of the liaison of the Sicherheitsdienst in this district; I have a rank because a man in this city who travels in a car without a rank is a man who is shot; I have been here since February; and I have known where you live since the month of April, because I read the register of the households of the officers, which is one of the things my office does."

"And what does your office want with a Frenchwoman from Strasbourg?"

"Nothing at all. My office believes that I am delivering you a document." He put a folded paper on the hall table: an order of the district, on German paper, concerning the evacuation of the civil administration. "That document is genuine, and it will be signed by the General's own hand when he comes back from Kraków on Sunday, and it will inform you that the administrative staff and their attached persons leave on the thirty-first of this month, and it will require you to be at the station at half past five in the morning."

She looked at the paper on the table.

"And you?"

"I have a pass for a place on the same train, which is worthless, because I have looked at the freight tables, madame; I have spent four months looking at the freight tables, which is what a civil servant in a uniform does in the last week of July. There is not going to be any train west of this city after the thirty-first, because the Russian aeroplanes are going to be over the bridges by then, and there are four hundred thousand German civilians in the east and eleven trains, and the arithmetic of it has been done in Berlin, and the arithmetic of it is that the German administration of a city that is about to rise is not going to be evacuated. It is going to be *abandoned*, and the people in it are going to be told that they are going to the west until very late in the day, and then they are going to be told that they are going to hold a fortress."

"You are telling me this for a reason."

"I am telling you this," said René Tissier, "because I want to be on the train that does leave, and there is exactly one train in the east that is going to leave this city in the first week of August, and it is a military train, and it is under the authority of the district's intelligence service, and I have been at some pains to find that out; and the woman of the General of the intelligence of the district is the only person in Warsaw who can put a man on it."

She stood in the hall of the house on the Bagatela and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was not about Tissier.

It was about the fact that the entire strategy of her life for five years had been to be nothing, and that the accounting had just arrived; and that René Tissier had been holding her name in his hand since November 1942, and was going to spend it now, in the last week of the war, in a marble hall, on a hot morning in July, in the form of a very small, very reasonable request.

"What, exactly, do you want me to do?"

"On the thirty-first," he said, "when your husband goes to the Blue Palace for the last conference of the district, you will come down to the yard with your coat and your case, and you will take the car, and you will go to the station, and there will be a man with a pass and a place in a carriage, and he will be me; and you will say one sentence to the officer at the train, which is: *this man is with the General's household.* And then you will sit down, and the train will go, and there will be two people of Paris in a carriage in the dark, going west, and it will be the end of it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I will do nothing at all," said René Tissier, "because I am not the man of a novel and I do not want a great deal; and if I do nothing at all, then I shall be a Frenchman of fifty in the city of Warsaw in the first week of August, and I shall be taken by the Poles or the Russians or shot by the German military police at a road block in the last day of the world, and there will be one less man with a newspaper." He picked up his cap. "But, madame, before you say the no, I want you to think of one thing, which is not a threat: I have known what you are since November of 1942, when I came up behind you in a corridor with a glass in my hand and said the name of a Polish family, and you went grey. In the nineteen months since then, I have not once said it, and I have not once written it. Do you know why?"

"Tell me."

"Because I am a man of fifty in a war who has never in his life been able to keep anything, and it is the only thing I have ever had." He put his cap on, and stood in the middle of the marble hall with his hands at his sides. "If you say no, madame, you will say it to the only person in Europe who has ever been able to keep your secret, and I want you to be aware of that, on the day, at the station, when you say it."

He went out through the front door of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, in the uniform of the office of the liaison of the Sicherheitsdienst, at twenty minutes to twelve on the twenty-sixth of July 1944, and the sentry at the gate saluted him because a Hauptsturmführer of the SD was a man whom sentries saluted; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the hall for a long time after the door had shut, with a genuine order of the district on German paper in her hand.

---

The Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska was in the drawing room on the Mokotowska with her case packed and a photograph in her lap.

She was sixty-nine, and she had spent the last week in the things that a woman of her class did in the last week of July 1944 in the city of Warsaw, and she had done them without any hurry at all: she had made the inventory of what was in the flat; she had given the piano to the parish; she had paid the deaf cook to the end of August, and she had sent her out of the city to her sister at Grodzisk on the twenty-second; and she had sat in the drawing room alone, in the chair that had been in the family since 1851, with the photograph of a man with a moustache in her lap, and had waited for the thing that was going to happen, the way one waits for a train that has been delayed in the snow.

At half past five on the twenty-seventh, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna came through the courtyard in the maid's coat, with a cabbage in a bag, the way she had gone to the Krasińskiego.

"They are going to do it," the Countess said.

"I do not know anything about it."

"Do not be tiresome with me, my child, on the last Tuesday of the world; I have been in this city since 1886, and I have been counting the German cars going west and the Polish boys coming down the street in pairs for a month; and there is not a woman at the market who does not know it, and there is not a German in the administration who has not written it in a report, and you, who have been in the house of a German General for two years, are going to tell me that you do not know anything about it." The Countess put the photograph down. "On the first of August, at five o'clock; that is what they are saying. I have it from the woman who does the flowers for the church."

She said it the way she said everything: with the flat, complete, unsentimental clarity of a woman who had been in the world for sixty-nine years and had buried a husband and a son.

"There is going to be no water and no electricity. The telephone will not work. There will be no bread for a fortnight, and there will be no medicine, and there will be a great many people in cellars, and I do not intend to be in one." She looked at the maid's coat, and at the bag with the cabbage in it. "I am going to the fathers of the Visitation. There is a house beside the church with a garden wall, and the superior has agreed to it, and there will be a room with a bed and a chair, and eleven other old women; and I have two hundred zloty and a rosary and a photograph, and I will be, God willing, alive on the fifteenth of September, or else I will be in the Powązki cemetery with my husband."

"Madame—"

"Do not." The Countess held up her hand. She was, in that year, the only human being who ever spoke to Krystyna Stankiewiczówna as though she were a person and not an instrument, and she never once, in the fourteen months, asked her a single question about her work. "I have one thing to say to you and one thing to give you, and then you will go, because you are in a maid's coat and my neighbours are Polish and it is half past five.

"The thing I have to say is this. There is going to be an hour on the first of August when every Pole in this city is going to look at every other Pole in the street, and the question that every one of them is going to ask in their own head, without ever saying it, is the same question, and it is: *what did you do?* And you, my child, are going to be standing in a German house, in a German General's clothes, with a German card in your bag, on the first of August at five o'clock in the afternoon, and there is not one man in this city who is going to believe the answer; and there is not one hero of the Rising who is going to make any room for you, this year or in the year 1950, and there is not going to be a stone in the city of Warsaw with your name on it.

"And I want you to know, before it begins, in this room, on the last Tuesday of July, that I know what you have done. There have been exactly four people in this city who have ever known it: a priest of the Visitation, a colonel of the cavalry, a captain in a German uniform, and an old woman of sixty-nine in a chair. Three of the four will be dead by the middle of September. So I am the accounting, and I am telling you: I know what you have done, and I have known since the month of November, and I have prayed for you every Sunday of the year, at the eight o'clock, in the second row, and a woman of my age does not go to the eight o'clock for nothing."

She stood, with some difficulty, and went to the secrétaire by the window, and took out of the drawer a parcel wrapped in paper, and gave it to Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, and said, in French, in the flat accent of a governess from Neuchâtel in 1887:

"The thing I have to give you is from your mother's drawing room. It is the one thing that Pelagia was not able to carry on the second of December 1939; the woman who had the flat after you took it out of the piano bench on the fourth of March 1940 and gave it to me, because on the fourth of March 1940 she was afraid of the world and there was nobody else in Warsaw to give it to. It has been in that drawer for four years. I do not know what you will be able to do with it, and I do not want to know; but it is yours, and there is no one left who has the right to keep it from you, and if you should be in this city on the fifteenth of September, and I should not, then I want you to open it in the ruins of your father's flat, and not before."

The parcel was the size of a book, and it weighed almost nothing, and it was warm from the drawer.

"Now go," said the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska. "And do not come back to this house; and do not kiss me; and in the rest of your life, when you are asked by anyone at all what you did in the war, I want you to say the only true thing there is to say, which is that you were a woman in a house, and that the house was not yours."

Ehlers broke on the twenty-eighth.

The news came to the district by way of the motor transport office at eleven in the morning: a raid on Posen, a hundred and eleven aircraft, the district of the north of the city, the street where his Marta had lived with her mother and her sister for the five years of the war.

He was in the yard when it came. She saw him from the corridor window of the first floor of the house on the Bagatela: a driver of thirty-four in a clean tunic standing in the middle of a yard with a piece of paper in both hands, and then going down on one knee in the gravel, in the ordinary way of a man whose legs have stopped being a thing that he owns; and the second driver of the district was with him in four seconds, and got him up, and took him into the kitchen.

She went down.

He was in the kitchen with his cap on the table in front of him and his hands flat on the cloth, and the German cook from Bydgoszcz was standing at the stove with her back to him, making a noise with the pans, because she had been in the administration for four years and had learned what the women of the country did when a man came into a kitchen with news.

"Ehlers."

"Gnädige Frau."

"The the street?"

"Four hundred metres," he said. "The letter is from my sister-in-law. My wife's mother is dead, and the sister has lost an arm, and the house is in the street, and there is nothing left of it, and there was a chestnut, and Marta's bicycle, and the piano that belonged to the family of the mother." He looked at his hands. "I have driven this car for the district for four years, and I have driven the General and the Obersts and the colonels of the Generalgouvernement, and I have never once had a thing in my hand that was mine, and I have had a photograph in the flap of my map case, and now the photograph is a photograph of a girl who does not have a street."

He looked up.

"And there is one more thing, Gnädige Frau, and I am going to say it now, in this kitchen, because the German administration of the district of Warsaw is going to be dead in a fortnight or in the street, and I have never once in four years said a thing to you that was not correct, and I am going to say one."

"Say it."

"I drove the General to Kraków on the nineteenth of July," said Ehlers, "and I came back on the twentieth, and I came back by the way that goes past the Bagatela, and I brought the Mercedes into this yard at four o'clock in the afternoon, and there was a woman of this house coming in through the garden gate from the yard of the building in the next street, with a case in her hand."

The stove ticked. The cook made a noise with the pans.

"I am a driver," he said. "And I have been a driver for the Wehrmacht for four years, and the first thing that a driver of a General learns is the thing that is in the hand of the woman of the General, because the woman of the General is the thing in the house of the General that is not permitted to be a danger to the General, and the car of the General is the place where the driver of the General thinks about the thing." He folded the letter with the greatest care, and put it in the pocket of his tunic, and stood up. "I am not going to say a word to the General, and I am not going to say a word to the SD, and I am not going to say the word to the priest of the district, and I am going to be at the wheel of this car on the first of August, and I have not told the Frau a single thing, because the Frau has never once told me a single thing either, and in this war there is exactly one kind of a friend that a man can have in a German house in Warsaw, and it is the friend who does not ask."

He put his cap on.

"And the one thing that I want," said Ehlers, "is the photograph in the flap of the map case, and it is the last object in the world that I have, and the man who is going to take it away from me is going to have to do it with his hands."

She wrote down the plan that night, and then burned it, and then wrote it out again in her head, in order, the way the men in Kraków had taught her to hold an address.

*The thirty-first, at half past five in the morning: the civilian train. The General will want to put me on it; the paper is already on the hall table; and the thing that I am going to do, at the station, in the dark, with the German military police at the barrier, is the hardest piece of the last month, because it is a lie that I am going to have to tell him with my face and my hands and my voice all at once, and it will be the last lie I ever tell him.*

*Tissier is on the platform. He will watch me decide. Whatever I do, he will see it.*

*On the first, at five o'clock, the city rises. The General will be at the Blue Palace or he will be here. If he is here, I am in the house with him; if he is at the Palace, I am in the house alone with a card in my bag and a folder in a suitcase and a woman with a horse in the yard behind the wall.*

*The folder goes with me; the suitcase is already in the laundry, behind the boiler, under the eleven fur coats which were moved there in the month of May by a woman of fifty with arms like a man's, and which nobody in the world is going to look through in the first week of August, because there is going to be a war in the streets, and a fur coat is not a thing that a soldier takes to a barricade.*

*And there is the amber in the parcel of the Countess, and the parcel is in the lining of the winter coat in the wardrobe of the room of the first floor of the house on the Bagatela, and if everything else goes, the parcel goes with me at the last minute, because it is my mother, and because I have decided that there is going to be exactly one object in the world that comes out of this city in my hand.*

She went to the Visitation on the twenty-ninth of July, at six in the morning, at the hour of the first mass of the priests.

The church was on the Krakowskie Przedmieście at the head of the Old Town, a great baroque barn of a place with a façade of the year 1770 and its treasure in the side chapels, and she went in by the sacristy door at the north, with the black coat and the headscarf of a woman of the Praga market on her head, and the priest was in the sacristy in the green vestments with the vestments put on already and the front of his face washed and shaved in the way of a man who has been up since four.

He was fifty-eight and his name was the name of a village in the province of Kielce, and he had been the channel between the house on the Bagatela and the officers of the district of the rising since the month of the November of the year 1943; and he looked at her for a moment in the light of the one lamp of the sacristy and said, without any greeting at all, in the Polish of a priest of a city parish:

"You are early. Sit down. There are the four minutes before the mass and there is nothing to confess in the four minutes, and no absolution in the four minutes, and I want you to listen."

She sat down on the chair at the wall of the sacristy.

"The first thing is that the hour of the thing is fixed, and it is the first of August at five in the afternoon, and I have it from the men themselves, and the churches of this city have it, and every priest in the deanery knows the hour and every priest in the deanery is hearing it in the confessional from the people of the city, and there is not one of us who is going to be able to say afterwards that he did not know." He put his stole straight, the way a man arranges his work. "The second thing is mine, and it is that the fathers of this house are not going to leave the city. There are eleven of us and us are going to be here, and the church is going to be in the middle of the fighting, because the façade of it is on the Krakowskie Przedmieście and the enemy is going to be three hundred metres to the west of the front of it; and the cellar of this house is full of the people of the parish since the month of the May, and it is going to be fuller, because the superior of the sisters of the Visitation has taken in eleven women of the old families of this city, in the house behind the church, in the last week of the month, and there is a woman of the household of the German general among the eleven who is known to you and who is not going to say my name to any German in the war."

"Jadwiga Ostrowska."

"Jadwiga Ostrowska," said the priest, "who came here in the month of June of the year 1886 for the first time in the arms of a wet nurse, and who has been at the eight o'clock for the forty years of the life of the parish, and who is in the house behind the wall of this church with a bed and a chair and a rosary, and who is sixty-nine years of age, and who asked me in the month of the May for a place, in the way of a woman who is settling an account."

The bell went for the mass.

"The third thing," the priest said, "is the last thing that I am going to say to you in the war, and it is not about the rising and it is not about the paper that you have carried out of the house of the German for the fifteen months of it: it is about the woman."

"Father—"

"Listen. In this house there have been four men and one woman who have known your name in two years, and I am the last of them who is going to be alive in the middle of the month of September, and I know that the way a man of fifty-eight knows when he is going to be in a cellar under the guns of an army of the east. So I am going to give you the last of my time to the part of the matter that the men of the command are not able to give you, and I am going to say it to you the way a priest says it to a woman at the end of a confession, without the absolution, because you have not asked for it and you are not going to ask for it, and because there is not one priest in this city who is able to give absolution to a woman for a thing that this city is never going to be able to know that she did."

He said it, in the four minutes, in the vestry of the Visitation of the Blessed Virgin Mary of the city of Warsaw, on the twenty-ninth of July 1944:

"You have been the paper of the intelligence of this city for the fifteen months of it, and you have been the woman of a German general for the two years of it, and there is not one person in the city of Warsaw who is ever going to be able to put the two of them together in a sentence: not a priest, not a colonel of the cavalry, not a boy of the Praga, and not the man who is in the corridor of the house of the German in the seven seconds of the evening. So I am giving you the instruction that is left, and it is the instruction of a priest of a city parish and not of a chaplain of an army: when it comes to the end, and there is nobody left to carry a paper to and no name to be given, then the matter is not the paper and not the name and not the city. The the matter is the woman. Keep the woman. There is nothing else."

He stood up and took the things of the mass off the table.

"And now go out of the north door and down the passage to the wall and into the street at the corner of the Miodowa," he said, "and do not come to this church again while the city is in the fire, and if I am alive and you are alive at the end of it, then I shall be at the eight o'clock in the second year of the peace, and you will be able to be at the eleventh row, and there will be no need for the two of us to say one word about the war."

She went out through the north door of the church of the Visitation at twenty minutes to seven in the morning of the twenty-ninth of July 1944, in a headscarf, down the passage of the side of the church, and out through a break in the wall into the street of the Miodowa, where the tram of the early hour was standing at the stop with the people of the market on it; and the woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw got on it and went home to the second breakfast of the house in a suit she was going to have to be seen in for the last time; and the priest of the Visitation went out into the church of the Visitation of the Blessed Virgin Mary of the city of Warsaw in the green vestments with the boy and the taper of the six o'clock and did the office of the first mass of the twenty-ninth of July 1944, in the ordinary way of the church of a city in the fifth year of the war.

The church of the Visitation, with the treasure of its side chapels and the fathers of the house and the eleven women of the old families of the city in the cellar behind the wall, was in the fire of the German army on the twenty-ninth of August 1944, at the hour of the middle of the day, and there was not one human being of the eleven fathers of the house who came out of it.

Eleven days, and the last of them began with a train.
