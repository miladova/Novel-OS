# Chapter 11: Eleven Days

The house of the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska was on the Mokotowska, four hundred metres from the house of the Colonel, and it had two floors of a building of 1898, and a courtyard with a well in it, and a drawing room that had been stripped in 1939 of everything but a piano, a portrait of a man with a moustache, and a set of chairs that had been in the family since the eighteen-fifties and had the last century in the wood.

On the Thursday, Marion Herschlag went to it at five in the afternoon in the coat of a Frenchwoman of the German administration, and the Countess received her in the drawing room in a grey dress and a brooch, and they sat facing each other across a small table, and for the first eleven minutes they spoke about the wildflowers of the Midi, and the Countess's maid, who was fifty-five and deaf, brought tea in a glass, and there was a plate with three small biscuits on it that had been made, Marion understood at the first bite, out of German flour with the stamp of the district on the bag, which meant that the Countess had a source, which in Warsaw in 1943 was the same thing as having a password.

Then the Countess put down her glass and said, in French, in the flat accent of a woman who had been taught by a governess from Neuchâtel in 1887:

"Now, my dear. Who are you?"

"Marion Herschlag."

"The woman who is in the house of the Oberst von Reuth."

"Yes."

"And in the house of the Oberst von Reuth, there is a Frenchwoman, aged thirty-one, from Strasbourg; she has been with him since last autumn; she goes to the German store on the Nowy Świat; she takes tea with the wives at the Blue Palace; and in the four years of the German occupation of this city nobody has ever come to me and said, 'Jadwiga, what do you know about the Oberst's Frenchwoman?'" The Countess looked at her with a face like a page of an old book. "That is not an accident, my child. That is a piece of architecture. There are five hundred women in this city who know the German administration's private life, and there is not one of them who would not have sold you for a kilo of lard three weeks ago; and the reason they have not is that you have never once, in seven weeks, been seen to be *anybody*; and I want you to understand that this is the most extraordinary thing I have ever seen a young woman do, and I have been watching the Polish women of this city survive the Russians, the Germans and each other since 1915."

"Madame."

"Do not interrupt me." The Countess poured more tea. She did it in the way of a woman whose hands had been pouring tea for sixty years. "Now. What are we to do with you?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"I mean that you were born in this city and that I knew your mother, and that on the second of December 1939 the blackshirts came to your building and burnt the papers in the courtyard, and I know about your father, because everyone in this city of the old world knows about everybody who is left, and I know that the Stankiewicz register has been closed since 1940, and that there is a caretaker's certificate in the housing office on the Krasińskiego with a professor's name on it, and that in the winter the certificate expires." She drank her tea. "And I know that there is a woman of twenty-eight with one short leg who works in the records office of the tram company, and that she buys an orange a day, which is impossible, which means she has been lying to her canteen for two years for the sake of an old man; and all of this I know because this is what we do now: we know everything about each other and nothing at all, because knowledge is the only currency in the world, and the Germans take everything else."

Marion Herschlag sat in the chair that had been in the family of Ostrowski since 1851 and looked at a woman of sixty-eight who had lost a son at the Bzura and a husband in the first week of the war; and she made, in four seconds, the longest calculation of her life.

"Madame," she said, "I cannot tell you."

"I have not asked you to."

"You have asked me who I am."

"And you have told me," said the Countess. "The one thing I wanted to know was whether you would tell me a lie, and you have not; and that is all I need to know, because the rest is your business and God's, and I am not in the business of either." She set the glass down. "Now, listen to me, because we are going to spend the rest of the war being extremely careful with one another, and there are four things I am going to give you, and you are not to thank me for any of them.

"The first is that in this house you are the widow of a Frenchman named Herschlag, and a distant connection of my late husband's family, and you are able to come here, because it is understood among the German ladies of this district that I am a harmless old woman with a piano who has a French niece; and I have been establishing that for six weeks, since the day I saw you in the Krasińskiego, in the way one establishes such things, which is by mentioning it, at parties, to the wife of the supply officer, twice, in passing, in the same sentence as a complaint about the price of butter.

"The second is that the woman who cooks for me was the cook at your mother's house in 1934, and she is deaf, and she has been in the back of my kitchen for three days with her hands shaking, and she will not look at you, and you will not go into the kitchen.

"The third is that if you are ever in a room with a German officer and you are asked, by that officer, whether you know the Ostrowski family, you will say: *no, but I have heard of them; the Countess has a bad heart and no sons*. That is the sentence. In that order. Because it is true, and because nothing a German officer hears is ever wasted, and because a woman who tells a small true thing instead of a large false thing is a woman who is believed.

"And the fourth is that on the days when they are taking people out of the buildings in the Krasińskiego and the Długa and the Miodowa, you will not be able to get to your father from the south, and you must not try, because on those days the north of the city is a trap with the door shut; and on those days Zofia knows what to do, and Zofia has been doing it since 1939, and I have paid for the little wood for the stove in the flat since February."

She stopped. The room was very quiet. In the courtyard the well chain knocked against the stone, in the wind, three times.

"Why?" said Krystyna.

"Because your mother," said the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska, "in the winter of 1935, took my son's side in a quarrel about a marriage, in a drawing room on the Nowy Świat, in front of eleven people, at the moment when nobody else in Warsaw would; and it cost her a friendship and a great deal of money and she never once mentioned it, and I have been waiting for eight years to pay it."

---

The Colonel put the captain in the house.

That was how it was arranged, and it was arranged the way everything in the district was arranged, by the requirements of a man who had been given too much work and had discovered that the only way to survive it was to bring the work into the house: from the second week of July, Hauptmann Brandt came to the house on the Mokotowska on Sundays and Thursdays with a leather case and stayed from eight in the evening until eleven, at the dining-room table, with the Colonel, going through the week's paper; and Anna Zaleska was told to leave out bread and cold meat and a bottle of the Hungarian wine at ten o'clock, and the door of the dining room stayed open, and Marion Herschlag, in the salon across the hall, read a book.

She was in the house. He was in the house. That was it, and it lasted eleven days.

She had prepared herself for the first Sunday in the manner of a woman preparing for an operation: with a rehearsal of every possible sentence and a plan for the worst case. The worst case was that he came into the salon; and he did, at twenty past nine on the first Sunday, to find the newspaper; and he stood in the doorway of the salon — a captain of the Abwehr in the house of his colonel on a summer evening with the windows open, a newspaper in his hand, a woman on a sofa with a book — and he said:

"Madame."

"Hauptmann."

"I am looking for the *Völkischer Beobachter*."

"It is on the second shelf. I have been using it to hold a vase."

He found it. He went out. That was Sunday.

Thursday, he was in the garden, looking at the chestnut with the fungus, because the Colonel had sent him out with a torch to look at the chestnut, and Marion was at the window of the salon with the lamp on behind her; and they did not speak at all, and she stood at the window for four minutes and watched his back in the dark, and the way he stood with the torch in his hand: not looking at a tree at all, but standing with his shoulders, in the dark of a Polish garden, in an attitude that she had last seen on a boy of nineteen in a Botanic Garden in June, which was the attitude of a man with a book under his arm who does not want to go home yet.

Sunday, the third time: nothing. He did not come into the salon. He came at eight, and went at a quarter past eleven, and the house was aware of him, and the house pretended that the house contained one woman and two officers, and Anna Zaleska cooked the cold meat and said nothing, and Piotr Zaleski, in the yard, sharpened an axe on a stone for one hour and did it on a Sunday, which was a thing that had never once happened in the three years of the German occupation of that house.

Thursday, the fourth time, she found the book.

It was on the piano. The upright German piano of 1898, in the corner of the salon, with the good action and the bad bass: a book lying on the closed lid, and the book was a Polish book, a thin octavo with a brown spine. She picked it up and opened it, and it was what it was, and it took her four seconds to be entirely certain: it was *Podstawy mechaniki*, the third edition, a textbook, and it was not the book that she had found in a crate in the rue de la Faisanderie — but inside the front cover, in the dark place where a library stamp goes, there was a bookseller's label.

*Księgarnia J. Barczyk, Nowy Świat 41, Warszawa.*

Lis's shop.

She stood in a German salon in an occupied city with a Polish textbook of mathematics in her hands, and the book was open at a page where somebody had, at some time, in a house in Warsaw before the war, written in the margin in pencil a small, impatient, one-line correction.

She read the correction. It was in her father's hand.

She stood there for four minutes. She did not sit down; she did not cry; and when she had put the book back on the lid of the piano, in the same position, with the corner facing the same way, she went down the corridor to the small room next to the Colonel's and shut the door and stood with her back against it and her fists in the sleeves of her cardigan, and did the only thing she could do, which was the arithmetic of a woman who has just been handed a live grenade by a boy in a thin shirt: *the book could not have come from the Colonel, because the Colonel does not read Polish and does not read mathematics. The book came from the flat of a Polish lodger, or from a box of paper the district has taken from a Polish house, which means it could be a piece of loot, which means it means nothing.*

*It means nothing.*

She said that to herself four times, and by the fourth time she had understood that the arithmetic was not the point; the point was that the book was on the piano, in the house, at the hour of a Thursday evening, in the exact place where a woman read a book; and that if a man of twenty-eight had chosen a Polish mathematics textbook, third edition, with a bookseller's label of the Nowy Świat on the cover, and had put it there, on the lid of a piano, in the house of a colonel of the Abwehr, in the middle of a war, in Warsaw, then he had chosen it in full possession of his intelligence, and had done it for one woman in Europe, and it was the single most dangerous thing that had happened to her since the milliner said the word *aunt* on a telephone in Paris.

Eleven days. It took eleven days.

She counted them afterwards, the way you count the length of an illness: eleven days from the Thursday in July when the captain stood in the doorway of a salon with a newspaper in his hand, to the Sunday evening in July when the house went out — the Colonel to the Blue Palace for a dinner, Anna and Piotr to the evening service at the church of the Visitation, at the other end of the street, because Anna Zaleska had gone to the evening service at the church of the Visitation every Sunday for three years, and had never once asked anybody for anything in that time, and had asked, that day, for leave to go a quarter of an hour early.

The garden was two chestnuts and a walnut and thirty metres of grass, with the side gate in the wall, and it came to be full of a Warsaw evening in July: the sky at nine o'clock the colour of a coal fire, the smell of the river three kilometres away, and, from the other side of the wall, in the next street, the sound of a wireless set playing a mazurka very quietly, because the Polish national wireless had been silent since 1939 and the only music in the city came out of the German stations and the gramophones of the Polish houses.

She was standing at the end of the garden by the walnut tree when he came out, because she had known, in the way she had known things since the age of eleven, that he was going to come out.

He walked the whole length of the garden, and stopped three metres from her, and did the thing that took her training and broke it: he did not speak. He stood there in the dark with his hands at his sides and looked at the walnut tree, for two minutes, with the sky going down behind the wall; and in those two minutes, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, who had spent three years learning every variation of silence in the world, understood that this was not a man being careful. It was a man deciding whether to say something that could not be taken back.

Then he said: "Eleven days."

"I know."

"I have had eleven days to work out how to say this, and I have not worked it out, so I am going to say the first true sentence and then you may do whatever you like with me." He turned his head and looked at her, and in the dark he was a stranger, a captain, a man with the scar at the corner of his mouth. "The first true sentence is this. On the eighth of April 1943, at eleven o'clock in the morning, on the platform at the Gare de l'Est, I watched you get into a railway carriage, and I stood in the corridor of that carriage for thirty-one hours, and I made a decision, and the decision was that if you spoke to me once — once, in that journey, in a year — I would get off the train at Breslau and disappear, and you would never see me again, and nobody in the world would be able to find out what I had known."

"What did you know?"

"Everything," said Phillip Brandt. "I have known everything since eleven minutes past four in the afternoon on the thirtieth of January 1943, when I saw the woman who was on the arm of my colonel at a dinner in the Hôtel Majestic turn her head at the sound of the word *Polish* in the mouth of a Luftwaffe officer, and I understood, in that second, three things: what she was, what she had been, and what she was doing with him. And then I went home and sat on the end of a bed with my boots off and did the arithmetic for four hours, and I decided to say nothing, and I have said nothing for six months, and I am thirty-two days from being posted to the eastern front, and there is not one person in Europe to whom I can say any of this." He stopped. "There. Now you may do whatever you like."

The wireless in the next street changed its tune, and the garden was full of a Portuguese fado that a German station in Warsaw was playing out of a Polish window into a Polish yard, because in the sixth year of the Reich the man who chose the programmes for the eastern stations had a sense of humour that nobody had ever managed to describe.

She stood three metres from him in the dark and did the arithmetic, and it took her nine seconds, and in the nine seconds she did the following: she considered killing him, which was not possible, and reported him to the Colonel, which was the end of both of them, and lying to him with the great beautiful efficient lie she had spent three years polishing, which would work, for months, for years, for as long as the war lasted, and which would be the single greatest labour of her life.

And then she did the thing she had been taught never to do in a room in Kraków in 1940, by a small man with a bad heart who had once sold books on the Nowy Świat.

She told him the truth, in the smallest possible quantity, in the exact place where the truth was most useful, and she did it in her own language, in her own real voice, which she had not used in the presence of another human being in the war.

"Phillip," she said, in Polish. "You have to understand something. I am not going to be able to tell you anything, ever, and you are not going to be able to ask me, and if you ever do ask me, in the house, on a stair, in the garden, in the street, I am going to lie to you in the way I would lie to a policeman, because that is what I am for; and if you take that personally, then you will kill me with your feelings, and there will never be one single moment in this war in which you will have done anything that a man cannot forgive in the last hour of his life."

"So that is a no."

"That is a yes with the war in it." She turned to him; and then, because the front door was open, because the servants were at the church, because there was a wireless set playing a fado in a Polish yard and the city outside the wall was full of people being shot for the exact thing she was doing, she crossed three metres of grass in a garden in Warsaw, in July 1943, and she reached up and put her hand flat against his face, once, for two seconds, the way she had touched her father's skull; and then she took it away and went into the house.

He said one word, behind her, in the dark; and it was not a word she had heard in three years; and it was the only thing anybody in the world had called her since 1940 that was true.

"Krystyna."

She went into the house. She went up the stairs, past the room of a doctor's daughter, past the door of the big bedroom where a general's blanket lay folded, into the small room next to the Colonel's, and shut the door and stood in the dark with her forehead against the door and her eyes closed; and heard the side gate shut, and the garden go quiet, and the wireless in the next street end its fado and go over to the news in German.

Eleven days.

At midnight the Colonel came home, and found her reading in the salon, and stood in the door with his cap still in his hand.

"You are up."

"It is too hot to sleep."

"It is." He came in and sat down heavily in the chair opposite her; and he had drunk; and he was, in the way of a man after a dinner at the Blue Palace, all the way through in one piece and about to tell her something. "Brandt was at table tonight. I have put him on the eastern list. They want him in Riga in September."

She kept her eyes on the page of the book.

"He will be sorry to go," she said.

"Yes." The Colonel looked at the window. "He said something to me tonight, at the door, when he was putting his cap on. He said, *Herr Oberst, the Poles in this district have a saying: the man who carries a lamp in the dark is the man who is shot at first.* And I said, *and what is the saying for the man who carries the lamp for somebody else?* and he said, *there is no saying for him, sir; he is the lamp.*"

He laughed, that short bark, the door closing.

"I like him," said the Colonel of the intelligence section of the Warsaw district. "I have never once known what he thinks, and I have never once had a reason to distrust him, and I shall put him on the Riga train in September with a letter in his file that will make him a major in a year, because that, my dear, is the whole art of a colonel: you take a young man who is nothing, you give him a lamp to carry, and you watch which way he walks with it."

He got up, and went to bed; and in the small room next to the big bedroom, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna lay in the dark, listening to the city, and did the last of the arithmetic she ever did with a clear head about the man on the other side of the wall.

*Ninety days have become fifty, and the fifty have become eleven, and the eleven have become a garden, and now there is a train to Riga in September, and there is a man who knows everything and has said nothing for six months, and there is a paper in a safe in the study with a serial number on it that I can read off a brass plate with a duster in my hand.*

*And there is a boy in a thin shirt in front of a glasshouse, in 1938, holding a closed book, in a photograph clipped into a German file.*

Anna Zaleska asked her on the eleventh of the month, in the kitchen, at the sink, with a bowl in her hands.

She did it in the way of a Polish woman of fifty who has been in the service of German houses for four years: standing at the sink with her back to the room, and the water running, and no introduction, no preamble, and no name.

"The Pan has a cousin in the Gęsiówka."

"I do not know the word."

"The Pan knows the word." The water stopped. Anna Zaleska put the bowl upside down on the board and took up the cloth and began to dry it, and she did not turn round. "The camp on the Dzielna. The one that is in the old prison building, where the Jews of the small towns were kept in the year 1941 and where the men of this city have been kept since the month of the November. My Piotr has a cousin there, and the cousin has been in it since the twenty-ninth of November, and the reason he is in it is a blue policeman on the Chłodna who wanted the room, and the cousin has a wife and two children in the Praga and the wife is my own blood, and the transports have begun."

Marion Herschlag stood at the table of the kitchen of the house on the Mokotowska with the plate of the evening in her hands.

"Say it."

"The transports have begun," said Anna Zaleska, "and the first of them went in the month of the February, and there is a paper of the administration of the district that puts the men of that camp into the transports of the labour administration of the Reich, and the wife of the cousin has been at the window of the house of the administration of the district on the avenue of the Ujazdowskie for three days, and there is nobody in the German administration of this city who is going to speak to a woman of the Praga in a doorway, and the family has understood that, and the family has decided that the person who is going to speak is the woman of the household of the Colonel of the district of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw."

The kitchen was quiet, except for the clock of the corridor.

"And what is the thing that the family has decided that the woman of the household of the Colonel of the district of the intelligence service is going to do?" said Marion Herschlag, in Polish, with her face composed, in the tone of a woman of a house asking about the price of the meat, because the door of the kitchen was open and the corridor of the German house on the Mokotowska had no door at the end of it.

"Take a paper to the office of the labour administration," said Anna Zaleska, "with the name of the cousin on it, and the signature of the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw under the name, and the word *unentbehrlich*, which is the word that the administration of the Reich uses for a man who is not able to be taken, and it is one line in one paper in one office, and the cousin comes out of the Gęsiówka on the cart of the labour administration of the district and goes to his wife and his two children in the Praga, and there is nothing in it that costs anybody anything except the ink."

"That is it?"

"That is it."

"Then let me tell you the truth about your line of one paper and one office, in this kitchen, in the German house, with the door open," said Marion Herschlag, very quietly, in Polish. "There is no line. There is a signature, and the signature is a general of the intelligence service of the German district of Warsaw; and a general of the intelligence service of a German district who signs a paper about a man of the Gęsiówka in the year 1943 signs a piece of paper that goes into a file, and the file is in the office of the administration, and the office of the administration of this district is at this hour in the hands of men who keep a card for every piece of paper in this city, and the card of that paper would say: a man of the Gęsiówka was taken out of the transport by an order with the signature of a general of the intelligence service of the district, and against it there would be a second card, and the second card would have a single question on it, and the question is my life. That is what the line of one paper would cost, and it is not the ink. It is the arrangement: the house, the woman of the household, the table, the bed of the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw, and every hour of the day of a woman of Alsace in the fifth year of the war."

Anna Zaleska put the cloth down.

"I know what it costs," she said, in her own language, to the wall above the sink. "I have known it since the month of the February, and I have sat with it since the month of the February, and I have not said it to you because you have not been a person to me, and this house knows what you are, and I have kept the soup and the linen and the floor, and I have not looked at your face for a year. But the transports have begun." She turned round for the first time and looked at her with her face in the light of the kitchen of the German administration of the city of Warsaw: the face of a woman of fifty from the Praga with the widow's cross of the year 1939 and the patience of a population. "So I am asking the German's woman the question, once, in the kitchen, and I know that she is going to say no, and I am asking it because one of us has to say it out loud in this house."

And Marion Herschlag, of the household of the General of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw, stood in the kitchen with the plate in her hands and did the arithmetic of the second and looked at it, and the arithmetic had one answer and it was the answer that she had known before the question was put to her, and the exact character of the thing she was doing was simple: she was going to say no to the wife of the cousin and to the cousin and to the two children of the cousin in the Praga, and she was going to say it in the kitchen of the German house, in Polish, to a woman of the same city as herself, and both of them were going to understand the reason and neither of them was ever going to say it.

"No," she said.

"I know."

"Anna."

"Take the plate to the dining room," said Anna Zaleska, "and do not say the name of the cousin in the dining room, and the rest of it is between the two of us, and there is nothing in the world that is able to be done about it, and that is the reason that this war is the war of the German administration of the city of Warsaw."

She took up the bowls and put them in the sink, and Marion Herschlag carried the plate into the dining room of a doctor's house and put it down in front of the Ordonnanzoffizier of the district of Warsaw, and set her face the way a woman of the profession sets a face, in eleven seconds, in a doorway; and in the five months of the war, in the city of Warsaw, in the house of a German general, the woman of that house never afterwards had a single conversation with the woman of the kitchen that lasted longer than the arranging of the soup.

*The cousin of Piotr Zaleski went into the transport of the labour administration of the district of Warsaw in the month of the March of 1943, and there is not one paper in the world about the rest of the life of the man, and the wife and the two children of the cousin of Piotr Zaleski went to the Praga and then to the province, and there is not one thing about it in this book.*

*And if I am going to use him, I have to decide, tonight, which of the two of us is going to be the lamp and which of us is going to be the thing that is shot at.*
