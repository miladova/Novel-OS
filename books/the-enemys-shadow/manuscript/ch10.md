# Chapter 10: The Streets Remember

Żoliborz in 1943 was a district of five-storey houses and small gardens on the north side of the city, built in the twenties for civil servants and schoolteachers and doctors, and it had been fought over in September 1939, house by house, by the Polish army of the Warsaw garrison; and it was, in the autumn of that year, the first place in Warsaw where an entire street had been emptied, in the middle of the night, by the SS, with lorries, and where the neighbours had stood in their nightclothes at the windows and watched, because that was what the first time was like: the whole street stood at the windows and watched and did not know what it was watching, and after that everyone in the city knew what it was, and the next time the whole street sat in the dark in the middle of the rooms with their hands over their children's mouths.

The Stankiewicz family had lived on the Mokotowska, in the centre, for Krystyna's girlhood. But her mother's sister had a house in Żoliborz, on a street called the Krasińskiego, with a garden and a pear tree; and there had been, in Krystyna's memory, one summer of 1931 in that house with the pear tree, when she was fourteen, in which she had been given a room of her own with a window that looked into the branches, and had learned a sonata in that room, and had fallen in love, in the way of a girl of fourteen, with a cousin of her aunt's husband's who had not known she existed.

She walked to Żoliborz on the ninth of June, on a Thursday, in the coat that was made of a Polish factory's wool and had been the coat of a maid, with a shopping bag for cabbage and a paper *Kennkarte* in her pocket.

She had spent three weeks working out how to do it, and it was not, in the end, clever at all; the cleverness came later, when she had to do it a second time.

The method was Anna Zaleska.

Anna went to the market on Tuesdays and Fridays and to see her sister in Praga on Thursdays, and once a month she went, in accordance with the German ration system, to register at a district office in the northern part of the town for the special provision of the Polish domestic staff of officers' households, which was a system the Germans had invented to demonstrate that the Poles who served them were better off than the Poles who did not, and which was, in the fourth year of the occupation, the only reason that two hundred thousand Poles were still alive in the city; and the office was at the corner of a street in Żoliborz, and it took an hour and a quarter to walk there and back, and it was possible, in that hour and a quarter, for a Frenchwoman of the house to *accompany the cook on an errand*, because a Frenchwoman in a Polish house, in the German administration of Warsaw, was a thing that nobody ever looked at twice: she was not a German and she was not a Pole; she was, in that city, in that year, the only kind of person who could walk in the street without being either watched or shot.

She invented the arrangement in the kitchen on the last Sunday of May, over the coffee, in Polish, in three sentences, and Anna Zaleska looked at her for a long moment with a face like a good loaf of bread and then said, in German, carefully, as a servant says a thing that may be repeated: "The lady should not walk so far. The lady should take the tram like other ladies."

And then, in Polish, quietly, and without any change in her face at all: "The day after tomorrow, at nine. Bring the bag. And do not talk to me in the street, because I have a sister in Praga and a cousin who is a policeman, and if the German police take my Piotr to the Szucha I will not have anybody to bring me the little wood for the stove."

---

The office was a ruin of a school that had been made into an office, and there were forty Polish women in the queue at nine in the morning, and Marion Herschlag stood in it with her bag like a woman who had been sent by a house; and no one spoke to her, and no one looked at her; and after eleven minutes a woman of thirty at the front of the queue dropped her registration book, and it slid a metre and a half along the pavement, and Marion picked it up and handed it back, and the woman said, "Thank you, madam," very clearly, and that was the transaction, and it took four seconds, and it was followed by thirty seconds in which the woman who had dropped the book went into the office and Marion Herschlag turned and walked away down the street of Żoliborz with a small piece of paper in the palm of her hand, folded four times, the size of a stamp.

She read it on the corner, in the shelter of a doorway, with her bag on the ground between her feet and her back to the wall.

*The house in the Krasińskiego was shelled in 1939 and the neighbours took the roof. The family went to the Praga side in 1942 and the daughter to the convent, and the aunt died in the summer. The professor is alive. He is in the old apartment, on the third floor, in the second room, with the woman Pelagia, who has the eyes of a blind woman; and the neighbours have kept him on the register of the house as a caretaker, because a caretaker is issued with a certificate; and the certificate is the only paper in the world that keeps him alive, and it expires in the winter.*

*There is no nurse. There is no medicine. He is fed by the neighbours.*

*You must not go.*

She stood in the doorway in the Krasińskiego with the piece of paper between her thumb and forefinger, in the middle of a street where she had spent the summer of her fourteenth year, with the pear tree that had died in the shelling standing black over a garden wall thirty metres away; and she read the last line four times.

*You must not go.*

She looked at her hands. She had, in the three years, never once been given an order that she could not obey; and she had been given orders by men with bad hearts and by men with no hearts, in rooms in Warsaw and Kraków and a cinema in Paris; and the discipline of it was that the order and the woman were two different things, and that the woman was nailed to a board somewhere a long way behind the order, and did what she was told.

She went.

---

The house was on the street where the family had lived for twenty-one years: a big grey building of 1910 with a courtyard and a gate that had a porter in it, and now had a notice in German and a Polish caretaker's wife who sat on a kitchen chair in the doorway of the gatehouse in the middle of the day because there was nothing else to do.

Marion Herschlag went in through the courtyard at eleven in the morning, with her cabbage bag, and up the back stairs, which were the stairs that the servants used and the stairs that the neighbours used, because the front stair was the one on which the German administration had posted its notices and its placard of the house's register and its list of the flats; and the third floor, and the second door on the landing, which had a brass plate on it with her father's initials and a card with the hour of the lessons he gave, and which now had nothing on it at all, only the marks where two things had been.

She did not knock for eleven seconds. She had been trained not to knock on doors that were not hers; she had been trained to knock on the door of a flat, in a house, only the way a *Polish servant girl with a cabbage bag* would knock, which is by opening it.

She opened it.

The flat, on the first inspection, was empty.

Two of the rooms had been taken by the German administration's housing office in 1940 for the family of a Polish railway official, who paid the rent to the house and not to the Stankiewicz; and the glass in the corridor had been broken in 1939 in the shelling of September and repaired in 1940 with a piece of plywood, and the plywood had been painted by somebody with a last of the white paint, and the paint was still there, and the corridor smelled of boiled cabbage and, under the cabbage, of the pipes and the dust and the particular smell of a Warsaw building that Krystyna Stankiewiczówna had smelled every day for the first twenty-two years of her life, and the smell was the thing that nearly killed her, in the four years, because it was not a smell, it was a room, and in that room there was a girl of ten coming in out of the snow with her hands burning, and a girl of nineteen at a window with a book, and a mother at the end of the corridor in a green velvet dressing gown saying *Krystyna, the piano*.

The third door was her father's.

He was in the doorway of it, in a coat, in the dark, with a book in his hand, and he was so thin that the coat hung off him like a coat on a nail.

He looked at her for four seconds.

"Woman," he said, politely, in Polish, "you have the wrong flat; the woman with the cabbage is downstairs."

And in the four seconds, and in the way he stood in the doorway of a room holding a book with his finger in it, she saw that he did not know her.

She stood in the corridor of her own house with a cabbage bag and looked at her father, and did the arithmetic the way she had done it in the safe in Paris, which was to say: *old man, sixty-eight, in a coat in June, in a room with no fire, with a book, and the German administration of the district of Warsaw between me and the woman who is going to cry*; and she said, in Polish, in the voice of a stranger:

"I am the woman who has come about the room. The caretaker's wife sent me. I was told the professor receives."

"There is no room."

"There is the room at the end of the corridor. I saw it from the courtyard; the window has a curtain. I was told that the professor has lodgers."

"Who told you?"

"An old woman at the gate."

Her father came two steps down the corridor and stood in the light from the window of the landing, and she saw his face, which was the face of the photograph on the mantelpiece of a flat in the Mokotowska: a wide, faintly Mongolian face with the intelligence of a mathematician in the forehead, and the lids of the eyes lower than they had been, and a certain grey slackness at the left side of the mouth.

He looked at her for a long moment, and the long moment went on being four seconds and then six and then eight, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the corridor of her father's flat and gave everything she had spent three years learning to a man with a book in his hand, which was: the droop of the shoulders of a woman who has been carrying a bag, and a French way of holding her chin, and the eyes going down at the last moment, and the face of a housekeeper.

"There is no room," said her father. "There is a woman with no eyes and a man who used to be a professor of mechanics and no longer writes; and the room at the end of the corridor has two hundred books in it that nobody wants. And if you have a cabbage in that bag, then take it downstairs, because the woman downstairs is the only one in the building who still cooks."

He went back into his room and shut the door.

She went down the back stairs and stood in the courtyard for a moment in the sunlight of June, with the cabbages, and she did not cry then and did not cry later, and what she did instead was to stand there with her back to the wall of the gatehouse and count the windows of the third floor on the courtyard, one at a time, four windows, and fix the position of every one of them in her memory, and to make, in the place in her head where the arithmetic happened, the biggest entry of her life.

Then she went home, and gave the cabbages to Anna in the kitchen, and said that she had been to the Krasińskiego and that the garden there had been stripped and that she had not spoken to anybody; and Anna Zaleska looked at her with a face like a good loaf of bread, and said nothing at all, and went on peeling, because in Warsaw in 1943 that was what one did with a thing which one could not ask about.

---

The second visit was on the twenty-fourth of June, at seven in the evening, in the golden hour of a Warsaw June, and it was possible because on the twenty-fourth the German administration of the city was at a reception at the Blue Palace in honour of the birthday of a General, and because her father's flat had a lodger.

The lodger was a concierge's daughter of thirty, named Zofia, who had one leg shorter than the other from a childhood illness and who worked in the records office of the tram company and who had come to the flat in October 1939 with a bag and had not left, and who was, in the fourth year of the occupation, the reason that Professor Stankiewicz was still alive, because she brought home, every day, from the tram company's canteen, an orange, and gave him half.

Marion Herschlag went up the back stairs at a quarter past seven with a bag that had two hundred grammes of German butter and a tin of German sardines in it and a paper of German medicine from the district pharmacy that the household of an Oberst was entitled to, and she knocked the way a woman with a bag knocks on the door of a flat where a woman has a parcel to deliver, and Zofia opened it, and looked at her.

"Zosia," said Marion Herschlag, in Polish, in the voice of a woman of the district, which is a voice with a particular flat clarity in it, "there is a parcel for the professor."

Zofia looked at the bag.

"From whom?"

"From the woman who came about the room."

And in that moment she saw, in the eyes of a woman with one leg shorter than the other who worked in the records office of the tram company and had spent three years and eight months keeping an old man alive on the strength of half of a canteen orange per day, the entire arithmetic of the city of Warsaw: the knowledge that this woman in the doorway with the German butter was a thing that would either kill them both or keep them alive, and the fact that Zofia had no way of knowing which, and the fact that in Warsaw in 1943 the only thing a person could do with a stranger at a door was to open it and see.

She opened the door.

Her father was at the table by the window with his books around him, and there was an oil lamp on the table, and a glass with a spoon in it, and the glass had medicine in it, and on the wall behind him, in the place where her mother's portrait had hung for twenty-one years, there was nothing at all, only the pale rectangle.

He looked up.

This time she did not stay more than four seconds. She crossed the room, and put the bag on the table, and put her hand on the back of her father's head, on his skull through the thin hair, the way she had done her life, since she was four years old, since the flat in the Mokotowska, in the garden, in the corridor, at the station, on the day in 1937 when she had left for a school in a town in the south; and she said, in Polish, in her own voice:

"It is me."

And her father put both his hands on her hand, one over the other, and held it on the top of his own head; and his shoulders began to shake, and he did not make any sound, and he did not turn around; and it lasted eleven seconds and then he took his hands away, and said, in a voice that had been scrubbed clean of everything, in the flat voice of a professor of mechanics:

"Zosia. Shut the door."

He did it right. That was the thing she would remember: her father, sixty-eight years old, with a coat and a book, took hold of his own grief with the flat of both hands and put it down, and turned around in his chair, and looked at his daughter in a maid's coat in the middle of his wrecked drawing room in the fourth year of the occupation, and said to her, in the way he had said things to her at the piano when she was nine:

"Sit down. Tell me what you are permitted to tell me, and nothing else, and no names. Then take the butter out of the bag in front of the woman, so that between us there is nothing hidden, because a thing hidden between three people in Warsaw is a thing that hangs them on the same morning."

She sat down. She told him nothing. She told him that she was alive, that she was not married, that she worked, that she had been in France, that she could not come often, that there was a woman in a coat from Alsace who had to be kept alive in her body, and that she would never tell him where she lived or with whom, and that the day would come, before the end of the war, when he would hear the truth of it in the street from a man he did not know; and her father listened to it with his hands folded in front of his mouth, the way he had listened to a hundred students.

Then he said: "Your mother's amber is not in the house. It went with the woman downstairs in December 1939, because the blackshirts came to the building on the second of December and took the man in the flat below and burnt the papers in the courtyard; and Pelagia took the amber out of the flat that night, and it is not in this building; and if you are ever in a position to ask a half-blind woman for a thing, then it will be the only object of your mother's that survives the war."

"And Bolek?"

Her father's face did not change at all.

"Your brother," he said, "works in a chemist's in the Praga district and is a fool and is not to be looked for by anybody, and I have not seen him for eleven months and do not intend to, and if you go to the Praga district to look for him you will kill him."

The lamp made a small noise. Zofia was at the other end of the room, doing something with the family's linen, which had been her mother's linen, so that the room had about it the smell of 1934.

"I am going to say one thing to you," said her father, "because I am an old man and I have been dying slowly for two years and I know how it is done; and after this I will not say it again, and you will not come more than once a month, or if you must come, you will do it exactly as you did it today, and Zosia will hang a white cloth on the line in the courtyard on the days when the house is watched. You are a woman in the house of the enemy; I do not know what you are and I am not going to ask, because a man of sixty-eight who has been asking questions in this city for four years knows that the only answer he will ever get is your name — which is Stankiewiczówna, which is the name of my father, and my grandfather, and a house in the Mokotowska that no longer has a lamp in the hall." He took his hands down and put them on the table. "I want you to understand that I am not proud of you, and that I am not ashamed of you, because I do not know which of the two things you are, and I shall never know, and a father is not required to know, he is required to *stand*: and the thing you must know is that your mother died in the first autumn of the war in a house in the country with Pelagia holding her hand, and her last words were the ones she said every day of her life, which were: *Krystyna, you are not playing the second movement slowly enough.*"

He stopped. In the corridor, from the flat of the family of the railway official, came the sound of a wireless set with a German voice in it. Zofia was folding linen.

"Go now," said her father. "And if you are ever asked, in a room, whether you have a family in Warsaw: you do not. There is no Stankiewicz in the register, there is a caretaker on the register, and that is the arrangement, and it is the best piece of work I have done since I stopped doing physics."

She stood up. She went to the table, and took the butter and the sardines and the medicine out of the bag, and put them down in a row in the lamplight, so that Zofia would see it and there would be nothing hidden between three people.

And then she did the thing she had come for, which she had been going to do since the ninth of June: she bent down and kissed her father on the top of the head, in the way of a daughter, and stood up, and went out of the room, and down the back stairs, and out through the courtyard, past the gatehouse, into the Krasińskiego.

It was a quarter to nine. The sky over the north of the city was the colour of a peach at the bottom and, at the top, the dark blue of June; and somewhere over the Vistula, at the same hour, at the Blue Palace, a General was raising a glass to the Führer with two hundred officers standing; and on the wall of a building in the Śródmieście there was a placard four days old, with eleven Polish names on it, one of them a woman's; and in a flat in the Praga district, if her father was to be believed and he was to be believed, her brother of seventeen was pretending to be a fool behind a chemist's counter; and in the flat behind her, an old man in a coat was sitting at a table with a tin of sardines in front of him, in a room where a piano had been, waiting for a woman with one short leg to bring him half an orange and the news of the world.

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna walked out of the Krasińskiego with her empty bag and her hat on and her gloves on, because a woman with an empty bag and her gloves on is a woman going home from shopping; and at the corner of the next street, forty metres away, standing at a tram stop with her hands folded in front of her and her face turned to the road with the patience of a Warsaw afternoon, was a woman in a grey coat whom she had known since the winter of 1937, who was the aunt of a girl she had been at school with, and who was the last person in Warsaw — the *only* person in Warsaw, according to three years of briefing, and eleven files, and a card index kept by a Hauptsturmführer with spectacles — who would look at the face of Marion Herschlag and see, without a doubt, without any doubt at all, the daughter of the Stankiewiczes of the Mokotowska, in the face of a woman with a cabbage bag.

They closed the street at four o'clock on the fourteenth of May.

It was a Friday, and the hour of the end of the afternoon of the working week, which was the hour that the administration of the district of Warsaw had chosen for the year; and it was done in the pattern that the population of the city knew the way a farmer knows the shape of a hailstorm: two lorries of the police arriving at the two ends of a street of shops at the same moment, the cordon of the blue police and the men of the formation of the east walking the pavements with the rifles at the low port, and the tram stopped in the middle of the block with its doors shut and the people of the tram in it, looking out of the windows the way the people of a burning house look at the yard.

She was in the German car in the queue of the traffic at the corner of the Krucza when the cordon went down.

There were four cars and a tram in front of the Mercedes at the moment the whistles went, and the queue came to a stop in the way of a thing that is standing in a river and has been told it is not going to move; and Ehlers turned his head, once, and then sat with both hands on the wheel and his eyes in front of him in the ordinary way of a driver of the German administration of a city of the east.

"Gnädige Frau."

"Go on."

"Gnädige Frau will be asked for the papers of the household of the General of the district at the barrier in the eleven minutes, and the papers are in the bag of the gnädige Frau, and it is an ordinary check of the population of this street, and the four cars in front of us are going to be let through one by one, and the men of the formation of the east are not going to look at the Mercedes twice, because a car of the district of the intelligence service of the city of Warsaw is a car of the district of the intelligence service of the city, and there is not one man of the formation of the east who is going to look at it twice." He was speaking very fast and very quietly. "And in the meantime the formation of the east is going to be doing to this street what the formation of the east does."

She sat in the back of the car and watched it through the glass.

They took the tram first, because a tram is a building with a hundred and forty people in it and a building is the cheapest thing to take: two men at the front door and two at the back and one man inside with a list, and the people of the tram came out into the light of the end of the afternoon in the way of a slow queue, with the bags and the parcels and the children, and stood in the road with their hands at their sides while a sergeant of the formation of the east walked down them and looked at the papers and put his finger out and touched a man here and a boy there, exactly the way a man of the market touches a melon.

A man of forty in a clean shirt, with a rucksack on his back, and his wife beside him with her hand in his elbow, and the wife said something to the sergeant; and the sergeant listened to it, with his head on one side, in the way of a man of the police who has been in a city of the east for four years and has heard the sentences of the women of the population of it; and then he took the man by the elbow and put him inside the cordon and walked on down the queue, and the wife stayed where she was in the road with her hands at her sides and her mouth open, and did not say anything else in the time.

They took the queue of the shops at the door of the provision store: eleven women and two men from the counter and the customers. They took a boy of about seventeen who came round the corner at a run with his cap in his hand and put him inside the cordon at the fourth step that he took, in the ordinary way of a man catching a hen. They took two men out of the tram queue and a third out of the crowd of the onlookers on the corner and a fourth who had come because there was noise, and by the time the whistles went for the second time there were twenty-nine people in the middle of the cordon in the middle of the street with an armed man walking around the outside of them.

Then a captain of the formation of the east came down the line of the cars.

He was about forty, and the two ribbons of the eastern campaigns of the year 1941 and the year 1942 were on the left breast of the uniform of the shirt that he was wearing in the heat, and he came down the line of the cars, and at the third car — the car of the district of the intelligence service of the city of Warsaw, with the flag of the district on the left wing and the driver of the General of the district at the wheel — he stopped, and straightened, and put his hand to his cap in the salute of an officer of the German army of the east.

"Herr Hauptmann?" said Ehlers.

"Nothing at all, nothing at all," said the captain. "A car of the district in a street of a cordon of the police of the district. This afternoon has been laid down by the office of the district, and it will take eleven minutes, and there is nothing in the city of Warsaw at four o'clock on a Friday afternoon that the office of the district is not able to arrange in a manner that is quicker for the Frau of the household of the Herr General of the district of the intelligence service, and it is a pleasure for the office of the district to be at the disposal of the wife of the Herr General."

He turned and put up his arm to the line of the cars, and the two lorries of the cordon were moved by their drivers out of the mouth of the street, and the queue of the cars went forward, one at a time, through the middle of the cordon; and the Mercedes of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw went through the middle of them at the walking pace of a funeral, at four metres from the middle of the cordon, and the woman of the household of the General of the district of the intelligence service of the city of Warsaw looked out of the window of the car, at a distance of the width of a street, at the twenty-nine people of the city of Warsaw in the middle of the armed men, at the wife of the man of forty in the clean shirt with her hands at her sides, at the boy of seventeen with his cap in his hand, at the crowd of the onlookers on the corner of the street, and at the population of the city of her birth looking back at her through the glass of a German car.

There was a woman of about fifty at the edge of the crowd with a shopping net, and she looked at the car and at the woman inside the car for four seconds, with the flat, patient, bottomless attention of a population of a city that has been in the same war for four years and has begun to draw the accounts; and the woman in the car held her eyes for the four seconds, and then looked at the floor of the car and then at the street in front of it.

*They knew what I was in the year 1938,* she thought, in the back of a car going through a cordon of the police of the German district of Warsaw, at four o'clock in the afternoon of the fourteenth of May 1943, with the papers of the household in her bag. *The the city of Warsaw knew what I was: the Stankiewicz girl, the one with the father the professor, and because the population of this city keeps the accounts in the ten fingers of it, and the population of this city is in the four thousand windows of the four streets of the German quarter, and every one of the four thousand windows is able to see the German car that is going through the cordon at the walking pace of a funeral with the woman in the back of it.*

The tram came. The woman turned her head.

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna did the thing that decided the next fourteen months of her life, which was not to walk past her, and not to turn away, but to walk directly towards her, and stop, and take off her hat, and say:

"Madame Ostrowska. Do you remember me? It is Krystyna Stankiewicz."

And the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska, sixty-eight years old, of the house of Ostrowski of the Ślepowron, the aunt of a girl who had been at school with her, who had held her in her arms in 1929, who had lost a son at the Bzura in the second week of September 1939 and a husband in the first — the Countess looked at her for a long, long moment; and her eyes, which were the eyes of the oldest Polish aristocracy of that city, with the four hundred years in them, went from the hat, to the gloves, to the bag, to the shoes, to the face, in the order in which the women of her world looked at a woman they were being introduced to.

"Krystyna," she said. "My dear child. How you have grown."

And then she took her arm, in the middle of the street, and walked her eleven metres, and said, in a voice pitched for the two of them, at a volume *just* slightly too loud, in the terrible, beautiful way of a woman of her class who has spent her life talking over the noise of a drawing room:

"We shall not speak here; we shall speak on Thursday at my house, and you shall tell me everything about the south of France, because I hear that the wildflowers there are still going; and in the meantime, my dear, I want you to promise me something."

"Anything."

"Never take your hat off in the street," said the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska, "when you speak to a woman who knows you."
