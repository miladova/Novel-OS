# Chapter 22: Names and Numbers

The German civil administration of the district of Warsaw left the city on the morning of the thirty-first of July 1944, at half past five, from the station on the Ochota side of the town, and it left the way such a thing always leaves: in a fog of paperwork and tears, with a hundred and forty civilians, four hundred metres of freight cars, eleven lorries of files that had been loaded by Polish labourers under the eyes of German military police, and a locomotive that had been reserved for the ceremony since the twenty-fourth of July, and which was the last locomotive in Warsaw that anybody in the administration had any confidence in.

Ehlers drove her there at four o'clock in the morning. The streets were empty and white with dust, and there was a red half-light over the Vistula, and at the corner of the Kaliska a Polish woman was already at the pump, filling two buckets before the day; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, sitting in the back of the General's car with her case on her knees, watched a city get up on the last morning of its old life and thought, without any drama at all: *In thirty-seven hours the second of these mornings will be the wrong one.*

The platform was a blaze of electric light, because somebody had ordered that the station be illuminated for the departure of the civil administration of the district, and because nobody in the last week of July had been willing to countermand it. Under the lamps stood the kind of crowd that wars make: mothers with children sitting on the trunks; a district official of sixty in a good coat sitting on a chair that he had brought with him from his own office, on the platform itself, with his hat on his knees, refusing to look at anything; eleven German nurses of the Red Cross in a row with their cases, laughing too loudly; two men of the railway administration arguing with a sergeant of the Feldgendarmerie about the loading of a pianola; and children, a great many children, in the coats of a normal August morning, dragging their suitcases and asking their mothers whether the war was over.

She got out of the car into the smell of hot metal and coal smoke, and the first thing that she saw on the platform, three metres from the door of the car, was René Tissier's leather writing case, on the ground, beside the buffers of a freight car, with a boot-print across the flap.

She looked at it for one second, and then did not look at it again, and she walked down the platform beside the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw with her case in her hand.

They had arrested Tissier on the night of the twenty-ninth, at his flat in the Żoliborz, with the key of the cabinet in the pocket of his tunic; and they had taken his papers, and his uniform, and his writing case, and the writing case had come to the station on the cart of the office of the liaison with the rest of the abandoned equipment of an office that had stopped existing, and it was lying on the ground near the buffers, and nobody was going to pick it up. There was, in the platform, under the lamps, no sign anywhere of a Frenchman of fifty, and there never would be again; and the last of René Tissier was a leather case with a boot-print on the flap and a man in the Pawiak who was going to be one of the eleven thousand people in that place on the morning of the second of August, which was a piece of arithmetic that she did not know and never learned.

"Come," said the General.

He had been awake the whole night at the Blue Palace. There was a grey band round his face and his hands were not steady, and when he had come home at eleven o'clock the previous night he had gone straight past her into the study and had stood in front of the open safe for a long time with his back to the room; and she had watched the light of the study lamp on the shoulders of his tunic through the crack of the door and had thought: *the only thing that will destroy me now is the man's memory.*

They walked to the carriage. It was the third one, a second-class carriage of the Mitropa, with the blinds down, and the compartment reserved for the General had a card in a brass frame on the door: *Gen. d. Inf. v. Reuth / Bezirksverwaltung*. There was no name card for a woman, because the General of the intelligence of the district had refused to have one printed, and the refusal, in its own way, was the most tender thing that had ever happened to her in Warsaw.

Ehlers put the case on the rack.

"Frau Herschlag," he said. He was standing in the corridor with his cap in his hand, and his face was the face of a man who had eleven days to think about a woman coming in from the yard of the next street with a case in her hand. "The car will be at the Palace of the district at eleven, and the General has given the order that the car is to be kept at the house for the morning, and I am to drive the Frau wherever the Frau wishes to go, and I am to be back at the house at four o'clock with the car because the staff of the district is to be at the Palace at five." He put the cap on. "There is one thing more, and then I am going to the engine and I am not going to say another word to the Frau about it. There is a ferry at the ferry-place below the Citadel which is going to be running for the night of the thirty-first, and it takes a man or a woman for a hundred zloty, and it is the last of the eleven ways to the other side of the river, and the ways have been closed since the twenty-eighth. If it comes to it, the Frau should go to the ferry-place and not to any of the eleven."

"Ehlers."

"Yes, Frau."

"Why?"

"I have a photograph in the flap of my map case," said Ehlers, "and it is the photograph of a girl who does not have a street, and in my life in this administration I have done nothing at all with it, and the thing that a man does in a war when he has nothing at all is the thing that he does for a person who has also nothing at all, and that is the end of the subject." He looked at her. "The Frau should not go on the train."

"Why not?"

"Because the train is going to be in the open country between here and Skierniewice when the city is on fire," he said, "and when the railway line is cut at Grodzisk the train is going to be standing in a field with a hundred and forty German civilians in it and a locomotive, and the Russian aeroplanes are going to be over that field in the morning." He turned and went down the corridor, and stopped, and said over his shoulder, in the ordinary voice of a driver reporting that the car was full of fuel: "And because the photograph in the map case is of a girl who was in a street in Posen that the Russian aeroplanes came to, and I have never once in four years been able to tell a woman in this administration a true thing, and now I have told it, and there is not going to be any more."

---

Half past five.

The platform was full of people who were saying goodbye. It was the part of the morning that she had spent eleven days preparing, and the preparation, when it came, was worth nothing, because in the eleven days she had prepared the words and she had not prepared the silence, and it was the silence that came first.

The General stood beside the open door of the carriage in his greatcoat, with his gloves in his hand, and looked at her.

"Well?"

"Friedrich."

"The train leaves in six minutes," he said. "The Frau Präsidentin of the administration is in the fourth carriage, and there is a place reserved for a Frenchwoman of the household of the intelligence section, and I have in my pocket the pass and the warrant for the place, and I am going to hand them to you in the next minute, and then I am going to put you into that carriage with my own hands, and then I am going to get into the car and go to the Blue Palace, and at eleven o'clock tonight I am going to be in the house on the Bagatela, and I am going to have a bath, and I am going to drink a glass of the Silesian at the table in the small room, and there is going to be a woman in the chair by the window, because for two years the house has been a house in which a woman of the household has been in the chair by the window when I come in, and I have never once said a word to her about it because there is no word for it in my language." He took the two papers out of the pocket of his greatcoat and held them out. "Take them."

The lamps of the station were on. The pianola was going up the ramp at the end of the platform, two Poles and a sergeant swearing at it in two languages. Eleven metres away a German nurse of the Red Cross was crying over a suitcase, and behind her a boy of about nine was standing with his cap in his hand looking at the train, and the smoke of the locomotive was rolling along the roof of the station in the light.

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna looked at the two papers in the hand of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, and did not take them, and stood on the platform, and did the last arithmetic of the arrangement: *the pass is a warrant of the district; the train is a train; the country between here and Skierniewice is not a country; the ferry-place below the Citadel takes a man or a woman for a hundred zloty; and there is a house on the Bagatela with a study in which the safe has been opened twice, and the third time will be by German hands, and there is a card in the lining of my coat with my name on it and a pencil sentence on the back of it, and there is a paper wrapped in paper in the wardrobe of the room on the first floor that has been in a drawer of the Mokotowska for four years, and there is a boy of twenty-one with a bandaged hand who is going to be in the second week, and there is a folder of the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw behind the boiler of a laundry in the yard of the building in the next street, and there is a city of a million people which is going to stand up at five o'clock in the afternoon of the first of August, with or without the two papers in the man's hand.*

And she said the sentence that she had built in the tram, in her head, at half past seven in the morning, going the other way along the Marszałkowska:

"I am not going."

The platform went on around them. Somebody's child was shouting at the end of the train; the locomotive let off steam; the doors of the fourth carriage were being slammed one after the other by a sergeant.

"There is a reason," said the General.

"Yes. There is a reason." She put her hand on the edge of the carriage door, on the brass handle, and looked at him. It was the only lie of the four years that she had ever rehearsed out loud, and she had rehearsed it in the tram for eleven days and knew every syllable of it. "In 1940 the administration of the district took the wife of a lieutenant of the railway police in the third week of the occupation, in the courtyard of this building, and shot her, because a woman of the household of a German officer of the railway administration had been seen in the market of the Wola, buying a chicken, and there had been a report that a German had been seen in the same market buying a chicken, and the two of them were the same report, and there was not one German in Warsaw who was able to say the truth about either." She did not take her hand off the handle. "I am a woman of the household of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw. There are four thousand men in this city who know my face; there are eleven German households, a furier, three orderlies, a driver and a cook who know the colour of my coat; there are two hundred Poles on the register of the district who have written my name in a column; and on the day that the first of these people opens a mouth, the arrangement is over. If I get on this train and go into the Reich, I am not the woman of the General; I am a woman of France with a warrant of the district and a passport of Strasbourg and a residence in the Warsaw register of aliens and no husband in the world, and there is not one office in Germany that is going to have any of it, and every one of the four thousand men will have the day to think about me."

"Marion."

"You asked me for one word at the station," she said, "and it is: no. Not because I am brave. Not because I am a heroine of anything. Because you are my the world in the war, and because in the two years I have never once permitted myself a single thing, and because I am not going to spend the end of it in a train in a field in a country that is not mine, eating the bread of the administration with the Frau Präsidentin. I am going to be in the house on the Bagatela at eleven o'clock tonight, and there is going to be a bath, and a glass of the Silesian on the table in the small room, and the woman in the chair by the window, because a house of a General of the district of Warsaw is a house in which there is a woman in the chair by the window."

And then she found out that she was able to do the other part of it: she looked at him with her face whole, her hands steady, her voice even, and she did not plan the tears; the tears came on their own, the way water comes through a wall, out of a place in her that she had been managing for four years and had never once inspected, and she stood on the platform of the station on Ochota with the tears coming down her face and her chin up, and she said the last sentence of the performance in the flat, even voice of a woman who has been speaking to her own heart for two years in a mirror:

"Put the papers in your pocket, Friedrich. I am staying with you."

There was a silence that lasted four seconds.

And what happened in the four seconds was the thing that she had known, in the small room of the house on the Mokotowska, since the first week of the arrangement, and had never once let herself look directly at, because in her four years she had exactly one piece of luck and it was the luck of a woman in a bed, and now she was standing in it under the lamps of a station and it was looking back at her out of the face of a man of fifty-five: *he believes me. He has always believed me. Every piece of it, from the wine of the first dinner to the child that was never born, has been believed, and the reason it has been believed is not that I am good at it: the reason is that a man of fifty-five who has buried a wife and has never in his life been able to say a sentence about his own heart has been standing in the middle of a war for five years with one woman in the world who has found the sentence, and he is not able to give that up; and I have spent two years taking his secrets out of the study of the house on the Bagatela and the reason that I have been able to do it is that the man loves me and knows the price of the knowledge and has decided to pay it.*

The General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw put the two papers back in the pocket of his greatcoat, and closed his eyes for a moment, and then he took her face in both of his hands, in front of the platform, and kissed her in front of the administration of the district of Warsaw, which was a thing that he had never once done in a public place in two years, and behind them two German women in good coats looked at each other with their eyebrows, and a Polish labourer with a cap in his hand looked at the ground and slid a packet of cigarettes out of a German officer's discarded overcoat, in the same second, without any hurry at all.

"Eleven o'clock," said the General.

"Eleven o'clock."

He got into the carriage and stood at the window with his hand flat on the glass. The whistle went; the locomotive took up the slack with clanking and shouting; and the last train of the German civil administration of the district of Warsaw pulled out of the station at seventeen minutes to six in the morning, and she stood on the platform with the crowd watching the carriages, and the hands that came out of the windows waving, and the boy of nine on his mother's arm waving back at the train, and a woman's voice shouting one word over and over at a window — *Käthe, Käthe* — and there was nobody in the train who could hear it.

She walked the length of the platform to the exit in the smell of hot metal and a hundred and forty lives, and Ehlers was at the car, and she thought, with the precision that only happens in the middle of enormous events, of the leather writing case beside the buffers, and of the small, clever, exhausted face of the only man in Europe who had ever known what she was, and of the sentence that he had said in a marble hall: *in the end a house in the country is only a room with two names on the door.*

"To the house," she said. "And then, at eleven, I want the car to go to the aleja Szucha, and I want the driver to wait for me at the corner for as long as I am inside the building."

---

The city, on the thirty-first of July, smelled of burning paper.

It was the smell of Warsaw in the last week of the occupation: every office of the German administration in the city burned its own files in the last four days of July, in the courtyards, in the stoves, in the great boiler rooms and in the gardens, and the Śródmieście lay under a low, brown-black cloud that went up over the rooftops in the still air until noon, and the smoke had in it the particular thick sweetness of the paper of a bureaucracy, and people stopped in the streets and looked up at it, and knew exactly what it was, and said nothing at all.

She was in the aleja Szucha at half past eleven in the morning, and the smoke was coming up out of the courtyard of the building of the Sicherheitsdienst behind the wall in a brown column four metres wide; and the guards at the barrier of the entrance were standing with cloths over their faces, and there were Poles of the fire service of the city with the administration, because the administration had requisitioned them to keep the burning from spreading to the roof, and the Poles stood with their hoses and their axes and their smoke-blackened faces and did the work of eleven men each with both arms, and everybody in the street knew what it was that was being burned: the registers of the population of the district of Warsaw, and the files of the security service, and the card index of the Hauptsturmführer in the room on the second floor with the bars painted the colour of cream.

She stood at the corner of the street in the heat for eleven minutes, in a grey dress, in the last week of the occupation, with a bar of chocolate and two hundred zloty in her bag and a card in the lining of her coat, and looked at the smoke of the safest thing that had ever happened to her.

Then she went home to the house on the Bagatela and did the last of the packing, and it took an hour and eleven minutes and produced three objects.

The first was the parcel from the drawer of the Countess Jadwiga Ostrowska, warm and flat and the size of a book, which went into the lining of the winter coat, on the hook in the wardrobe of the room on the first floor; and the winter coat was, in the full summer of 1944, the most invisible object in the entire house, because at the end of July there is not a man in Europe who will look inside a winter coat.

The second was the small, flat, black-lacquered Minox, four hundred grams, of the pattern that the intelligence service of the district had bought in eleven lots in the winter of 1941 for the photography of the documents of the east. It had come out of an office of the district in April of 1943, in a parcel of typewriter ribbons, into the hands of a Frenchwoman of the household of the General; it had made its records in a bath, in a wardrobe, in a linen cupboard, at an open window at night, with the curtain drawn and the lamp under a towel; and now she loaded it with the last film of the arrangement — thirty-six exposures — and put it in the bottom of her bag, in the inside pocket of the lining, where a woman's hand goes for a lipstick.

The third was a page of her own writing, folded twice: five lines of résumé in her own hand of the five sheets of the folder of the Fall *Wotan*, the four dates, the two trains of the last week of July with the destinations in the west, and the line about the rolling stock that had been requisitioned in the district of Warsaw for the ninth of August. She wrote it in the laundry that afternoon, sitting on an upturned tub in the heat with an ironing board for a table, and it took her half an hour, and then she went to the tram stop at the corner of the Polna and stood in the queue and waited for the number 6.

The boy with the bandaged hand got on the tram behind her.

He was twenty-one, and his name was Janek, and he had been one of the eleven people in Warsaw who had ever seen her face and known her; and he stood beside her in the crowd of the tram with his right hand wrapped in a grey bandage and his left hand on the strap, and he was, in the four years of the occupation, the one person who had ever touched Krystyna Stankiewiczówna in a public place with her permission; and when the tram stopped at the corner of the Krucza and the crowd pressed, he put his left hand on her elbow, the way a grandson does for an elderly woman in a crowd, and the folded page of five lines passed from her bag to his sleeve, and neither of them looked at the other, and at the Rotunda he was off the car and gone down the Krucza into the crowd of the afternoon.

She got down at the Bagatela and walked the last street.

There was a woman in the doorway of the building next to the house with a bucket in each hand, taking water up to a second floor, and a man of seventy on the pavement outside the furier's with a chair and a hammer and four boards, repairing a door that had come off its hinge on the twenty-ninth; and in the garden of the house on the Bagatela, under the walnut tree, a Polish gardener of about sixty was picking up the dead leaves of July from the gravel in the ordinary way, with a rake, on the last afternoon of the occupation, and he looked up when she came to the gate, and he said, in Polish, very quietly:

"Frau, if it comes, put out the light."

And he went on raking the gravel.

---

The General came home at eleven o'clock in the night of the thirty-first of July, exactly as he had said on the platform of the station that morning, and the bath was drawn, and the glass of the Silesian was on the table in the small room, and the woman of the house was in the chair by the window with the blackout blind drawn and the lamp turned low.

He stood in the doorway of the small room and looked at her.

Then he took his cap off and put it on the sideboard and went, for the first time in the two years, on his knees on the carpet beside the chair, and put his face in her lap, and stayed there without saying anything for four minutes, and the shoulders of the tunic of a General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw shook once, and the fabric of the grey uniform was under her hand and the weight of the man was against her knees.

"I have three things to say to you," he said at last, into the grey wool of her dress. "The first is that the High Command of the army has ordered the district to hold the Vistula line until the middle of August, and that there is not one German officer, from the General commanding down to the last lieutenant of the engineers, who believes that the line can be held, and that everybody has known for eleven days that the Russians are going to be on this side of the river by the twentieth of September."

"And the second?"

"The second is that the Sicherheitsdienst have orders to take the population of the Pawiak to the west on the second, and that the eleven thousand people in that building are going to be in a great hurry on the morning of the second, and I have signed eleven of the papers for the transport because a general of the intelligence section of a district signs what the administration brings him, and there is not one of the eleven thousand who is going to see the inside of a German prison in Germany, and every man in the district knows it and no man in the district has said it out loud, and I have said it to you now because in the two years there has not been a single truth that I have kept from your attention."

"And the third?"

"On the second of August at four in the morning," said the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, with his face still in her lap, "I am going to be in the building of the command at the Blue Palace with the staff of the district, and the engineers are going to wire the district of the command, and the city is going to burn in eleven places at once, and there is going to be no way out of the centre of it in any direction, and the woman of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw is going to be in this house, four hundred metres from the building of the Sicherheitsdienst, in the middle of the German district of the city." He raised his head and looked at her. "And the third thing is that there is a car, and a driver, and a place in the district of the engineers on the northern bridge, and the order of the transfer of the household of the General of the district is on the table in the study, and it is for the Frau of the household, and the Frau of the household is going to be sitting in the car at five o'clock tomorrow afternoon with her case, whatever happens on the street, and Ehlers is going to drive it, because Ehlers has my trust for the four years of the war, and there is nobody else in the German administration in this city whom I trust with the object."

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, sitting in the chair by the window in the small room, with the hand of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw on her knee, took the last decision of the first part of her life, and said:

"Friedrich, I am not going to be sitting in any car at five o'clock tomorrow afternoon."

"Marion—"

"There is a house," she said. "There is a house with a study in it and a safe in the study and your papers in the safe. There is the intelligence service of the district of Warsaw in that study, and if the city rises tomorrow, there is not one German soldier in the building of the command who is going to be able to come to the Bagatela and put it in a case; and I have spent two years in this house and I have never once been asked for anything by it, and tomorrow I am going to be in this house; and at five o'clock in the afternoon I am going to put your papers in the safe and close it and turn the dial twice on the last number, which is a trick that I was taught by a man named Thomas in the rue de Rivoli in Paris in the spring of 1943, and then I am going to sit in this chair in the blackout and wait for you to come home."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"You are not to be in this house tomorrow," he said.

"I am going to be in this house tomorrow."

"Marion."

"Friedrich," said Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, and she put both her hands on his face, and she was in that minute exactly what she had been made to be: the one object of the life of a man of fifty-five, and the instrument of the death of the German garrison of a city of a million, in the same room, in the same chair, in the same pair of hands. "I have never once in my life asked you for anything either. I am asking you to believe me on the last night, and to go to the Palace at four o'clock tomorrow, and to leave the house and the papers in my hands, and to come home at eleven o'clock like the man on the platform of the station on the Ochota this morning; and if it is not possible for a man in this city to come home at eleven o'clock on the first of August because there is a war between the district of the command and his house, then there is still one thing that is possible, and it is a telephone call, and there is a telephone in the study and a telephone in your office, and I am going to be in this house until I hear the telephone. That is the thing. That is all of it. That is everything that I have ever wanted out of the war, and I want it handed to me now, in this room, with your hand in mine, before either of us is a person in a war."

And the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, on the thirty-first of July 1944, at half past eleven in the night, in the small room of the house on the Bagatela, in the blackout, said: "Yes."

---

The first of August 1944 was a hot, still, brilliant day, with the high white August light of Poland standing over the city and not one German aeroplane in the sky, because the German aeroplanes were on the airfields at Kraków and Łódź and there were no aeroplanes in Warsaw on the first of August.

She was up at six, and she made the coffee herself, because the cook was in the third day of the ordinary German terror of the civilian in a city that is about to be a battlefield, and had spent the previous evening in the kitchen with a case packed and her hands in her apron, asking every half hour whether the Frau thought the trains were running.

At eight she gave the cook the money in the house, eleven hundred and forty zloty, and a letter in her own hand to the sister in Bydgoszcz, and sent her out to the station at the Ochota with the female porter of the house opposite; and the woman cried in the hall and kissed her hand, in the German way, and said the thing that German women had been saying to other German women in the east for the last year:

"You should come, Frau. They will kill us all, and you are not one of them, and they will kill you worst of all, and you have been kind to me for two years and four months, and I have not once in the time understood a single thing about this house."

Then the door closed and the house on the Bagatela was empty, and it stayed empty for the rest of the morning.

She did the day in the order in which she had written it on the back page of a notebook in the laundry of the yard of the building in the next street, and burned the page in the stove.

At ten o'clock she took the amber necklace, the parcel of the Countess, the three things that were hers in the world, and sewed the parcel into the lining of the winter coat with a needle and a black thread in the wardrobe; and it was the first time in four years that her hands shook, and she stopped, and stood in the dark of the wardrobe with her hands in her lap until the shaking had stopped, because a woman with a needle and a shaking hand makes a thread that a person can see.

At eleven o'clock the city began to get ready.

She heard it from the closed windows of the house on the Bagatela, which stood on the edge of the German district of the city, four hundred metres from the Sicherheitsdienst and the same distance from the avenue, and what she heard, in the middle of the day, was not the ordinary city of an occupied capital: it was the sound of a million people being very busy and very quiet at the same time. There was no tram on the Marszałkowska at twelve; there were no Polish shops open at one; there were, at two in the afternoon, four German lorries of the garrison going up the avenue with the men sitting in them with their rifles on their knees, and then no more of them; and at half past two she stood at the window on the landing of the first floor, behind the lace, and watched a woman of about forty in a black coat coming down the Bagatela on the pavement opposite with a shopping basket on her arm, walking fast and unhurried at the same time, exactly the way the Poles of Warsaw walked on the first of August 1944; and behind her, twenty metres further down the street, came a young man with no parcel and no basket and no destination, whose whole business in the street was to be twenty metres behind the woman of forty; and the two of them went down the Bagatela and round the corner of the Polna, and the street was empty.

At three o'clock the telephone rang.

It was the General, from the Blue Palace, and he had the flat voice of a man who had been in a room with twenty officers for six hours; and he said: "The district of the command is at full alert, and the garrison is in the buildings, and the Governor's people say that there is nothing whatever going to happen in this city, and the intelligence section of the district knows that there is something going to happen in this city, and I have looked at the map of the district for the day and I have told the office at eleven o'clock that the women of the staff of the district of the command are to be in the cellars of the Palace by four o'clock, and there is exactly one of them who is not going to be, and she is in the house on the Bagatela, and she has been the day in the house, and she has sent the cook out of the city, and she has promised the man who is talking on the telephone a bath and the Silesian and the woman in the chair by the window."

"I have."

"There is one more thing, and it is not about it," said the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw. "At four o'clock this afternoon the staff of the district is going to the Palace, and the papers of the district are going with us, and the safe in the study is going to be sealed and left, and I have told the Ordnungsoffizier that the key of the house of the General is to remain in the hands of the General, and the Ordnungsoffizier of the district is a young captain of the intelligence section who has been in this building for the year, and I have said to him at three o'clock this afternoon, in the corridor of the office, that the household of the General is in the house and that he is not, under any circumstance, to be in the house in the afternoon, and that is a sentence that a General says to a captain of twenty-nine exactly once in a war, and I have said it, and that is the telephone call."

The line was bad. She could hear an office behind him: a typewriter, a man's voice reading figures, a telephone ringing somewhere in a corridor of a district of a city that was going to be on fire in an hour and a half.

"Friedrich."

"Yes."

"Come home at eleven o'clock," she said, in the flat voice of a woman of the household of a General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw, into the telephone of the study of the house on the Bagatela, on the first of August 1944, at three o'clock in the afternoon. "I am going to be in the chair by the window with the blackout blind drawn and the lamp turned low."

---

She was alone in the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw at a quarter to five in the afternoon of the first of August 1944.

The house was cool and dark behind the blinds, and it smelled of beeswax and the dead leaves of the walnut tree; and the life of the arrangement was standing in the hall of it, over the sideboard of the hall, waiting to be wrapped in a piece of newspaper: the porcelain dog that Anneliese had put on the sideboard in 1932 and the silver of the family and the portrait photograph in the study; and the woman of the house came out of the small room, in a grey dress and stockings and a pair of grey shoes, with her hair up in the style of the women of the avenue Henri-Martin as of the summer of 1944; and she stood in the marble hall and looked at the clock on the stair, and the clock said fourteen minutes to five.

She went to the window of the drawing room, and stood behind the blackout blind, and lifted one edge of it with her finger.

The Bagatela in the last quarter of an hour was a street with nothing in it at all. There was no tram in the avenue; the yellow building opposite had eleven windows with the blinds down; the Polish gardener had gone at noon with the rake over his shoulder; and at the corner of the Polna, where the pavement went round into the little street of the Novum, there was a young man sitting on the step of a doorway with a book on his knee, reading it in the ordinary way, in the ordinary afternoon; and she watched him turn the page, and she saw it: the page was turned in the direction that a man turns a page when he is not reading, and under the book, on his knee, in the fifth year of the German occupation of the city of Warsaw, there was a wristwatch, and he was looking at it every eleven seconds.

She let the blind fall.

Then she stood in the middle of the drawing room of the house on the Bagatela with her hands at her sides, and the sound of the house was the tick of the clock on the stair of the hall and the sound of her own breathing and, in the far distance, down in the direction of the Ujazdowskie avenue, one single telephone ringing in an office where nobody was going to answer it.

Five minutes.

She heard the front gate.

There was a step on the gravel of the garden and a step on the stone at the door, and it was not the sentry, because the sentry of the house of the General of the intelligence of the district of Warsaw had gone to the Palace at four o'clock with the guard; and there was one knock, and it was a knock that she had heard on every door of her childhood, and it was the knock of a man who has been told not to be in the house in the afternoon.

The clock on the stair said three minutes to five, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna went across the marble hall of the house on the Bagatela, in a grey dress, on the first of August 1944, with the doll's house and the silver on the sideboard behind her and the winter coat with the parcel in the lining upstairs, and put her hand on the brass of the door.

And the first shot of the Rising of the city of Warsaw came, from somewhere down in the direction of the Wola, one street over, the way a shot comes at the beginning: not in the least like the end of a world, but like a door.

Then the street gave it back to her, in eleven seconds, from the Polna, from the corner of the Novum, from the roofs, from all four directions of the city, and the glass of the fanlight over her head came down in a white rain on the marble of the hall.
