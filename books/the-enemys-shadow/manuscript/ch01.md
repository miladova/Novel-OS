# Chapter 1: The Diamond Room

He slept the way a man stands at attention: on his back, arms at his sides, chin up, as though even unconscious he might be called upon to report. Marion Herschlag lay on her side of the bed with her eyes open in the dark and counted his breathing, and when she had counted a hundred she began again from the beginning, because a hundred was all the counting she could do without moving her lips, and moving her lips was a thing he noticed.

The apartment was cold. Coal came to the avenue Henri-Martin in sacks with a military docket on them and burned in a marble fireplace built for a different century, and the cold that came in through the tall windows was not the honest cold of a street but something older and thinner, the cold of a room that had been loved once by other people. The clock in the hall struck three. Under her hip the sheet was still damp where he had put her on her stomach an hour ago and held her there with one hand flat between her shoulder blades — not cruelly, not even roughly, but with the terrible patience of a man signing a document.

She had learned to keep her face still in the minutes after. He liked to look at her afterwards. He would prop himself on one elbow and study her body the way he studied a map, and there was nothing she could do about it except be studied and remember to breathe. In October, in the first week, he had told her that she had the shoulders of a peasant and the hands of a countess, and she had laughed and said that he had the hands of a man who had never once in his life had to open a door for himself, and he had struck her across the face. Not hard. A courtesy blow, like a man pushing a dog away from a table. Then he had taken her against the window with the blackout curtain trembling an inch from her cheek and the glass so cold it burned, and afterwards he had apologised with his mouth against the back of her neck, and she had understood him in that hour. He was not a sadist. That was the thing nobody in Paris understood about Oberst Friedrich von Reuth. He was a man in mourning for an order of the world, and every woman he took to bed was a small ceremony in which the lost order was briefly restored.

He slept. The clock in the hall wound itself on. Three-nineteen in the morning, the eleventh of January, nineteen forty-three.

Her eyes had been open long enough to read the room by. The rugs rolled back from the bed and stacked against the wall, because he did not like the way they slid under his boots. His tunic over the chair with the Knight's Cross pinned to the breast as though the chair had earned it. And on the low table by the windows, where he had set it down when he came in at eight o'clock with his cap still under his arm, his briefcase.

It was not locked. He had never locked a thing in his life in her presence. That was the arrangement, and she had made it deliberately, in bed, in November, in the voice she used when she wanted something and could pretend she was only teasing. *Do you know what a woman thinks about when a man leaves the room? Not where he is. What he left.* And he had laughed, that short laugh like a door closing, and said, *You may think about it as much as you like, my dear. It is all in my head. That is the only safe place.*

She let the count run out to two hundred. Then she moved.

Out of the bed in one slow uncoiling, weight first on the outside foot, which was the one that did not make the frame complain. Her nightdress — a ribbon and a yard of grey silk, chosen because it made a sound when it moved and therefore taught her to move soundlessly — lay on the floor by the footboard. She did not stop for it. The cold took the skin of her thighs and her arms in one bright announcement, and she stood still and let it come, and let her body remember that cold was information, nothing more.

Three steps to the window. The briefcase was black leather, worn at the corners by twenty years of railway carriages, with his initials in gold. She did not open it where it lay. She carried it, breathing through her nose, to the bathroom, because the bathroom had a door, and because the door was the difference between a woman who was curious and a woman who was a spy.

The bathroom had belonged to someone who liked sycamore and green marble. There was a shaving mirror on a jointed arm and a comb with two long black hairs still in it that were not hers. There was a bar of soap in the dish, cracked with age, with the imprint of a name that Marion Herschlag had never been permitted to learn. Twice a week a German orderly cleaned this room and left the soap where it was. Nobody had told him to. Nobody had the authority to tell him. It had simply survived, the way certain things survived in Paris, like the concierge who could not hear German and the cats in the courtyard who went on being fed.

She closed the door, put the briefcase on the closed lid of the toilet, and opened it. She did not have to look for anything. She knew where he kept the papers: in the flat compartment under the lid, because he was a man who had spent a war being shelled and had learned to distrust anything folded. What was in there was a map of Le Havre harbour with pencilled annotations, a bundle of railway timetables, a typed memorandum of four pages she could not read at a glance, and a single carbon copy, thinner than the others, of a list.

She had four minutes. She had measured her last four visits to this room against the clock in the hall, and it was always four, because his body was regulated like a garrison and his bladder woke him at four and a half.

The list was in French. That was the first thing that caught and held her eye, because everything else in that briefcase was in German, and a French document in that briefcase at that hour meant that a French document had come from somewhere that frightened people. It was headed *Éléments à rechercher — zone sud*, and under the heading were names.

Lists of names were ordinary. Paris in 1943 was a city of lists, and every list was somebody's death. She had carried nine documents out of this apartment in five months, and eight of them had been lists, and she had learned to handle them the way a butcher handles a carcass: by the weight, and not the face. But she read the fourth name on the second page, and stopped.

*Szmul Reinhold, tailleur, 14 rue des Rosiers.*

She knew the address because she had once, in another life, in the autumn of 1939, when things had still been merely terrible and not yet finished, eaten a bowl of sorrel soup in a basement on the rue des Rosiers and been talked to for an hour by a tailor's wife about the price of buttons.

She stood in the cold bathroom and looked at the name until the letters stopped being letters. Then she put the picture of the tailor's wife behind her ribs, where she kept everything else, and took from the pocket of her dressing gown — which she had remembered to bring after all — a small black camera the size of a matchbox.

It took two hands and eleven seconds to set. The trick was the shutter, which was a lever rather than a button and made a sound like a fingernail tapping glass, so she wrapped the whole body of the camera in the edge of the green velvet curtain that hung, absurdly, beside the bath, and pressed it down against her thigh, and did it twice. She did it twice because nine names on the second page and a heading in French meant paper that would travel by courier, and a courier could be held up a week; the film would be in Warsaw in four days.

She wound the film back with her thumb, not fast, not slow, and then sat on the lid of the toilet with the briefcase on her knees and her hands shaking, which they always did afterwards, and which she permitted for exactly as long as it took to count nine breaths.

She was careful to keep it that way, the world believing she had nerves of steel, because the truth was a system of allowances. The hands were allowed to shake in the bathroom. The mind was allowed a minute of the street in Warsaw where she was born, if it wanted one. The body was allowed to be angry for as long as it took to dress. Everything else was expenditure, and she had been taught in a room in Kraków, in the winter of 1940, by a small man with a bad heart who went by a dog's name, that expenditure was how a woman was caught.

*Memory is the only suitcase they cannot open, my little rabbit. Everything else is theirs.*

She put the camera in the toe of her second-best shoe. She put the list back in the briefcase in the exact alignment of the rest, spine against the leather, the corner of the map pointing east, exactly as it had been. She stood, looked at herself in the shaving mirror, and did the last of it.

It took forty seconds and it was nothing but arithmetic, and arithmetic was how she survived. Names. Szmul Reinhold, tailor, 14 rue des Rosiers. Second page of the carbon. Two names above, five below. The heading. The date stamp in the corner, *22 XII 42*, and under it, in pencil, the letters *III b*, which meant nothing to her and would mean something to someone.

She closed the briefcase. She breathed. She put her shoulders back the way a woman does when she is about to walk into a room and has decided what the room is going to see.

On her way out she looked at the bathtub and thought, without meaning anything by it, that this was the only room in the apartment where she had ever been alone.

---

He was awake when she came back.

She knew it three steps from the bed, from the change in the air. There was no light; the tall window had a blackout curtain the colour of cream, and cold came off it in a thin draught that reached her ankles. She made herself walk to the bed. She made herself not hurry. Under the sheet he was a long shape in the dark, and she could feel him looking at her the way you feel a hand lifted near your face.

"The water," he said. "I heard the water."

"There was no water."

"Then the pipe."

"There was no pipe either." She sat on the edge of the bed with her back to him, because a woman who is lying goes on being busy. "I couldn't sleep. And I thought I might be sick, and I would rather not be sick on the carpet of a house I don't own."

There was a pause. She could hear the clock in the hall and, beneath it, a long way off, the low continuous mutter of Paris, which is the sound of a city occupied: a truck, a dog, and an engine idling.

"Come here," he said.

She came. She lay down on her back, and he turned his head and looked at her from a distance of two inches: the pale eyes, the heavy brow, the mouth of a man who had been handsome at twenty-five and had spent the rest of his life using what was left. He reached out and put his hand flat on her stomach, the way he did when he wanted to know whether she was lying. It was the same hand that had put her on her stomach an hour before. It was the same hand he signed things with.

"Your hands," he said.

"Mm."

"Cold."

"It's January."

He lay still for a while, and she lay looking up at the ceiling, where the shadow of the curtain lay across the plaster like a bandage, and thought about eleven seconds, and velvet, and a click that was not a click. Nine names. Five below, two above.

"I had a telegram at nine o'clock," he said at last. "You were in the bath."

"You never tell me about your telegrams."

"No." His thumb moved once along her hip. "Röhrig was shot on Tuesday. In the rue de la Pompe, in the middle of the day, by two men on a bicycle. They put three bullets in him and took his briefcase."

She lay perfectly still. It was not hard. It was the one thing five months in this house had taught her beyond price: that the surface of her face belonged to her as long as she did not think about it.

"Was he in your office?"

"He was at the Militärbefehlshaber." He said it flatly, the way he said everything that mattered. "He was carrying a list of people to the rue des Saussaies. It makes no difference now. It is a stone in a stream."

"It is not."

He turned his head. She looked at him with her mother's face, which was the face of a woman who was never afraid of anyone, and which she had discovered, in this room, wearing this skin, was the most useful thing she had ever been given.

"Someone is putting bicycles in the middle of your day," she said. "I would not sleep either."

For a moment she thought she had made a mistake. He lay there with his hand on her stomach, and she watched him calculate, and there was a long flat tone in the room, like the held breath before an orchestra comes in, in which he could have asked her any question at all. Then he laughed: once, from the chest, the way a man laughs when someone has put a finger exactly on the bruise he has spent the evening hiding.

"Rot," he said. "It is all rot. But yes. One of them was a boy. Twenty-two, on a bicycle, in a good coat. It is the good coats I mind. In 1918 it was the good coats."

"Don't," she said. "Don't do the war with me. Not tonight. Not in this bed."

He was silent. Then he rolled onto his back and looked up at the ceiling, and she could feel the heat coming off him, the same heat as an hour before, banked down and working. His hand slid down her stomach and closed on the inside of her thigh. Not as a question. He asked nothing with that hand.

She had learned to divide her life into rooms, because it was the only way to carry it. On the far side of the curtain she was Marion Herschlag, and nothing at all, and safe. On this side she was the body of a woman with a job to do, and the job had been explained to her in Kraków in a sentence she had never been able to stop hearing: *they will never look at your hands if they are looking somewhere else.*

So she turned to him in the way that had taken her two years and four men to learn: without hurry, without pretending, without once letting him think he had won the room. "No," she said, and moved his hand away, and heard him breathe. "Not like that. Like this."

She took him into her mouth in the dark with the sheet over her shoulders, and it was neither love nor hate, and it was not a punishment and not a favour. It was work, like any other work. That was the thing she would never be able to say to anybody in Warsaw, or in London, or in any of the rooms where she was a document instead of a woman: that for four minutes in a cold bedroom in Paris, with her eyes closed, she had chosen this, because choosing it made it hers.

He made a sound. She knew his sounds the way a farmer knows weather. His hand came down on the back of her head, heavy, proprietary, and she lifted her free hand and closed it around his wrist — not to move it, to *hold* it, to remind him who was where — and the sound he made after that was something she never let herself think about afterwards, because it was not the sound of a colonel or a widower or a man in mourning for the world. It was the sound of a man untied, and it was intimate in a way no other minute of the day was intimate, and intimacy was the thing she was paid to take from them, and she had never once been paid for this.

When it was over he did not say anything. He put his arm over his eyes, which was a thing he did, and after a while his breathing flattened out, and she lay beside him in the dark, awake, with the taste of him in her mouth and the film in her shoe and the tailor's name folded behind her ribs in the cold place where the tailor's wife was, and she did not sleep.

At four-forty she heard the floor creak in the corridor: Kleist, the Colonel's orderly, going down to the kitchen to put the kettle on the gas ring, exactly as he did every morning, in his slippers. Fifty-seven years old, a photograph of two children in the pocket of his tunic, terrified of the rank they had given him at the depot in Breslau. He would be down there now; he would lay out the razor; he would warm the water in the small copper pot that had come from the kitchen of the woman who used to live here.

She turned on her side and put her face into the pillow, and in the warm dark that smelled of him she permitted herself the other thing she was permitted, which lasted one minute and never longer. She permitted herself the rue des Rosiers, and the soup, and the sound of a doorbell, and the certain knowledge that somewhere in this city, in the files of the Militärbefehlshaber, in the mind of a clerk with good handwriting, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna was — by some clause of paper, some list drawn up before she was born — still, in a technical sense, a person.

Then she got up, and went to work.

---

The morning was Kleist's. If the nights belonged to the Colonel, the mornings belonged to a fifty-seven-year-old man with two children in a photograph, and Marion had come to think of that hour as the only honest hour of the day.

He brought the tray at seven. Coffee was a memory of a thing; what was in the pot was chicory, dark and faintly sweet, with a slice of bread and a smear of ersatz jam, the Colonel's iron pills, the post, and a pressed carnation from the hothouses of the avenue, which were German now, like everything else that flowered.

"Guten Morgen, gnädige Frau."

"Good morning, Herr Kleist."

It was the single German sentence she permitted herself, and she permitted it because it kept him safe. There was a rule in the house, written nowhere: the Poles spoke French, the Germans spoke German, and the woman in the bedroom belonged to the first category. Paris had decided that Marion Herschlag was Alsatian, which was why her consonants sat slightly wrong in their chairs; Alsatians were, for Wehrmacht accounting purposes, almost German, and for SD purposes a permanent question. She had never once had to defend it.

Kleist set the tray as he always did, but this morning he did something else. He stood in the doorway with his cap in both hands, looked past her at the window, and said, quietly, in German, "There is a man in the street."

"What kind of man?"

"A young one. He has been there since six. He is doing nothing." He looked at her then, and she watched him decide how much of his own thought to lay down on the tray with the coffee. "There is a book under his arm. It never changes."

"Perhaps he is a student."

"Perhaps," said Kleist, who had been a bank clerk in Breslau for twenty years and had a son in a graves-registration office in Smolensk, and who had spent the last winter keeping a photograph dry in a pocket. "The Oberst will be at the Majestic until eight. I will be in the kitchen if the Fräulein needs anything."

He went out. She stood in the middle of the room with the tray in front of her and the smell of ersatz coffee going up like a rumour, and she did not go to the window, because going to the window was the first thing she wanted to do and the last thing she was entitled to do. The curtains were cream, the window was tall, and the man in the street had a book and a good coat, and good coats were what the Colonel minded, and the Colonel's orderly had just walked into a bedroom in the sixteenth arrondissement and said so out loud, to a woman he believed to be from Strasbourg, because he had decided that this was the kind of house in which somebody had to say something true at seven o'clock in the morning.

She ate the bread and drank the coffee down, and while it was still going down she went through the four questions she went through every morning, in order, because order was what kept the fear in a box. Who has seen me where I should not have been. Who has heard me say something I should not have said. Who has seen my hands. Who has seen the inside of my head.

The answers, since October, were: nobody, nobody, nobody — except that the last one had to be looked at properly, in daylight, like the blade of a knife. Because here was the trick they did not teach you in Kraków, and which she had grasped in the fourth month, in the green marble bathroom: a spy did not fail because somebody noticed that she was clever. A spy failed because somebody noticed that she was *careful*. So she had made herself careless in exactly the right places. She left books in the bath until the paper wrinkled. She was late, sometimes, to meals. She was occasionally, deliberately, too much of a coward to open a door at night, and the Colonel liked that; the Colonel liked a woman who needed his arm on the stairs.

She went down the corridor to the small room at the back that the Colonel believed she used for her dresses and which was in fact a room with a north light and one window onto the courtyard, through which, standing a certain way behind the curtain, you could see a slice of the street door.

There was a man in the street with a book under his arm. He was young, in a good grey coat, and no hat, which in January meant either a fool or a message. She stood behind the curtain and watched him for forty breaths and beat her own body at its own game, because the mad, hot, stupid thing that came up in her chest every time she saw a stranger who might be a Pole was the thing that would kill her, and it had to be lived with in daylight, like the cold, and made into arithmetic.

The boy turned a page he had not read. He looked at his watch, walked to the corner, looked down the avenue, came back, and stopped in the same place. Then he tipped his head back and looked up, quite deliberately, at the third floor, for a count of three, and walked away.

She stood with her hand on the shutter for a long minute after he had gone.

Then she went to get dressed, because at eleven she had an appointment about a hat.

---

Odile Marchand had a shop in the rue Cambon with a green door and no name on it, because in Paris a shop without a name had a clientele, and a clientele without names had money. She made hats for the wives of the German administration, and she made them with the kind of obstinate, exquisite, faintly sarcastic craft that said, to anyone capable of reading it, that the folding of a hatband by a French hand was the last irreducible frontier of Europe.

She was thirty. She had been beautiful in a way that had been described in three newspapers before the war. Her hands were the only luxurious thing about her now, and she did everything with them: she talked with them, she lied with them, she cut, she smoothed, she touched the collar of your dress to fix it, and when you had known Odile six weeks you would have let her hold your life in her left hand and your purse in her right and lost track of which was which.

"Madame Herschlag." She came round the counter and took both of Marion's hands and held them out at arm's length, looked at them, and instead of kissing her on both cheeks kissed the air beside her ear and said, very quietly, in Polish, "There is a bad smell in the street."

"Four of them?"

"Two, and a car with the engine off. Since Thursday."

"Because of Röhrig."

"Because of something." Odile released her, went back behind the counter, and held up a shape of green felt in both hands, all business. "The Colonel will want the black, I think. I have almost finished the black."

"The black," Marion agreed, and sat in the low chair by the mirror, and looked at a hat, and talked about a hat, for eleven minutes, which was how long it took Odile to be sure.

*Röhrig, shot in the rue de la Pompe. They are watching the avenues near the office. There is a man with a book outside the house at six o'clock this morning; I have no photograph of him, he has no hat, he is young, grey coat, he read a page that was not a page.*

Odile listened to a description of a hat the way other women listened to a confession. When Marion finished she said, "The black will want a veil. A short one. Because the Fräulein's face is not a face that should be looked at from all angles at once," and she smiled, and pointed with her chin at the long mirror, and Marion stood and looked at herself in it: the grey wool dress, the plain earrings, the hair that a hairdresser on the avenue had taught to fall in exactly the way that cost the Reich nothing.

"He is coming for me at eight," she said. "He is taking me to the theatre."

"Then he will want you to wear the green."

The green was silk, pre-war, from a couturier who had gone to New York. It had belonged to a woman who no longer lived in the apartment on the avenue Henri-Martin, and Marion had found it in the wardrobe in the second bedroom in November, hanging with eleven others behind a curtain, and when she asked, the Colonel had said only, *Take them. They are regulated now.* She had worn nine of them. She never wore the ninth, which was a pale, soft, dove-coloured thing with a wide collar, because it had a smell in the lining that came up when you pressed your face to it, which she had done exactly once.

"The green, then," she said.

Odile was pinning something. Then, without looking up: "The boy who came."

"Which boy?"

"Do not be stupid with me. The boy who walks on the avenue at six o'clock in the morning with a book. He came in on Tuesday and wanted to know the price of a felt." The pins went flick, flick. "I told him three hundred francs. He did not blink. He is not a buyer, and he is not police, because police do not waste an afternoon on felt. He is one of ours, or he is yours, and I do not know which, and it would interest me a great deal to know which."

"You don't have a network," Marion said. "You have a hat shop."

"I have a hat shop and a brother in the Auvergne with a wireless set and a very bad temper," said Odile. "*You* have a colonel, and he has a briefcase, and last Friday a man from the rue des Rosiers came to my back door and asked me to ask you whether the list has moved." She looked up. "He was polite. He was very polite. He had a paper bag with a chicken in it, which is a thing that no longer exists in Paris, and he wanted me to understand that it was not a bribe but a courtesy. That is how they are, your people. They bring you a chicken and ask you for a miracle."

Marion sat in the low chair and looked at herself in the mirror, and the woman in the mirror looked back with her mother's face.

"Nine names," she said. "Tell him nine names, and two above, and five below, and Reinhold is one of them, and if the chicken is still a chicken on Thursday, then he should ask me again, and I will tell him what the paper under the photograph in the Colonel's study says, because he has taken to keeping the paper under the photograph."

Odile put down her pins. "Under the photograph of the dead wife?"

"Under the photograph of the dead wife."

There was a silence in which the shop put itself back together. Then Odile said, in a completely different voice, the voice of a woman humming at a counter at eleven in the morning in the rue Cambon, "Would you like to see the veil, madame? I think the veil. Not the feathers. Never the feathers. Feathers are for women who have not decided which war they are going to survive."

She was wrapping the hat in tissue when she said, in Polish, without looking up — and this was the thing about Odile, the thing that made the two of them possible: she never asked the question when it could be done with the hands.

"Does he know?"

"Who?"

"The boy in the street."

"He is not mine," said Marion. "I have no one in the street. I have a hat, a colonel, and an address in Alsace where a woman named Marion Herschlag was born in 1919, and all of it is true on paper, and none of it exists, and if you ever meet anyone who claims to have known me before October, that person is a liar or a policeman, and you should run."

"The Colonel will be pleased with the hat," Odile said, and tied the string.

---

Out in the street the air was a colour between ash and milk. The trees along the Champs-Élysées had been pruned into knuckles, the German flags on the hotel fronts hung limp and enormous, and there was a queue outside the butcher's on the corner that had been there since five in the morning and would be there at five the next morning, and it was, in every direction, an ordinary Thursday in an occupied city, which is the most terrible thing a city can be.

She walked, because a woman of her position did not use the Métro unless she wanted to be looked at, and because walking made her feel the streets were hers, which was a lie, but a useful one. Twice she stopped at a shop window with her hands in her pockets and looked at the reflection behind her: the crowd, the grey of it, a boy on a bicycle, a girl with a basket, a fat woman with a poodle, and in the middle distance, keeping pace with her at the crawl of a man who has nowhere to be, a grey coat.

She did not hurry. She crossed the avenue at the lights the way the best-dressed women in Paris crossed, with total disregard for everything except her shoes, and went into the church of Saint-Roch, because it was Thursday and the church was full of women who had all claimed to be somewhere else, and because there was a stairwell on the north side where you could be alone with a candle for four minutes and nobody in Paris would wonder why.

The candles were cheap and the saints were real. She put a franc in the box and lit one for a tailor and one for a man who had been shot in the rue de la Pompe, whom she had never met and who might very well have been the man who signed her death, and she sat down in the dark and put her head in her hands and allowed herself ninety seconds of being twenty-five years old.

Then she took the matchbox out of her shoe.

The stone niche behind the second column of the north aisle was where the parish kept a broom and a bucket, and where in winter a draught came down from the gallery and moved the dust along the floor in long slow fingers. She knelt by the niche as though to fix a stocking, put her hand in the dark at the back of it, and set the matchbox down, and felt with her fingertips for the tin that should have been there, and found it: a flat tobacco tin, out of which she took a folded square of paper the size of a stamp, and into which she put nothing, because it was not emptied by her.

She had one job here, and it was to be someone else entirely if anyone came.

Nobody came. She read the paper in the light of the candles, holding it inside her glove. It was four words, in a hand she knew better than her own: *MOTHERS MEET THURSDAY. SAME HOUR.* Which was Lis, with a bad heart and a worse sense of humour, telling her that a courier was confirmed and the road to Marseille was open, and that she should keep doing what she was doing, and that the network was still alive.

She burned the paper over the candle, which was a thing that had cost her a burn on the inside of her wrist in Prague in 1941, and which she had learned to do with the edge of a fingernail ever since, and she watched the ash go up and settle on the shoulder of a saint.

When she came out into the rue Saint-Honoré, the grey coat was standing at the corner forty metres away, looking at the window of a shoemaker as though the shoemaker's window had ever in the history of the world required looking at.

She walked east a hundred metres, then turned north into the small streets behind the Tuileries, where the wind came off the river and the buildings were dark with soot and a woman could hear her own footsteps on the pavement and count them, and understand, in that cold, clean, animal calculation that was the only kind of thought she still trusted, exactly what her body was for and how long it could be expected to last.

He followed. He was twenty metres behind her when she came out on the quai. She had decided in the rue Saint-Honoré that she was going to let him follow, because a woman who turns and looks at the man behind her is a woman who knows she is being followed, and a woman who has nothing to hide never notices anything at all — and because there was, in the deepest and most carefully locked drawer of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna's file, a hope she had been carrying since the platform at the Gare de l'Est in October, and it was this: that someday someone from home would look at her with recognition in his eyes. She would rather be shot that afternoon than let herself have that hope, and she had not been able to kill it in five months.

She stood at the parapet with the wind coming off the water and the smell of cold iron, and behind her, at a distance of four metres, a young man in a good grey coat stopped, put his hands on the stone, and looked out at the river, and did not say a word for two minutes.

Then he said, in French, with the accent of a Pole who had learned it from a book, "It is a bad time to be out walking."

She looked at the water. Her heart had gone somewhere she could not follow.

"It is a bad time to be alive," she said. "Yet here we are. And the river does not care either way, monsieur, and neither do I."

There was a silence in which she could hear him breathing, four metres away, and in that silence she felt the whole thing — the apartment, the diamonds, the list, the tailor — hang by a thread over a drop she could not see.

"The book," he said at last. "You have not asked me about the book."

"Should I?"

"It is Polish," he said. "It is a book about the botany of the Kampinos Forest. There is nobody in Paris who would buy it and nobody who would read it. If anyone asks, I am an idiot who is homesick for a forest. It is important that when they take me, and they will take me, they take me for that."

Marion Herschlag turned from the river and looked at the young man for the first time, and saw what she had known from the street: the face of a boy of twenty-three who was already dead and did not know it, and who had come out to the river in the middle of the day to say something to somebody who might carry it home.

"Yes," she said, in Polish. She said it in Polish because she had to, because he had given her the only thing he had, and because she would rather have cut off her own hand. "Speak to the hat shop. Never to me. And go home the long way, through the market, and buy something, anything, and be seen buying it."

"Then you are—"

"I am nobody," she said, and she said it in Polish too, which was a mistake, and she knew it the moment it was out of her mouth, and it was too late. "I am nobody, and I have never been to Warsaw, and I was born in Strasbourg, and if you see me again you will not know me. Now go. And read the book. It is a better book than the men who sent you."

She walked away up the quai with her hands in her pockets and the wind in her eyes, and she did not look back, and behind her she heard nothing at all except the river, and the city, and somewhere a car changing gear.

She was four hundred metres from the apartment when she understood that she would have to go back into that bedroom tonight, and take her clothes off in front of the mirror, and be a woman who had been to see about a hat.

---

The theatre was at eight. The play was Molière, played to a house of grey uniforms and furs and a scattering of the sort of Parisians who could still, in the third winter, find the money for both. In the second interval the Colonel stood in the foyer with his back to a mirrored pillar, a glass of something pale in his hand, and watched the room watch his woman, which was, she had understood in December, a distinct form of pleasure to him — not vanity, exactly. He was fifty-four, with a limp from Smolensk and a chest full of iron, and possession was the only thing left that he could be said to enjoy without irony, and he had decided, sometime in November, that Marion Herschlag was the possession he was going to be buried in.

Two Hauptmänner came and said good evening. A Generalmajor's wife with a face like a good plain soup said something kind about the play and looked at Marion with an expression Marion had catalogued in October and could now recognise at forty metres: the look of a German woman of fifty who has decided that the beautiful French girl is not a whore but something worse, which is a *comfort*, and who will be pleasant for exactly as long as it takes to be certain.

The Colonel put his hand on the back of Marion's neck. He did it while he was talking, without pausing, which was the thing that made it belong to her and not to the conversation: a thumb, a pressure, the short hair at her nape, a claim registered in front of the room.

At the far side of the foyer, beside the doors, stood a small man in a good coat with his hands behind his back, and he was looking at her, and he was smiling.

René Tissier was forty-nine years old and edited a weekly sheet that had a circulation of forty thousand and a staff of four, and he was, in the ordinary run of things, a matter of no interest to her at all, except for two facts. The first was that in the spring, at a party at the Hôtel Majestic, he had put his hand on the small of her back and said, *I knew a little actress in Kraków who had your mouth*, purely to watch her face. The second was the end of November, in a corridor at the Militärbefehlshaber, when he had come up behind her with a glass in his hand and said, in Polish, quietly, and completely correctly, *You have grown up very well, Panna Stankiewiczówna* — and then walked away, and been sick, or pretended to be sick, into a courtyard, before she could find her voice.

She had spent a month proving to herself that he didn't know. She had tested it nine ways. He had never said it again. He had come to a dinner in December and been charming and left early. He was a man who collected things — paintings that were not his, women who were somebody else's, facts about people who could not afford to have facts about them, filed in his head — and he had the one property that made him dangerous to a person like her: he did not spend a thing until it was worth more to hold. He had held this since November. He was still holding it now, in the foyer of the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, with his hands behind his back and his smile going nowhere.

Halfway through the third act, Marion Herschlag leaned to the Colonel in the dark and breathed against his ear, "I want to go home."

He turned his head. In the dark his face was all bone. "Are you ill?"

"I am bored," she said. "I am bored, and I am cold, and I want to be taken to bed by a man who has been thinking about it all evening, and all evening you have been thinking about the Eastern Front."

That was the trick. There were only ever two tricks, and she had learned both of them in Kraków: give them the thing they already want with your hand rather than theirs, and name the thing they are hiding, in a voice that means *I have forgiven it, come here*.

He took her home. In the car, with Ehlers driving and the blackout lamps throwing a moving yellow smear along the grey fronts of the sixteenth arrondissement, the Colonel sat with his hand on her knee and said nothing at all, and she looked out at the shut shops, the swastika over the hotel door, the queue that had not moved since five in the morning, and felt, coming up through the floor of the car, the particular silence that meant he had decided something, and that she would be told when he was ready to be told.

At the door of the apartment he dismissed Kleist for the night. That was the first sign. The second was that he did not put on the light in the long sitting room, where a Picasso that the requisition board had not yet got around to ran the length of one wall, grey under its dust sheet, like a mountain in fog.

"In the hall," he said, "there are the ear-clips. On the table."

She went into the hall. It was a small paper box, from a jeweller on the rue de la Paix that had changed its name twice since 1940. Inside were two diamonds, small, good, in white gold, and old — she could see that they were old before she saw that they were beautiful, from the cut; they had come out of somewhere else, a drawer that had belonged to a woman who had been taken somewhere in 1942, and had been reset in a window on the rue de la Paix by a jeweller who no longer asked questions, and bought by a colonel of the Abwehr.

She stood in the dark hall with two other women's diamonds in a paper box in her hand, and heard the second thing he had to say, from the dark of the sitting room.

"I have written to Ilse about you."

She closed her hand around the box.

"About what?" she said.

"That there is someone." His voice came out of the dark, level, and beneath the level, for the first time in five months, something she had not heard before, which was uncertainty, in the middle of a man like a wall. "That she is not what I expected. That she is better than what I have deserved."

The diamonds were cold, and in the dark of the hall, with her coat still on, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood and worked out, with her trained and terrible mind, which one of them she was supposed to be for the next four minutes — the woman who said *thank you*, or the woman who said *don't* — and discovered that she could not choose, because both of them were true, and she had just, for the first time in five months and the third time in her life, put her hand out in the dark and touched a thing she could not have, and the name of the thing was: *he means it.*

She took the earrings out of the box. She did not put them on, and she did not go to him, and she did not answer. She stood in the dark hall of the requisitioned apartment at 12, avenue Henri-Martin, with the cold coming off the door and the diamonds in her palm, and above her head, three floors up, a family's chandelier hung in a room she had never entered, and in the bathroom down the corridor there was a comb with two black hairs in it, and outside the window, in the dark of the avenue, there was a boy in a good grey coat who had been playing with a page in a Polish book all afternoon, and she thought: *the two of them do not know about each other.*

And then she thought the thing she would remember, at the end, at the very end, in the dust and the noise and the walls coming down: *but they will.*
