# Chapter 3: The House of Stolen Things

The apartment had been the Vasseurs'.

She had learned that in November, from the concierge, who had done it the way the French did everything under the occupation, which was with a perfect surface and a bitter sediment underneath. The Vasseurs had been in the silk trade. The Vasseurs had kept a box at the Opéra and a house at Deauville and a daughter who rode; and in the second bedroom there was a mark on the wallpaper the size of a small rectangle where a picture had hung for eleven years, and in the wardrobe behind the curtain, hanging in a row like people waiting for a train, there were the eleven dresses of a woman who had gone away in 1942 to a place that was not Deauville.

The Colonel had taken the apartment in September: a requisition form, a signature, a set of keys. Three-quarters of the Vasseurs' things had gone to a depot in the rue de la Faisanderie; the rest was the furniture that was too heavy to move and too good to throw away, and it stayed where it was, in its rooms, being itself: a piano, two candlesticks, a screen of Coromandel lacquer, the green marble bathroom that had been the husband's, the small north room that had been the daughter's.

Marion Herschlag had moved into the house of stolen things in October with one suitcase and a hatbox and a story about Alsace, and she had learned, in the four months since, that the essential problem of living among stolen objects was not the guilt. It was the *attachment*. A chair is a chair; but if you sit in one chair for a hundred mornings and look at a screen of lacquer while you drink your coffee, then the chair and the screen become part of the architecture of your own survival, and one day you find that you love a thing that was taken from somebody, and you have become, without meaning to, a thief.

She solved it the way she solved everything: by inventory. Twice a week, in the mornings before Kleist came up, she walked the whole flat room by room and looked at every object as though she had just arrived: *this is not mine; I did not buy it; I have no right to it; I could leave it in the street tomorrow and my life would not change.* It took eleven minutes. It was the only housework she had ever done with any diligence.

The Vasseurs' daughter had been called Odette, and she had been at the door of nineteen, and on the back of the door of the small north room there was still a printed paper horse, faded, from a riding school in the Bois, with a date on it in July 1938.

That room was where the hatbox lived, on a table, with a red ribbon on it, and nothing in it.

---

On the twentieth of February she was invited to lunch with Frau Bruhn.

The wife of Oberstleutnant Bruhn was forty-six and came from Kehl, which was the detail that had made the invitation inevitable; and Kehl was, in the story of Marion Herschlag, the place where her mother's people had come from, which meant that Frau Bruhn had a claim on her, a piece of her, and would come to lunch to collect it.

The flat was the ground floor of a house in the avenue Émile-Deschanel, requisitioned from a family who were, they said, in the south, which was what everyone said in Paris in 1943. Frau Bruhn had done the room with German thrift and German taste: the Vichy-approved reproduction, the Christmas cactus, the photograph of a son at the front, and a copy of a Munich paper on the table, folded to a column.

"You have no accent at all," said Frau Bruhn, when the maid had brought the soup. "That is what they tell me in the section, and it is the strangest thing, because I have heard Alsatian women all my life and they have a way of turning the 'r' inward. But you — nothing."

"I was in Strasbourg very little. My education was in Paris."

"Then your mother must have been very grateful to your father."

"My mother died when I was twelve." That was true, and it was the only true sentence she would say for two hours, and she used it the way she used everything true: carefully, and once, and in the right place. "After that there was a school at Sèvres, and then France."

Frau Bruhn put down her spoon.

"Tell me," she said, "about the flood."

Six hundred kilometres away, in a room in a house on the avenue Kléber, there was a file with thirteen pages in it about Marion Herschlag of Strasbourg, and she had written the file herself, in outline, in September 1942, in order that she might be able to answer six questions exactly; and 'the flood' was the fourth. There had been a flood of the Rhine in the winter of 1919, a bad one; five hundred people had been out of their houses for a fortnight; and a small child of two in a stroller who had been carried by her uncle through a metre of water would have been told about it at every family dinner for the remainder of her girlhood.

She told Frau Bruhn about the flood. She did it in the voice people use about a thing they have heard a hundred times: with boredom in it, and a small piece of family detail, and a slight, comfortable impatience. She described the church with the water-mark at the height of the door lintel. She described her uncle's boots.

Frau Bruhn listened, and something relaxed in her face.

"Of course," she said. "The boots. There was one man in our street who never got them off." She laughed. "My dear, forgive me. When one is out of Germany, one becomes provincial. Every woman at our table is from somewhere, and every one of them is from somewhere else by the following week, and we all exercise each other like a piano with a tuning fork."

"Then we shall be friends on the strength of the boots."

"We shall be friends," said Frau Bruhn, "on the strength of your not being a Jew, which is more than half this street can say; and if you will take my advice, you will find out what the Colonel's wife was like, because he will bring it into the room at least once a month for the rest of the war, and it is better to be prepared."

"There is no wife. There is a dead woman. There is a difference."

"There is no difference at all, my child, and you know it, and that is why you have to plan for it."

It was the most useful hour she spent that winter, and the most dangerous, and she came out of the house in the avenue Émile-Deschanel with her hands steady, and walked to the Gare du Nord for no reason at all except that it was the wrong direction, and stood for ten minutes on the platform watching the trains to Germany and the trains from Germany, and permitted herself, without moving her face, one minute of the pure animal hatred that she kept in the same box as the fear, in the same drawer, under the same lock.

---

The film came back to her on the twenty-fourth, in the form of a book.

That was Odile's method, and it was beautiful: a parcel of books for Madame Herschlag, ordered from a shop in the rue de Rivoli, four of them, from the shop's own stock, so that the new books had the shop's paper and the shop's string. Inside the binding of the third book — a novel she had asked for and did not intend to read — there was a thin strip of paper, and on the strip, in Lis's hand, there were three words.

*CINEMA SAINT-SÉVERIN, TUESDAY.*

She went on Tuesday, at four, to a small cinema in the fifth with a cracked ceiling and forty people in it and a Charlot picture that everybody had seen twice. The seats behind the pillars were empty; in the third row of that block a bald man with a bad heart sat with his hat on his knees and watched, for a few minutes, the screen.

"It is not the film I came for," he said.

"It is never the film you come for."

"You are well?"

"I am well." Down on the screen, the little man was being chased by a policeman through a set of doors, and forty Parisians laughed, and the sound went up into the ceiling and came back like rain. "The film went out on the twelfth. It was confirmed on the twenty-first. Nine names, one page of a carbon, date stamp twenty-two twelve forty-two, and a pencil mark, three-vee-bee."

"Three bee." Lis was quiet for a moment. "That is a registry. The Abwehrstelle keeps a registry of names it has collected but not yet acted on, and the figures are the section that opened the file. Three-B is.. the section that deals with the east."

"You are telling me that a list of French names, in the south of France, opened by the section that deals with the east, came out of a colonel's private briefcase in Paris in December."

"I am telling you," said Lis, "that the war is being rewritten, my little rabbit. Look at the last three months. Stalingrad. Africa. They have stopped planning to win. They are planning to *hold*, and the plans are not in Berlin, they are in the field offices, and a field office with a plan is like a widow with a bank account: everyone comes to visit." He coughed, quietly, into his hat. "There is a courier leaving on the fourth of March for Warsaw. That is the last of the spring, because after March the frontier people are being reinforced; and after that, nothing goes out of Paris by road, everything goes by the diplomatic bag, and the diplomatic bag does not stop for me."

"Then whatever I take before the fourth of March is the last thing that goes."

"Good girl."

"And what am I taking?"

Lis watched the screen for a while.

"The safe," he said. "In the study, in your apartment, behind the photograph."

"Then I am going to need the combination and I am not going to be able to ask him for it."

"That," said Lis, "is why I am telling you in February and not on the third of March."

She looked at the light on the ceiling, thrown from the screen, moving, like water in a well.

"I need to tell you something," she said, "and then I need you to be quiet for a moment and not ask me any questions."

"Very well."

"There is a captain coming into his office. A new adjutant. Half Polish, from Nancy, name of Brandt." She kept her voice flat, and she kept her eyes on the screen. "I knew him before the war. In Warsaw. He is the brother of a girl I was at school with. He is nobody to me, and he is going to be a catastrophe, because he is the only man in Paris who can put my face to a Polish name."

The little man beside her did not move for a long time.

"Has he?" he said.

"Has he what?"

"Seen you."

"Twice. The second time was across a corridor at the Majestic, at nine metres, in a dinner suit."

"And?"

"And he gave me the look an officer gives his colonel's woman." She breathed out. "But there was a half-second in it, and I do not know what the half-second means, and I have eleven days of not knowing."

"I see." Lis put his hat on his head. "Then we must proceed on the assumption that he has not, because if he has, we are not in a film; we are in the rue des Saussaies, and there is no version of that in which I am able to help you. But Krystyna—"

"Don't."

"I will say it once." He stood up, hunched, a bald man with a bad heart and no coat worth the name, who had once sold books in a shop on the Nowy Świat and had been, in his own way, the best teacher she had ever had. "If he knows, then you must go to him before he comes to you. That is all. Whatever he is, he is a man, and men are rivers. You get there first, or the water does."

"It is impossible."

"Everything is impossible. That is what we do." He put five francs on the seat for the usher and looked down at her, and in the dark of the little cinema there was the ghost, for a second, of the man who had stood in her father's library in 1938 and argued about Chamberlain. "You have a face on you, my rabbit, and it is a good face. But I have known you since you were a girl with a ribbon, and I do not need to see it to know when you are frightened, because you have come to a cinema in the fifth arrondissement in the middle of the afternoon to tell me about a captain who speaks Polish. If you could not help yourself, you would not have told me at all."

He went out up the aisle, into the light of the doorway, and did not look back; and she sat through the last four minutes of the picture with her hands folded, and then she went home and was Marion Herschlag at the door of the apartment on the avenue Henri-Martin, and smiled for Kleist, and asked him about his son in Smolensk, because he liked to be asked.

---

On the first of March, in the bookshop in the rue de Rivoli, René Tissier bought her a book.

She had gone in for the parcel of the month; it was a part of the pattern, a woman of her position had subscriptions and accounts and a shop she was seen in. And she was at the counter with her gloves in her hand when a voice behind her said, "Mademoiselle will find the new Simenon disappointing," and she turned, and there he was: a small man in a good coat, with the eyes of somebody who has counted the change on the counter and decided it was short.

"Monsieur Tissier."

"Madame Herschlag." He took off his hat and held it against his chest like a man at a funeral. "We have not met since November, and in November you were unwell, and I have been carrying the burden of having caused it ever since."

"You had caused it."

"I had." He was smiling. He had a smile he had spent money on once, in the twenties, at a dentist, and he used it like a tool. "May I buy you a coffee four doors down, and apologise in the correct manner, which is with your permission and at your convenience, and in a public place, so that you are never obliged to be charming to me for longer than it takes to drink a cup of the horse they call coffee in this city."

There were a hundred reasons to say no. She said yes, because the first rule of her profession — the one Lis had taught her and the one Kraków had not — was that you always look at the thing that knows your name.

The café was warm and full and had one waiter, who was eighty. Tissier ordered two coffees, and then he talked, for four minutes, about a play, in the way that men of his kind talked: digressions, jokes, a piece of café gossip, one line of Racine. He was good at it. That was the thing that had made his sheet worth reading; he had a real ear, and he wasted it.

Then he put his cup down and said, "You are Alsatian."

"So everyone says."

"I did not say it as an accusation. I have a German accent in my filing system, and I do not know where you get yours, and the reason I do not know is that—" he made a small gesture with two fingers, "—there is nothing wrong with it. That is what makes it beautiful. Nobody in this city has an accent that is *nothing*. Even the Parisians have an accent. Only two kinds of people have no accent at all: people who spent their childhood being moved from school to school, and people who have been taught."

"Or, in the third case, people whose ear is better than your filing system."

"That is possible," he said, delighted. "That is entirely possible." He looked at her over the top of the cup. "I knew a little actress in Kraków. In the spring of 1939. She was nineteen. She had a mouth like yours — no, not the shape; the mouth of a woman who is going to talk before she has decided whether to. And I have been thinking, since November, about the way in which you reacted when I told you so. You went grey. You went grey, madame, in a corridor of the Militärbefehlshaber, in front of a hundred people, and then you looked at me with your mother's eyes and walked away, and I have been thinking about it ever since, and do you know what I am thinking now?"

"You are thinking that you are making a mistake."

"No. I am thinking that you have not asked me not to say it again." He leaned back. "A woman who is what she says she is would have had me thrown out of the house for saying a thing like that. She would have complained to the Colonel. She would have asked what the words meant, in an innocent voice, so that I could hear myself explaining them. You did none of those things. You have simply — been careful. And so I have decided to be a friend, madame, and to say this to you today, in a warm café, in front of a waiter, at four in the afternoon: I am nobody's enemy. I do not inform. I have never informed. I am a man who is trying to be in the room when the war ends, with a well-known newspaper and a clean pair of hands, and I have discovered that the way to be in that room is to know which of the beautiful women of Paris are what they say they are." He picked up his hat. "And what I know is this. You are not Marion Herschlag of Strasbourg. What I do *not* know is what that makes you. And I am happy to leave it there, indefinitely, because a thing you do not know is worth more than a thing you have been told."

He put money on the table for the coffee, and stood.

"One day," he said, "I shall come to you with a favour, and it will be small, and you will do it, because you will remember this conversation: that I could have said it to a German officer, and did not."

He went out into the cold with his hat on. The waiter took the money. Marion Herschlag sat at the table for six minutes, and drank the coffee down to the bottom of the cup, which no Frenchwoman would ever do, because she was no longer thinking about how she appeared, and because, in the small cold part of her mind where the arithmetic was done, she had just understood something about René Tissier that she would need later: he had told her exactly what he was, and men who do that are either honest or frightened, and he was not frightened.

---

On the eighth of March the Colonel took her to the rue de la Faisanderie.

He had been in a good mood for three days. There had been a message from Berlin about his section; there had been a visit from a Generalmajor who had told him he was being watched with approval; and in the mornings he had taken to shaving with the wireless on, which made Kleist nervous and delighted, because there is nothing so frightening to a servant as a happy German.

In the car he said, "I want your opinion on something."

"On what?"

"On furniture."

The depot in the rue de la Faisanderie had been a garage before the war and it was a warehouse now, and it belonged, according to the brass plate at the door, to the *Einsatzstab* something or other; Marion never learned the full name and never asked it. Inside, it was three floors and a courtyard of things.

Rugs rolled and standing on their ends like columns. Chairs in pyramids. Two hundred clocks. A wall of paintings, hung three deep, from which the mad, patient eyes of the nineteenth century looked out at nothing. Crates of silver in straw. Six chandeliers lying on their sides in a velvet bed, like enormous animals asleep. Four pianos. A glass case of watches. A desk that had a blotter on it still, with the impressions of letters in it, and a pen in a stand, and a pair of spectacles folded on the corner, and on the wall above it, forgotten, a photograph of a family on a boat, still in its frame, because the frame was not valuable.

The foreman was a German corporal of forty with a pencil behind his ear, and he took the Colonel round the way a head gardener takes a cardinal round an orchard.

"From the rue Copernic, Herr Oberst. From the avenue de Wagram on Wednesday. This is the lot from the rue de Bassano, which has been picked over, but the marquetry is good, and there is a buyer in Cologne for the clock."

The Colonel walked through it as though he were in a museum in a foreign country, with his hands behind his back.

"This is the greatest waste in Europe," he said.

"Sir?"

"Not this." He turned to her. "This. The collection. I have read the reports; there are eleven warehouses like this in Paris and the total is beyond calculation, and it will sit here for another year and then be loaded onto a train, and the train will be *requisitioned*, and by the time it reaches the Reich, half of it will be stolen, and the rest will be given to men who have no idea what they have been given; I personally know an Obersturmbannführer in Kassel who has a Rubens in his hall he thinks is a copy." He looked at a chandelier. "The Führer has a plan to build a museum in Linz, and that plan is the only sane sentence in the last ten years, and it will never happen, because we are losing, and when we lose, this will all go back to the people it came from, and the people it came from will be dead. So the things will be in warehouses in Warsaw and Prague and Amsterdam for fifty years; and then some boy of twenty will open a crate in 1998 and find a clock with a note inside it, and there will be no one left alive who knows whose clock it was."

"Colonel," said the corporal, in the voice of a man who had been standing in the cold for eleven minutes and had heard enough philosophy for one afternoon, "the mattresses."

"Ah, yes." The Colonel turned to Marion and took her elbow, and he was, for a moment, almost gay. "The mattresses. My adjutant has reported to me, three times in nine days, that the mattresses in the officers' quarters are filled with wood shavings; and last night I found out that the reason is that the supply in the western sector was bought in February by a captain who resold it in the black market at a profit of four hundred per cent, and I intend to hang the captain, but I do not intend to sleep on filings, and there was a delivery here on Monday from a shop on the rue Saint-Honoré." He gestured with his stick at a cart. "Find me a mattress, madame, and be quick about it, and this will be your contribution to the war."

She went with the corporal to the cart, which was loaded with the household goods of eleven flats, and she looked, with a face of amused competence, at mattresses, and she found two that were good, and while the corporal made a note she went on looking, because a woman looking at mattresses is a woman who is not looking at anything else.

The cart stood by the wall of the courtyard. There were two bedside lamps, a tea service, a child's rocking horse with a broken ear, a box of linens tied with string, and, at the bottom, in a third crate, half buried, standing on their ends like men in a trench, forty or fifty books.

The silver had all gone from the crates. The china had gone. Nobody had taken the books, because books are heavy and worth nothing and there are five million of them in any city, and they had survived the sorting of three flats, and lay in a heap in a straw crate in the rue de la Faisanderie with the rain coming down beyond the arch of the yard.

She bent down to look, the way a woman bends down to look at books in a crate, and she did not have to search for long, because it was on the top of the second heap, and because she would have known it in the dark: a slim octavo with a brown spine, and on the cover the faded gold stamping of a Polish title, and on the title page, when she turned it with two fingers, a bookplate: an oak and a river in black ink, and beneath them, in her father's hand, *Tadeusz Stanisław Stankiewicz*, and a shelf number, and a date in 1931.

*Podstawy mechaniki* — *The Foundations of Mechanics.* Third edition. There was a fold in page ninety, where he had stopped; and a pencil line in the margin in his handwriting, the small impatient line he drew when a student had failed to understand something; and on the flyleaf a small brown ring that she knew with her whole body was the mark of a coffee cup set down on a book in 1936, at the table in the Żoliborz apartment, on a Sunday, in the argument about the bridge at Ivangorod.

The corporal said something about the mattresses. The rain went on coming down in the yard. Marion Herschlag straightened up with the book in her hand, and looked at the door, and at the wall, and at the deep grey light over the roofs of the sixteenth arrondissement, and put it into her bag, and it was the first thing she had stolen in her life that was hers.

There was a packet in the desk.

It had been there since the third week of November, in the second drawer of the writing table in the small room that had been the daughter's, and she had found it on the fourth day of her inventory: nineteen letters tied with a bootlace, a certificate of the first communion of a girl with a name that was not on the door, and a photograph of two women in the garden of a house at Biarritz in the summer of 1925. She had put the packet back in the drawer under the blotter and had not written it on the list, and she had not given it to the Colonel, and she had not given it to Odile's channel, and the packet had stayed in the drawer of the writing table of the room of the daughter of the Vasseurs for four months while the woman of the house inventoried everything else in the apartment twice a week.

She was never able to explain that to herself afterwards, and the only explanation she ever found was the true one, and the true one was not a principle: it was that the drawer felt like a room she was standing in front of with her hat in her hands.

The bell rang at four in the afternoon of the ninth of March.

Kleist was out — he was at the rue de la Faine with the coal, and the woman who came in to do the floors had gone at three, and Marion Herschlag was alone in the apartment of the Vasseurs on a Tuesday in the spring of 1943 in a grey dress with the door of the small room open behind her; and she went to the door herself, which she was not supposed to do, and opened it on the chain, and there was a girl on the landing.

Twenty years old. A grey coat with the collar worn through at the fold; a hat she had made over herself, badly; a pair of gloves with the fingertips mended in a different thread; and the face of a girl who had been at a good school and could not now afford the shoes to go with it. She was holding a cardboard box in front of her with both hands, the way a person holds a box that has been carried across a city.

"Madame Herschlag."

"Yes."

"You are the German's."

The chain held the door eleven centimetres open. Down in the courtyard, the concierge was beating a rug over the rail, and the noise came up the well of the stair with the cold.

"I am the woman of the house," said Marion Herschlag.

"You are the woman of the house," the girl said. "It was my mother's house. There is a small room at the end of the corridor with the window on the courtyard, and the paper in it is green with the small flowers of the year 1902, and there is a mark on the wall beside the door where the handle of a trunk has knocked the plaster for twenty years, and there is a writing table in that room, and in the second drawer of the writing table, under the blotter, there is a packet of nineteen letters tied with a bootlace, and it belongs to me, and I have come to ask for it."

She said it in the flat voice of a person who had rehearsed it in the street.

"What is your name?"

"Odette Vasseur."

"Where have you come from?"

"From the rue Lamarck. I am with my aunt. I walked." She shifted the box from one arm to the other. "I am not going to ask to come in. I am not going to ask for the silver, and I know where the silver is, and I know what it has become, because a woman from this building told my aunt in January. I am not going to ask for the piano. I am only going to ask for the packet, and then I am going to walk back to the rue Lamarck, and I am not going to be at this door again."

There are four seconds in a life of this kind, and afterwards a woman of that profession either remembers them for fifty years or does not permit herself to remember them at all.

Marion Herschlag took the chain off.

"Stay on the landing," she said.

She went down the corridor of the stolen apartment, past the drawing room where the marks on the wall still showed the shapes of frames that had been taken down before the requisition, into the small room with the green paper, and she opened the second drawer of the writing table and took out the packet of nineteen letters tied with a bootlace, and stood with it in her hand for one second, in the light of the window, and then she went back down the corridor and out onto the landing and put the packet into the hands of the girl who had made over her own hat.

"Do not come here again," she said.

"I know that."

"Do not talk about today in the rue Lamarck, and do not talk about it to the aunt, and do not talk about it in the street where you live, because there are eleven people in this building who watch the comings and goings of the door of the woman of the German, and because the concierge has a cousin in the police, and because if I am asked about the ninth of March by a man of the office of the German administration I am going to say that I was alone in this apartment for the afternoon and that I let nobody in, which will be the truth as it will be written down, and the two sentences are going to be in the file of the one building and there will be nobody to put them side by side."

The girl looked at her.

"You are not French," she said.

"I am a woman in a house," said Marion Herschlag. "Go down the back stair. The door of the courtyard is at the end, and the concierge is at the front, and the hammering of the rug will cover the descent."

Odette Vasseur went down the back stair with the packet of nineteen letters under her coat, and Marion Herschlag closed the door of the apartment and stood in the hall of the house of the Vasseurs in the middle of the afternoon and did the arithmetic that she was going to have to do for the rest of the war, and the arithmetic was this:

*In the list of the objects in this apartment there is now one item that is not in the apartment, and the list is in my head and in the head of nobody else in the world; and the difference between a woman who steals the letters of a dead woman's daughter for a district of the organisation in a city in the east and a woman who gives them back on a landing is a difference of one item, and there is not one officer of the intelligence service in this whole city of Paris who is able to see it, and it is the only piece of arithmetic in my life in which I have ever chosen the wrong number on purpose.*

She went into the kitchen and made the coffee that Kleist would drink at half past five, and when he came in from the rue de la Faine with the coal he said that a young woman had been at the door of the building with a box and that the concierge had told her, in his hearing, that the lady of the fourth floor did not receive callers.

"What did she want?"

"The concierge says she is the daughter of the doctor," said Kleist, in the ordinary voice of an orderly of the district, "and she wanted to know whether the furniture of the fourth floor was for sale, and the concierge told her that the furniture of the fourth floor belongs to the Wehrmacht of the German Reich and that there is no furniture in Paris for sale."

"That was very correct of the concierge."

"It is a very correct building," said Kleist.

That night the Colonel took her back to the apartment and, because the mattress was good and because he was a man who liked to consecrate an acquisition, he had her in the bed of the Vasseurs with her wrists held in one of his hands above her head, in the slow cruel way that he had, and afterwards, when he was asleep, she got up and went into the small north room and sat on the floor in the dark, and put the book down in front of her on the boards, and opened it, in the dark, to the page where a man who had been Professor of Mechanics at the Polytechnic of Warsaw had stopped reading in 1939, and she put her two hands flat on the paper and sat like that until the window went grey, without crying, for her father's sake.

In the morning she took the book apart with a knife from the kitchen, page by page, and burned the spine and the boards in the fireplace when Kleist was out, and kept the thirty pages with the pencil in them, folded into a small square; and went down to breakfast as a woman of the sixteenth arrondissement, who was the crowned jewel of occupied Paris, and who had just spent a night in the house of stolen things and come out of it carrying exactly one page too many.
