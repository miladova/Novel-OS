# Chapter 13: Amber and Ash

In September the war turned, and everyone in Warsaw knew it within four days, and nobody in Warsaw was permitted to say a word about it.

The Italian armistice came on the wireless on the ninth, in German, out of the loudspeakers that the administration had put up at the corners of the Marszałkowska and the Nowy Świat; and the Poles stood under the loudspeakers in the ordinary way and listened to a German announcer explain, in an even voice, that the German Reich had foreseen it and had taken the necessary measures; and a man of fifty in a cap, standing beside Marion Herschlag, said to nobody at all, in Polish, at a normal volume, "Ah. The necessary measures," and eleven people in the crowd laughed, and the German soldier on the corner did not turn his head, because the city had learned that a Pole who laughed at the loudspeaker in the street and a German who heard him were two men in a legal relationship that had been settled in 1940 and was not worth reopening.

She bought a newspaper. She read it in the tram, standing, in the ordinary crowd, and her education in the reading of German newspapers had been the gift of a small man with a bad heart who had once sold books on the Nowy Świat, and the education was this: *read a German paper for the verbs, not the nouns; the nouns are wishes, the verbs are facts*. And the verbs in the German press of September 1943 said: *shortened*; *straightened*; *consolidated*; *will be held*; and nowhere in the sheet, in an entire page of the eastern front, was the simple transitive verb that the German Reich had been using since the twenty-second of June 1941, which was: *took*.

She got off the tram at the corner of the Nowy Świat and went into the German store and bought coffee, and on the way home she did the arithmetic that she had spent the summer putting off: *the war is going to end, and it is going to end in this city, and when it ends, the men in the blue palace are going to become an army in a trap; and a man in a trap is a man who can be killed with his own household.*

That afternoon, at four o'clock, in the courtyard of the Blue Palace, she saw a man with a green notebook.

He was standing by the door of the SD building, which was on the other side of the courtyard, with his back to her, and he was doing what he had done in Paris: a small man with the shoulders of a swimmer, standing with his hands behind his back and his attention on the yard; and she knew him by the way he stood before she had seen the smallest fraction of his face, and she went on walking towards the car with her training holding her body steady, and Ehlers opened the door, and she got in, and the car went out of the gate.

"Herr Kroll," said Ehlers, from the front, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather.

"You know him?"

"The the city knows him now. He came in August; he is the deputy chief of the SD office; he is the man who has the two hundred thousand names of the Poles of this district in a card index, and he is the man they send when a German officer in the east is killed and they want to make an example of a hundred Poles in one afternoon." Ehlers drove. "My Marta's uncle was a tram conductor in the Praga district and he was taken in March and we do not know where. Kroll is a man with a card index and a good pair of shoes, and everybody in this administration would rather have a hundred men like Freytag, because a man like Freytag is a butcher and he is finished by dinner, but a man with a card index is a man who is still there in the spring."

Marion Herschlag sat in the back of the car and looked out at the city, and the sun was going down over the roofs of the north, over the wall, over a district of streets in which a hundred and fifty thousand people were living in a space the size of a small town, and she thought about a room on the second floor of the Hôtel Majestic in Paris, in April, with a table and two chairs and a window with a blackout on it, and a man who had stood up when she came in, and had said, in eleven minutes, the single most frightening sentence that anyone had ever said to her: *there is no gap in your twenty minutes; that is why I am here: not because you are suspected, but because you are available, which is a different matter, and always will be, until I have found the actual hole in the building.*

*You did not find the hole, Hauptsturmführer,* she thought, as the car went along the Kopernika. *You found the building. And now you have come to the second building, and there is a card index in it the size of a piano, and in that index there is one card with a woman's false name on it, and in the six months to come you are going to look for a hole in the German administration of Warsaw, and what you will find, in the end, is the wife of a colonel who has been in two cities and who has never once been a person.*

---

The Colonel gave her the combination on the twentieth of September, in the dark, in bed, in a manner so entirely characteristic of him that she thought, afterwards, that if she had been asked to invent a scene to explain Friedrich von Reuth to a court, she would have written down exactly that.

He had come home from the Blue Palace at half past eleven with the Italian business on him, which is to say: he had spent the day in a room with a General who had been told from Berlin that nothing had changed, and he had spent the day knowing that everything had changed, and he had come home and eaten his dinner and drunk four glasses of the Hungarian wine and had then taken her to bed and done what he did, with the weight of a district, in the way of a man who is trying to prove to himself the only thing about the world that he still believes.

Afterwards he did not put his arm over his eyes. He lay on his back in the dark and looked at the ceiling, which had the shadow of a curtain on it, the same bandage, the same ceiling, one wall away from the ceiling of the doctor who had built the house in 1911, and he said:

"Anneliese was born on the ninth of April."

She lay beside him in the dark. She said nothing at all, which is the correct answer to every sentence of that kind.

"Nine. Four. Thirty-six." He said it slowly, one figure at a time, into the dark, in the voice of a man reading a number off a stone. "That is the combination of the safe in my study. It has been the combination of every safe I have ever had, in Berlin, in Riga, in Paris, and here. When I got the new one in April, I told the Organisation Todt man to set it to that, and he did it, because I am a colonel and he was a sergeant and the war is nearly over and nobody is going to be court-martialled for a safe."

She lay perfectly still.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because in the last month I have been in two rooms in which men I have known for twenty years explained to a General that everything was in order, and I have seen the General's face, and on the twenty-second of August a man I was at cadet school with, a colonel of the infantry, was killed on the road to Kharkov by a mine, and his estate consisted of a trunk and a dog; and because of that, and because of a piece of paper I received on Thursday from the office of the district court concerning the probate of my wife's estate, which has now taken four years and eleven months to be settled, I have decided to put my life in one place where the Organisation Todt cannot lose it, and there is only one place in this house where a man's life will not be opened by a Pole with a screwdriver in the last week of the war, and it is that safe." He turned his head. In the dark she could see nothing, and she could feel the entire enormous fact of him beside her: a colonel of fifty-four, in a house he had been given, in a city he had been given, in a war he had been given the previous year to lose. "You will remember that number."

"Yes."

"A woman may forget what a man tells her in bed. It is permitted; it is one of the graces of the sex; and I do not mind about the rest of it, because the rest of it is nothing." He put his hand on her stomach, flat, with the whole weight of it. "But you will remember that number, because there is going to be an afternoon, my dear, possibly in the spring, possibly later, when I am not going to be able to come home, and there is going to be a woman in this house who will be visited by men in uniforms who are not mine, and on that afternoon the only thing in my existence that will not be in the hands of those men is going to be the contents of a steel box in the wall of a study behind the photograph of my wife's garden. And I want you to take it. Whatever is in it — money, and the deeds of the Pomeranian land, and a portfolio of my wife's paper, and two letters from Ilse — I want you to take it, and walk out of the front door of this house with it in your hands, and leave the rest, and not look back at the house."

"Friedrich."

"Say that you will."

"I will."

He fell asleep, holding her, with the weight of his hand; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna lay beneath a sleeping colonel of the Abwehr in the fourth year of the war and looked at the ceiling with a bandage of shadow on it, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was this: *nine, four, thirty-six. The ninth of April, thirty-six. The day before the tenth. Six days after my own birthday in nineteen seventeen. In my head, on the shelf, in the long room at the back, next to the manufacturer's plate on the Organisation Todt safe and the number of the combination written in a procurement clerk's table in a document concerning office materials, and now, at the front of the shelf, where my hands can reach it at any hour of any night in the coming year.* And she lay awake with her heart going slowly and perfectly, and, on the other side of the wall, in the room of the Ordonnanzoffizier's papers, in the drawer of a desk in a house on the Mokotowska, there was a file with a boy's name in it that had been saved, and a woman in a garden at four o'clock in the afternoon in September in the second year of the occupation had been given the power of life and death over the man who was asleep with his hand on her stomach.

*It is arithmetic,* she thought. *And it is the worst thing that has ever happened to me, and I am going to use it, and there is not a single hour between this one and the last one of the war in which I shall be able to put my hand on this bed and feel anything at all.*

---

The man with the book came in the last week of September, at the church of the Visitation, at four in the afternoon, as she had been told in May, by a girl of seventeen with a bucket of paper flowers.

He was a priest.

That was the first surprise, and it was a good one, and she understood, standing in the aisle of a church in the fourth year of the occupation of the city of Warsaw, why the network had been built the way it had: a priest is the only man in Poland who can be in a church at any hour, and can speak to any woman in the city, in the daylight, and be seen by no one at all.

He was about forty-five. He had the face of a man who had been in a seminary and then in the army and then in a parish, and the hands of a man who had worked; and he heard her confession in a manner that was, in its whole structure, exactly what it appeared to be, because she had been told to make a real confession of the small things and to keep the large ones; and when the small things were done, and the church was empty, and the smell of the candles and the damp of the stone and the Warsaw autumn came in through the great door, he said, in Polish, quietly, with his hands on the grille:

"I am told that you have never asked for anything."

"I was told to ask for nothing."

"By a man in the west who is dead?"

"By a man in Paris who had a bad heart."

"Then the arrangement is now in the hands of the men of this city, and the men of this city are extremely hungry, and I am told that you have, in your possession, in your head, a certain amount of a certain paper, and that the paper is three months old and worth less every day, because paper in this war is like bread: it is the same loaf, but the city is eating it and nobody wants to pay for a week-old piece." He paused. "Tell me it, and do not explain it."

She told him.

She did it for eleven minutes, in a whisper, in the smell of the candles, with the discipline of three years: the III b registry in Paris, forty pages, a grid per city, three hands, three inks, and the fact that it was a map of the Polish underground in France drawn by somebody inside it; the Fall *Wotan* page with eleven towns and eleven dates and the last date in the right-hand column, the eighth of August 1944, and the pencil sentence underneath it, *die Linie ist bis zum Datum ununterbrochen zu halten*; the personnel file of a captain of the Abwehr born in Warsaw in 1915, with seven weeks written over twice in the autumn of 1939; the number of a manufacturer's plate on a safe in the study of a colonel of the district intelligence section of Warsaw; and four names of the district's section chiefs, and their habits, and the fact that the General telephoned Berlin twice a week from his bedroom and never once from his office.

When she had finished, there was a silence in the confessional that went on for a long time.

"A map of us," the priest said at last, "drawn by one of us. That is the first thing, and it is the thing for which Paris is going to burn. And the second thing is a schedule with a date in it, and the date is in the future, which in this war is the rarest thing in the world: everybody's paper is about the past, and here is a page about August of next year, and it says that a line is to be held until a fixed day, which means that somebody in the east has already decided what is going to happen in the east, and the Polish underground is going to be standing underneath it when it does." She heard him breathe. "And the third thing, my child, is a man who has been sitting in a German office in this city for a year and a half with seven weeks written over twice in his file, and who has the district's paper on his table, and who has an aunt's nephew's face and a Polish mouth, and you have told me about him in the same breath as the number of a safe, and I am an old priest in a parish in the centre of Warsaw and I am going to tell you the thing that the men of the *Kedyw* would tell you in a cellar with a bottle: that the third item is the only one of the three that is standing next to you in the room."

"He is not an item."

"Everything in this city is an item, and you know it, and I know it, and the two of us are going to pretend, for the rest of the duration, that we do not." He was quiet for a moment. "What do you want?"

"I want the paper to go out."

"It is already out. It has been out since the spring; it went to London in June in a diplomatic bag, and the men in London have known the III b registry since the fourth of July, and it is being worked on by three men in a house in a suburb, and that is nothing to do with this city."

"Then I want something to be done with the rest."

"The rest is a date in a schedule and a safe in the study of a colonel." The priest's voice, through the grille, was entirely without warmth and entirely without cruelty, which is the exact manner of a man who has been in the world for forty-five years and in the church for twenty. "What do you want to be *for*?"

She knelt in the dark of a confessional in Warsaw in September 1943, on a wooden board that had been worn by four hundred years of the knees of the women of that parish, and looked at her own life the way a woman looks at a ledger; and she said the thing that she had come to say, and it was not the thing she had planned to say in the tram.

"I want to be the woman who walks out of that house in the last week of the war with the paper of the district of Warsaw in her arms," she said. "And I want it to be known, by the men in the cellars, that in the year and a half I have been in the house of a German colonel, I never once asked for a thing for myself. And I want, when it is over, in whatever is left of this city, to be able to say my own name out loud in the street."

The priest was silent for a long moment.

"That is a very large request," he said. "And the last part of it is the largest."

"I know."

"Then here is the arrangement, and it is the only arrangement I am able to offer, and you may take it or leave it." She heard him shift on the board; and, beyond the church, the city: a cart, a tram, a bell. "You will do nothing until the spring. In the spring, a man will come to you, and he will not be a priest and he will not be a girl with flowers, and he will say to you a sentence about the Vistula, and then it will be his business and not mine. Until then you will be the woman of the house of the Oberst von Reuth, and you will go to the wives at the Blue Palace, and you will drink the tea, and you will tell no one in this city anything at all, not even me, not even a man in a garden, and above all: you will not open the safe."

"Why?"

"Because there is a card index in this city kept by a man with a green notebook," said the priest, "and the index is a list of the holes in the buildings; and the last hole in Warsaw, the one that no one has found, is a Frenchwoman from Strasbourg in a doctor's house on the Mokotowska, and she is worth more than the paper in the safe, and if you open the safe in October, you will be caught in November, and in December the underground of this city will be looking for a new woman to live in the house of the German intelligence chief, and there is not one, my child. There is not one, and there never will be again."

---

Her father died on the eighth of November, at ten minutes past four in the morning, in the flat on the Krasińskiego, with Zofia in the room and the city blacked out and a German patrol going along the street outside, and she was not there.

She was told at seven in the morning by Pelagia.

Pelagia was the family's old maid, and she was seventy-eight, and she had been half-blind since 1937, and she had taken the amber out of the flat on the second of December 1939 and had kept it for four years wrapped in a man's handkerchief in the lining of a winter coat in a room in the Wola district, in a building in which the population had been emptied twice, and in which an old woman of seventy-eight with a bad heart and a family photograph and no papers had somehow, in the four years, been left standing like a post.

She came to the house on the Mokotowska at seven in the morning, which was a thing no Pole in Warsaw ever did, and she came to the front door and not the back, which was a thing no Pole in Warsaw had done since 1939, and she stood in the doorway of the German house in the fourth year of the occupation, in a coat that had been good in 1935, with her white stick, and said to Anna Zaleska, in Polish, at the top of her voice:

"Tell the woman of this house that the Professor is dead and that she has to come for the amber."

And in the garden and the yard and the street, in the middle of a German morning in a German district, with the flag on the Blue Palace four hundred metres away and a Hauptsturmführer of the SD with a card index in a room in the Szucha, the truth of the house of the Oberst von Reuth was said out loud, in Polish, at the top of an old woman's voice, by a half-blind maid of seventy-eight who had no idea in the world what she was doing; and Anna Zaleska stood in the doorway of the German house for one second, and then did the thing that saved the war for the woman inside it, which was to take the old woman by the arm and say, loudly, in the doorway, in Polish, in the voice of the Polish servants of the German administration of Warsaw:

"Old woman, this is the house of the German Oberst. The woman you want is the caretaker's woman in the Praga district, and you have walked four kilometres in the wrong direction, and the street you want is on the other side of the river, and if you wait here in the doorway one more minute, the SS will come and take us both."

Then she shut the door, and went into the kitchen, and stood with her back to the stove for four minutes with her hands over her face.

Marion Herschlag heard it from the top of the stairs. She was standing barefoot on the landing of the first floor of a doctor's house on the Mokotowska at seven in the morning in November 1943, in a dressing gown, having listened to her own life being shouted at a German front door by a woman of seventy-eight with a white stick; and she stood there on the cold boards and did the arithmetic for the space of eleven breaths, because there was no room in the world any more for anything except arithmetic.

*Eight and a half hours ago, my father died.*

*There is a woman of seventy-eight on a pavement in the Śródmieście with the last object of my mother's life wrapped in a handkerchief in the lining of her coat, and she has come to a German house at seven in the morning and shouted, and she will do it again; and somewhere in this city there is a Hauptsturmführer with a card index, and a card with my false name on it, and the German administration of Warsaw, and the year to come, and the paper in the safe in the study behind the photograph.*

*And my father is dead, and I have not seen him since June, and my life is a thing that a half-blind woman shouted at a German door.*

She went down the stairs. She walked past Anna Zaleska, who did not look at her; through the kitchen, where the fire was on and the copper for the laundry stood by the wall; out of the back door; and down the garden through the chestnuts and the wet walnut tree, in a dressing gown and shoes, in the cold, in November, to the side gate in the wall, and through the side gate into the yard of the block in the next street, which was the way out of that house that no German in the district had ever used and no Pole in the district had ever had to be told about; and she went out of the yard into the street, and stood at the corner of the Mokotowska in the grey morning of Warsaw in November 1943, in a dressing gown and shoes, in the city where she had been born, forty metres from the house of the German intelligence section of the district; and she burst into tears, in the street, in the daylight; and a woman with a basket stopped, and looked at her for four seconds, and then did the thing that would have been done in that city by any woman in the world, in any language, in any war: she put down her basket, and took off her own coat, and put it around the shoulders of a stranger in a dressing gown in the street; and said, in Polish:

"Come. Walk. Do not stand still in this cold; it is the standing still that kills people." And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna walked the length of the Mokotowska in the coat of a woman she had never met, in the grey morning, beside a woman with a basket, in silence, and cried the way, and at the corner of the Wilcza the woman took her coat back without one word, and went off towards the market.

The amber came two days later, by way of Zofia and the back gate of a building on the Dzielna, in a paper packet: one necklace of twenty-eight beads, of the Baltic amber that the women of the north of Poland had worn since the age of the Romans, in a grade of a colour like strong tea in the sun; her mother's; her grandmother's; and there was a piece of paper in the packet, folded twice, with six words on it in the handwriting of a woman of seventy-eight who had learned to write in a village school in the province of Płock in 1876.

*Idź i nie daj się zabić.* Go, and do not let them kill you.

She wore it once, that winter, on the night of the ninth of November, under the grey dress, at a dinner at the Blue Palace, with the district at table and the German administration around her, and a colonel of the intelligence section of Warsaw, at the head of the table, drunk on the month, reached over and touched the beads at her throat with two fingers in front of a General and said, "Amber. Poland. From the generation of the grandmothers."

"Yes," said Marion Herschlag. "It was my mother's. She is dead."

And he let the necklace fall back against her throat with two fingers, and smiled at the table, and the table laughed; and she sat through the rest of the evening with the amber of her mother against her whole skin, and the truth of the room in her mouth, and eleven metres away, across the table, a captain of twenty-eight sat with his whole face composed and did not once, in four hours, look at her throat.
